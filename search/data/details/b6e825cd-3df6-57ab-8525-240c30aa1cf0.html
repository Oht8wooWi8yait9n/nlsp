Documentation of the Action of Metoclopramide

Record ID: DSO 439
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Space motion sickness (SMS) differs in many of its signs and symptoms from 1g motion sickness, so much so that the traditional signs of SMS cannot be used to evaluate the presence, severity, or treatment of SMS. On STS-7 and 8 it was found that bowel sounds were strikingly absent during SMS and that GI symptoms were consistent with ileus (temporary paralysis of the bowel). A method of recording bowel sounds had been devised and demonstrated on STS-8. A ground-based study of subjects with acute MS was also done.

This study had three objectives:
1. To demonstrate that presence or absence of bowel sound was related to SMS and especially GI symptoms.
2. To document any changes in motility, as evidenced by bowel sounds, produced by a drug known to effect GI motility in many situations.
3. To document any effects of weightlessness on bowel sounds of individuals not affected by SMS.

APPROACH:
Crewmembers with SMS were requested to make a recording on the day of recovery from the symptoms with a non-affected crewmember making a control recording if possible. Anyone electing to use the motility stimulating medication metoclopramide was requested to make a 45 minute recording during the initial drug dosage. The hardware consisted of four miniature professional two channel tape recorders and two small microphones and miniature battery supply mounted on an elastic Velcro coupled belt. Operations consisted of turning on the recorder, checking preset contro1s and connecting the microphone belt and placing it around the waist. A recording time of 45 minutes per cassette side was available and requested. The crewmember was free to perform his usual tasks during the recording.

The recordings were analyzed by replaying and: 1) recording through a filter on a high frequency two channel graphics recorder; 2) having a skilled observer listen through stereo headphones and electrically mark the graphic record whenever a sound was heard. The marks were then counted, averaged over five minute epochs, and plotted or otherwise analyzed.

RESULTS:
During investigation of space motion sickness on STS-4 through STS-8, it was discovered that the upper gastrointestinal system's normal action is stopped or greatly reduced. This explains the vomiting without nausea and other stomach complaints. On these flights, the drug metaclopramide, which restores GI activity in certain conditions, was effective in restoring bowel sounds and reversing symptoms in two individuals. It was ineffective in two others who were taking scopolamine, a known antagonist of the drug.

Three subjects made a total of four in-flight and three postflight recordings during STS-41D. Two of these were during the periods of SMS and one subject without SMS took the metoclopramide drug in-flight. Since this was the first opportunity to use this methodology after STS-8, a number of points are of interest including: with a trained observer it is possible to reliably record and quantify bowel sounds in all but the most active or the noisiest conditions in-flight. Secondly, the scheme works with a minimum of training and crewmember interference. With SMS there is barely detectable bowel activity and after recovery (MD-7) bowel sounds were essentially normal. Bowel sounds were also normal during the period of SMS in a third subject who had been affected on a previous mission. The differences are orders of magnitude.

On STS-41G, four subjects made preflight records, and all except 1 recording (microphones were apparently not actuated) were nominal and reproduced without difficulty. Inflight, a total of 8 recordings were made. Only one channel was recorded on three of the recordings; three other recordings seem as if the microphones were partially elevated from the body. The recordings were reproduced and analyzed with the exception of the latter three. No crewmember made recordings during SMS or medication.

That any recordings were made is a success in view of the few that have been obtained. New data were added to help understand the problem. Unfortunately, there was not a recording of the drug's action nor a complete series from any subject with SMS (pre or post, during sickness, during recovery or late in the flight). These records add support to the concept of bowel sounds as an unequivocal marker of SMS, and further suggest a rebound in GI activity on recovery from SMS (and possibly hyperactivity in weightlessness in some subjects--a new finding).

Regarding hardware anomalies inflight, the loss of 1 channel on the three STS-41G records was due to the record gain knob being mis-set. On disassembly, the circuit board supporting the gain control was found to be cracked, thus interrupting the signal to the channel.

This experiment was later included as part of DSO 456.
