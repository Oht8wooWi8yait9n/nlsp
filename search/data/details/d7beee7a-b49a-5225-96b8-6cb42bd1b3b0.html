Metabolic Adaptation of Skeletal Muscle to Training/Detraining: A Systems Model

Record ID: NCC958NPFR00206
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Space travel (detraining) has detrimental effects on skeletal muscle structure, metabolism, and function, including reductions in muscle size, strength, and endurance. Exercise (training) in space can counteract some of these deleterious effects. Experimental studies are still being conducted to determine both the cause of muscle deterioration and the type and characteristics of exercise training programs needed to counteract muscle atrophy. Physiologically-based computational models of human function can integrate cellular to whole-body data, provide a framework for quantitative understanding of the skeletal muscle metabolic responses to exercise after loading or unloading, and help in the development of countermeasures.

The original specific aims of this project were: a) To identify the metabolic adaptations to training and detraining to develop databases containing information on the structural, metabolic, and functional adaptations of skeletal muscle to microgravity and exercise training and the underlying biochemical mechanisms mediating these adaptations, b) To develop mathematical models of intermediary metabolism in skeletal muscle that account for the effects of training and detraining, c) To investigate the relative significance of model parameters affected by training or detraining on work capacity and efficiency, and d) To simulate the effects on skeletal muscle intermediary metabolism and energetics of space flight and exercise in space, to quantitatively test the hypotheses that after a period of space travel or exercise training, the observed changes in the rates of carbohydrate and fatty acid oxidation in skeletal muscle are a result of a partial conversion of slow-twitch to fast-twitch fibers and alterations in glycolytic and oxidative enzymes.

APPROACH:
Flow was reduced over a wide range and for a sufficient duration in order to investigate the sequence of events that occur during the transition to a new metabolic steady state.

RESULTS:
From model simulations, the investigators were able to link changes in enzyme activities at the cellular level to patterns of fuel utilization at the whole tissue level. This finding has great potential for the optimazation of training regimes as countermeasures. Once the model of the whole body is completed the effects of dietary manipulations could be investigated. During the third year of the study, the investigators concentrated on the development and implementation of the computational model of whole-body metabolism. This model integrates cellular to whole-body data and incorporates parameters representing enzyme activities which have been identified to be altered with chronic loading or unloading.
