Sociokinetic Analysis as a Tool for Optimization of Environmental Design

Record ID: LMLSTP3.7
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

The Lunar-Mars Life Support Test Project (LMLSTP) Phase III testing experiment was designed to develop a practical application to the design of enclosed environments, such as those for long-duration space missions.

Social interaction in a built environment is a dynamic constant that expresses itself as a type of kinesis, or a choreographic pattern, by which the members of a group occupy a given place. The force and effectors to any given interaction lie partly in the realm of personal and group psychology. Behavioral psychology demonstrates many ways of viewing the inherent relationships in a group. Personal or subjective studies alone are insufficient to explain the behavior described as social interaction, because the environment in which these interactions occur contains elements that tend to stimulate or suppress specific behavior.

In general, these cues seem to complex to identify, isolate, or characterize in terms of their behavioral impact. The design of enclosed or hermetic habitats for long-duration human life support in extreme situations, such as Arctic or Antarctic research, space exploration, or lunar-Mars bases, is of extreme importance. Under such circumstances, the habitat itself takes on a uniquely influential role as the primary or sole environment and is thus critical in either supporting or undermining the mental health, productivity, and interactions of its inhabitants. Therefore, the conscious control of environmental cues such as programming, acoustics, and orientation, becomes fundamental to the facility's design and, by extension, to the success of the mission.

Sociokinetic analysis, or the study of the patterns in which a group of individuals within a given environment make use of that environment, was developed at NASAs Johnson Space Center (JSC) in 1997. This method involves: (1) capturing hard data regarding the use of volume within a hermetic habitat, and (2) applying statistical analyses from this data to the resident group.

Strict documentation of the habitat is then weighed against the results in order to force certain environmental design cues to the forefront.

The first run of this method involved an objective study using patterns of JSC's 20-foot chamber during Phase III of the Lunar Mars Life Support Test Project (LMLSTP) over the 91-day time span from September through December 1997.

APPROACH:

Cameras were placed throughout the three-level facility to monitor the environment; two were mounted on level one, one on level two, and one in the corridor area of level three. Level one was a multifunctional area consisting of a galley and wardroom, science workstations for advanced life support studies, principal personal hygiene and waste compartments (shower and toilet), and a dedicated exercise area in the airlock. The airlock was smaller than other rooms and was loud when in use. In addition, the airlock was the only location in the chamber from which the crew could be observed by anyone walking through the Building 7 high bay. The common room housed the entertainment center (TV and VCR), as well as the only table large enough for communal activities or large work tasks. Despite the room's cramped and intermingled location, it housed all of the equipment most desired for group functions.

Level two housed all principal equipment that supported the basic functions of the chamber, including bioreactors and gauges. The average noise intensity on level two was 70 decibels. Fluorescent fixtures arrayed vertically along the walls so that the occupants experienced a combination of glare and reflection at all locations on that level. A generous workstation table was provided on this floor, the same size as the wardroom table on level one but without the crowding and intermingled location.

Level three was the uppermost and most private level of the chamber, housing four identical crew quarters and a toilet. This level had one opening from a central landing at the head of the stairs.

To establish the desired habitable living quarters for the four-person crew, it was essential to find a quantitative, nonintrusive method of studying their use patterns in the 20-foot chamber facility over a three-month time span. The approach utilized the four cameras already present in the chamber, because the intrusiveness of any measurement system would have inherently affected the data collected. The cameras recorded the chamber 24 hours a day, seven days a week, over a three-week span. The question of adaptation or change was also a consideration. Therefore, data was taken during the early, middle, and late stages of the LMLSTP, specifically, weeks 3, 7, and 11.

Investigators were able to document when the crewmembers were enjoying the privacy of their personal quarters without invading that privacy, because the camera on level three recorded the public segment of that floor off of which all four crew quarters were located. Thus, the advantage gained was having complete and reasonable access to the crew's activities in a manner that was nonintrusive. For instance, the crew was alerted that taping would begin at a certain time on the evening before each week that was observed. By week three (the first week studied), this alert was of negligible impact to the data, because by this time the crew had become accustomed to the constant vigilance of the control room staff and had begun to ignore the presence of the cameras, or to accept them as a simple fact of daily life. An onscreen video time stamp was used, which permitted the researchers to verify the actual recording time against the time marked on the cassette.

At the completion of the test, a total of 512 hours of video was tracked using statistical analysis software, and the period of each crewmember's tenure on each floor was quantified in seconds. These units were then tracked against time, total duration, and the simultaneous activity of other crewmembers. The principal questions under consideration were:

 

 

  • Did the crew's preference for group versus private areas or other use patterns change over the duration of their confinement?
  • Did the crew prefer more private locations or more public/shared locations, with all things being equal (i.e., specific site-related activities aside)?
  • Were any marked social patterns or behaviors anomalous to non-confined groups?
  • Did any marked behaviors reflect in an unambiguous fashion to known conditions of the crew's environment?

    RESULTS:

    First, an analysis was made of the percentage of time the crew spent on each floor during each week of the test. Although there was less variation from week to week than had been anticipated, a slight but steady trend was seen toward less use of the airlock and level one (the group areas) in favor of level three (private zones).

    Although this difference is not considered statistically significant, a significant trend was detected in the comparison of individual room usage within each week. Furthermore, this trend held true across all three weeks of testing. This trend was identified after the project using Tukey's analysis.

    Of greatest interest for future refinements of this test was the analysis of use patterns against the time of day during the study period. While the rates of use for the airlock and level two appear to be similar, the incidence of that use is different. Because of the exercise function in the airlock, its use by only a single crewmember at a time was extended throughout the waking day with small peaks between 08:00 and 10:00 and between 18:00 and 20:00 as personnel exercised. Level two usage, however, was almost exclusively in 20-second increments steadily throughout the day - in other words, the amount of time it took for a person to traverse the level two landing on the stair while in transit between levels one and three. Occasionally, crewmembers would spend slightly larger blocks of time on level two to check or maintain equipment, but the greatest percentage of use stemmed from the transit function, which was more or less constant throughout the day.

    Another important (although less marked) data point was the set of locales for socialization. While level one-group interactions included any number of crewmembers up to four, the group interactions on Level Three were never more than three and predominantly of only two persons at one time. Moreover, crewmembers were never seen entering one another's private quarters. The group appeared from very early on to have established an unofficial protocol whereby people talking would stand just outside or in the doorway of another person's room, thus establishing a territorial boundary between the semiprivate realm of the corridor/landing and the private realm of the crew quarters. Mutual-boundary interactions also took place as crewmembers stood in their own doorways and conversed with one another across the landing.

    Use of level one was almost perfectly mirrored by the use of level three; in other words, when personnel were not on level one , the chances were very high that they were on level three and vice versa (rather than in the airlock or level two). In many instances this use at specific times of day was unsurprising. Level three, for example, was the area of choice between 24:00 and 08:00, while the crew was sleeping; and level one was most popular around 12:00 and 20:00, at lunch and dinnertime. However, this mirroring was also unaccompanied by anything but a static, constant baseline in the airlock and level two areas, suggesting that the use or disuse of levels one and three had bearing on one another but no bearing on the occupancy of level two and the airlock. Thus two of the four areas of the habitat (some 35-40% of its total available area for habitable use) went virtually unused except by necessity, while the other two areas became in essence the whole inhabited volume of the test chamber.

    By separate use of these terms a distinction has been established between "habitable" volume and "inhabited" volume. The difference between the former and the latter is that "habitable" volume can be occupied by humans, whereas "inhabited" volume will be occupied and used by humans.

    This is tremendously useful data because it identifies which environments the crew found acceptable, and which they did not. The evidence is nearly overwhelming that the crew preferred levels one and three of the 20-foot chamber over the airlock and level two. This is true to such a degree that these areas almost constitute wasted volume in that, despite the expressed needs of the crewmembers for greater privacy and flexibility within the habitat, they largely rejected the use of two semiprivate areas, which could have been, utilized as offline workstations and/or relaxation areas. In addition, the semiprivate landing area of level three could have become less of a public site for mutual-boundary interactions had it been possible for the crew to interact "offline" in some non-private room other than the level one common area.

    Thanks to this pattern of nonuse, environmental factors were identified that people clearly found unacceptable to the point of rejecting their use. The airlock was a small, cylindrical area that was not comfortably outfitted but rather housed only the exercise and other mechanical equipment. Furthermore, it was the only part of the habitat exposed to the exterior, resembled a"goldfish bowl" and sensibility may have swayed inhabitants. Other than exercise, there was no other activity associated with the room, and it was not very welcoming. Level two, on the other hand, had a pleasant level of illumination, carpeting, and a cozy corner or two to offer. Only two factors were less than optimal in it's outfitting, yet these appear to have had a decisive effect on the usability of the room: the very loud acoustic environment and the direct-glare lighting. While the room appeared calm, it was extremely difficult to make oneself heard for the noise generated by the equipment. Also, although the lighting levels were acceptable for tasks, the angle of lighting was extremely unpleasant.

    Thus a few important rules for design of inhabitable built environments have been derived from this experiment:
     

    1. Finishing's, dimensions, and privacy affect the usability of an area
    2. High-glare illumination can render an area unusable to the resident population
    3. An unacceptably loud acoustical environment can render an area unusable to the resident population. The private/semiprivate/public boundary issues raised by interpersonal communications on level three also suggest some useful rules of programming (i.e., functional allocation of volume) for future hermetic habitats
    4. In a restricted habitat, private rooms are considered inviolable territory and will not be invaded unless by explicit invitation
    5. The territory immediately adjacent to private rooms may/will be annexed as a semiprivate social center (unless other areas specifically intended for offline socializing are provided).

    In conclusion, environmental conditions have been found to affect the efficiency and usability of a closed environment facility.
