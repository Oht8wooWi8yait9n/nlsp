Solar Particle Event Observations with MARIE and Correlations with Earth and Near-Earth Instruments

Record ID: NAG91427
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Large solar particle events (SPEs) pose a significant radiation risk to astronauts’ health during long-duration missions, and they will be of critical concern during an interplanetary voyage. SPEs are created by fast coronal mass ejections (CMEs) from the sun accelerating out into space in a type of shock wave. The movement of these radioactive particles ejected from the sun during one of these events is difficult to observe due to its three-dimensional nature, lack of recorded observations, and the complex nature of other processes occurring near the sun during a CME.

The main objective of this experiment was to characterize the Martian radiation environment to better manage the radiation risk to crewmembers during future human missions to Mars. The Martian Radiation Environment Experiment (MARIE) instrument, designed to characterize the Martian radiation environment, was used to provide observations of SPEs in a critical region of the Mars heliosphere.

APPROACH:
Data collected by MARIE during SPEs was examined to extract critical information about acceleration of energetic radioactive particles, including timing and magnitude of event onset, peak, possible shock-enhanced peaks and subsequent delay.

RESULTS:
Initial work examined MARIE data collected during SPEs, extracted critical information including timing and magnitude of event onset, peak, possible shock-enhanced peaks, and subsequent decay. The data can be correlated with Earth and Near-Earth particle flux data (from GOES, ACE or other spacecraft) on the same event. The data can also be correlated with images from SOHO and other solar observatories of the solar source event. Because the separation of Earth and Mars varies over the nearly two-year orbital period of Mars, Odyssey observations help establish the heliospheric extent of SPEs (when do both Earth and Mars experience the same SPE, and when are SPEs noted at one, but not the other). These data can be used to impose constraints on SPE models, even when the observational information is limited to event onset, peak, and decay times.

Having a sensitive radiation sensor at Mars provides a unique opportunity to further our understanding of SPEs through the collection of distributed, multipoint observations. From March 2002 through October 2003 (the period during which MARIE was active) the Earth/Mars heliolongitude separation varies from zero to a full +/- 180 degrees. Heliolatitude separation varies from –46 to over 38 degrees. During the extended mission period, mid 2005 to mid 2007, the angle again varies from zero to a full +/- 180 degrees, while the Heliolatitude variation varies between +/- 45 degrees. This variation provides a natural laboratory to probe various geometries of interest. CMEs expand outward radially from the sun. They vary in extent from 10s of degrees to well over 180 degrees. The faster CMEs responsible for SPEs are generally the larger ones in extent. When Earth and Mars are within 90 degrees of one another, they are likely to both be affected by any shock wave associated with these larger CMEs. The extent, strength, and evolution of this shock enhanced peak, at the nose of the shock and along the leading and trailing edges of the shock, provides insight into the physics of the acceleration process and is of vital interest to astronaut safety.

The character of the SPE relative to the location of the center of the CME is one of the most important parameters for forecasting the evolution of the first few hours of the SPE (away from the shock enhanced peak). The accelerated particles that have escaped from the shock move out through the heliosphere “clockwise” along the “Archimedean spiral” of the interplanetary magnetic field line. The curvature of the spiral is a result of the Sun’s rotation and it depends on the solar wind speed. The solar wind typically varies from 300 to 500 km/sec. The ambient solar wind speed can be measured near Earth and estimated for Mars. The faster the wind, the lower the curvature.

Solar Particle Events are roughly correlated with the solar cycle, occurring more frequently during seven years of solar active years than the three or so years near solar minimum. The current solar cycle (Cycle 23) has produced several large solar particle events, many during the period MARIE was collecting data. Solar activity and SPEs decrease during Solar Minimum, and the period of the PS will overlap Solar Minimum. However, during the approach to Solar Minimum, there have been several significant SPEs and a few periods of minor events. Events during Solar Minimum are generally isolated events occurring in a simple solar wind background. These events are particularly good for testing models of event propagation. However, this requires multipoint observations. Continuing observations at Mars with the same data source used since 2002 (the GRS data) would continue to complement data collected at Earth by GOES and other spacecraft.

The MARIE instrument was optimized for high energy, low count rate GCR and secondary particles (including neutrons scattered up from the planetary atmosphere). The very high count rate experienced during large SPEs saturated the instrument. The PI prepared techniques to collect critical data as much as possible during SPEs as part of the core MARIE mission.
