Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to microgravity is known to have profound effects on fluid homeostasis within the human body. The headward shift of fluids which occurs in microgravity results in an increased excretion of fluids and electrolytes. Fluid and electrolyte balance in the body is regulated by several systems, any or all of which are potentially responsible for the microgravity-induced changes in fluid balance. The kidneys play an important role in regulating fluid and excretion/retention, and changes in renal blood flow may prove to be an important factor in adapting to space flight. Many endocrine and circulatory factors contribute to fluid homeostasis, both in conjunction with and independently from the renal system. In addition, energy utilization and dietary intake of fluid and electrolytes may have profound effects on the body's ability to adapt to microgravity.

The hypotheses of this study were:

1. Loss of body water is complete by the third day of inflight and is maintained at a reduced level. This will be reflected by decreases in total body water (TBW), extracellular fluid volume (ECF), urine, and losses of electrolytes; as well as changes in peripheral venous pressure (PVP).

2. Plasma volume (PV) will decrease early in flight and is sustained at a lower level than preflight. This decrease in PV will affect neuroendocrine controls (e.g., plasma renin activity, epinephrine, and norepinephrine).

3. Enhanced excretion of sodium may be influenced by altered secretion of hormonal endocrine factors, e.g., atrial natriuretic factor.

4. The primary controlling factor for antidiurectic hormone (ADH) release is plasma osmolarity. As a result of exposure to microgravity, the effects of ADH on urine output may be counteracted by altered renal function.

5. Energy utilization during space flight remains similar to preflight levels even in the presence of reduced exercise.

The purpose of this study was to determine the effect of microgravity on:
1. TBW, ECF, PV
2. Body fluid distribution
3. Sodium/potassium balance (i.e., intake, serum concentrations, excretion)
4. Fluid/electrolyte regulatory hormones
5. Energy expenditure (EE)

APPROACH:
Flight Requirements and Procedures

Preflight, In Flight, and Postflight:

1. Participants drank enriched water, which contained naturally-occurring stable and nonradioactive isotopes after taking baseline urine and saliva samples.

Void-by-void urine samples and two saliva samples (at 3 hours and at 5-7 hours) were collected post isotope ingestion according to the timeline in the activity schedule. Samples of galley water were collected once every 24 hours in flight to monitor the isotope enrichment that occurs onboard. At L-3 days a dose of enriched water was ingested to increase the background of stable isotope concentrations. No samples were required on that day. On L-15 days through launch and at landing through R+6 days, participants (not the control subject) ate prepared meals which they had chosen from a menu.

A control subject was required for EE. Preflight activity included one training session at L-4 months, and saliva and urine collections according to the timeline in the activity schedule. There were no postflight requirements, and the only ingestion of enriched water was at L-3 days. In-flight, the control subject collected one saliva and one urine sample each flight day (FD) plus follow-up samples on test days.

2. Extracellular Fluid Volume (ECF):
ECF was determined through measurements of bromide dilution. Bromide is a stable compound that distributes throughout the extracellular space. The technique involved the collection of a 2 milliliter (ml) baseline blood sample, followed by ingestion of a specific amount of bromide. After three hours, a 2 ml blood sample was taken. Subjects were fasting and did not exercise for at least 12 hours prior to ingesting the bromide.

3. Blood/Serum Analyses:
Blood/serum was obtained via an indwelling catheter preflight, in-flight, and postflight for biochemical and hormonal analyses. Preflight and postflight, a 20-minute rest period was required after catheter insertion to restore resting catecholamine levels. Whenever possible, testing was coordinated with Medical Operations' activities to minimize crew time. In-flight catheter insertion, blood draws, analyses, and preparation of blood samples for storage was required.

4. Urine Collection:
All preflight and postflight urine samples were collected using void-by-void techniques. In-flight urine collection involved drawing syringe samples from a urine collection device (UCD), which contained lithium chloride inside as a marker. Specified syringes were frozen, and others were stowed along with UCD's in the urine sample containment bag.

5. Intake/Exercise Log:
Food/fluid/medications/exercise was recorded preflight and postflight according to the activity schedule. In-flight, the participants used bar code readers to record this information. Weighed (pre-proportioned) meals were provided from L-15 days through R+6 days.

6. Peripheral Venous Pressure (PVP):
A peripheral indication of central venous pressure was measured preflight and postflight using a strain gauge manometer. Inflight, the PVP measurements were taken using equipment in the Life Science Laboratory Equipment (LSLE) In-Flight Blood Collection System.

Preflight and Postflight Only:

1. Plasma Volume (PV):
PV was determined using a nonradioactive indicator dye (indocyanine green) injected through a catheter after the 2 ml baseline blood draw. One ml blood samples was drawn each minute for 10 minutes, then at 15 and 20 minutes. PV was performed simultaneously with ECF testing preflight and postflight.

2. Renal Clearance (RC) Tests:
Two indices of renal function, glomerular filtration rate (using inulin) and effective renal plasma flow (using para-aminohippuric acid [PAH]), were measured preflight and postflight. After a 2.5 ml baseline blood draw was taken via catheter, the insulin was infused at 0 minutes, and the PAH at 40 minutes. At minutes 90, 108, 126, 144, and 180, 1 ml blood samples were obtained.

3. Measurement of Body Weight (in conjunction with dietary intake monitoring):
Participants were weighed daily on calibrated scales preflight and postflight during L-15 days through L-0 and R+0 through R+6 days, in conjunction with the pre-proportioned diet.

Crew Training:
Training sessions were required for achieving proficiency in phlebotomy (i.e., venipuncture techniques), PVP, tracer handling, sample processing, and urine and saliva collection techniques, as well as blood sample analysis for specific in-flight portions of the investigation. Hardware training sessions covered the bar code reader and LSLE equipment.

RESULTS:
During flight, TBW decreased slightly and ECF did not appear to change compared to preflight. ECF and PV were slightly elevated after flight, most likely resulting from the pre-landing saline load. GFR was slightly elevated after flight, whereas the ERPF was lower. Body weight tended to be lower after flight. Hematocrit values during the flight were unchanged from preflight values. During flight, plasma concentrations of ANP and aldosterone were lower than preflight values. The plasma concentration of ADH didn’t change during flight. Plasma renin activity was elevated during flight and upon landing, and returned to normal on R+6.

The plasma concentration of ACTH didn’t change during the flight; however, there was a substantial increase in plasma ACTH at landing (R+0). Serum sodium concentration varied during flight, with a small elevation above preflight values on FD2 and a decline below those values on FD7. Serum potassium concentration increased slightly during flight, as a function of the flight duration. Serum chloride was unchanged on FD1, but increased on FD2 and FD7 and postflight. Urine volume during flight was below preflight values, and a return to preflight levels did not occur until several days after landing. Urine potassium excretion tended to be lower during flight and after landing. Urinary excretion of creatinine tended to decrease during flight. Urinary cortisol excretion was increased during flight, but quickly returned to normal after landing.
