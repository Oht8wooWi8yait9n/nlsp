3D Tissue Analogues For The Study Of Varicella-Zoster Virulence And Infectivity

Record ID: ROI_VZV
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Varicella-Zoster virus (VZV), present in 93% of the population here on Earth, remains largely inactive the human body after the initial immune response at normal gravity (1G). When a VZV infected human leaves 1G for space flight, the normally inactive virus begins to re-express, presenting a potential health and safety problem. VZV may assume increased virulence and/or live virus numbers in microgravity. This could, in combination with a general diminished immune competency, lead to heighten states of infection for in flight astronaut populations and present a risk for associates of returning crews. This study will demonstrate the sensitivity of the model and provide an operational deliverable in the form of a reliable test for live quantifiable virus. Specific goals of this study were to establish and validate Normal Human Neural Progenitor (NHNP) as viable target for VZV infection, establish 3-D NHNP as a ground-based analog for study of VZV in modeled microgravity, and use 3-D NHNP as an operational analog for VZV in microgravity.

APPROACH:
As samples of activated VZV from astronauts are difficult to obtain and work with, this study utilized a laboratory strain of VZV, appropriate ground patient samples, 3-D recapitulated lung (MeWo) and neural (NHNP) tissue analogs that mimic aspects of the human physiology. This allowed a further exploration of the native and in-flight virus replication and virulence, while also validating the 3-D Neural Model. Currently laboratory work with VZV is done with 2-D malignant melanoma lung fibroblast cells (MeWo). This experiment developed 3-D Normal Human Neural Progenitor (NHNP) model with VZV and compare the results to the 2-D MeWo model. Uninfected and infected 2-D MeWo cells were expanded and conditioned in a media designed to support the rigorous nutritional needs of 3-D cultures. This media is called GTSF2. Thereafter all MeWo and NHNP cells, (2-D and 3-D) were maintained in GTSF2 in order to adequately support cells in the bioreactor and to eliminate the variable of having multiple media used in a single experiment. Three dimensional models are proving to be excellent predictive models of physiologic response to disease and infection. It is anticipated that VZV will infect this three dimensional model. If it does so, this in itself will be an accomplishment as VZV is not regularly cultured in neural cells but rather lung tumor cells, the wrong anatomical target. Furthermore, having a nuerotrope like VZV infect the correct target tissue rather than lung will undoubtedly allow for study of the virus in a more in vivo like environment and will likely yield new, more accurate information as to the growth and replication of the virus. This approach planned to establish and validate 3-D NHNP as a viable target for VZV infection. Comparing the results of VZV grown in modeled microgravity to VZV collected from the saliva of ground based patients or returning astronauts can determine similarities and differences in DNA sequenced between the actual and analog virus samples.

RESULTS:
NHNP cells were cultured in 3D on a scaffold of Cultispher beads in a rotating wall vessel (RWV) bioreactor and analyzed by multicolor fluorescence in situ hybridization (mFISH), flow cytometry, confocal microscopy and RT-PCR to assess their genetic stability as compared with 2D NHNP cell cultures. Comparison of 2D and 3D cultured NHNP cells by mFISH respectively showed no chromosomal rearrangements or breaks, indicating genetic stability of NHNP TLAs for at least a six month period in culture. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that after 180 days in culture, NHNP TLAs expressed neuronal progenitor markers CXCR4, CD133, CD105-Endoglin, CD 90-Thy-1 and CD49f-a6 Integrin at levels comparable to the parental NHNP (2D) cell population. Markers of hematopoietic differentiation, CD38+ and CD45+ were not detected in either culture. An example of the tissue-like complexity of the NHNP TLA constructs is shown by environmental scanning electron micrographs (ESEM), which illustrate the relative size, density, and indistinguishable nature of the individual neural cells in the tissue assembly. In addition, confocal microscopy confirmed that NHNP TLAs expressed mature neuronal markers: neuron-specific nuclear protein (NeuN), ß-Tubulin-III, microtubule associated protein A&B (MAP 2 A&B), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). TLA microcarrier substrates alone did not show autofluorescence. Similarly, progenitor (Nestin) and mature (ß-Tubulin-III) neuronal markers were detected by immunohistochemistry in human TG removed at autopsy. Quantitative analysis of mRNA expression revealed higher levels of the early progenitor markers CXCR4 and CD133 in NHNP TLAs than in human TG even though only few NHNP cells expressed the two proteins. Later stage developmental markers, CD105, CD90 and CD49f, were expressed to a lesser extent in NHNP TLAs than in human TG, suggesting that NHNP TLAs are less differentiated than primary TGs. Neuronal markers Nestin and ß-Tubulin-III were expressed at comparable levels in TG and NHNP TLAs. Lower expression levels in the NHNP TLAs than in human TG were observed for the neuronal marker neurofilament 200 (NF-200), again indicating NHNP TLAs are generally less differentiated than human TG. Taken together, the data indicated that NHNP TLAs, which had undergone 18–20 fold expansion over the three-month time period, share some similarities but are less differentiated when compared to mature human TGs removed at autopsy.
