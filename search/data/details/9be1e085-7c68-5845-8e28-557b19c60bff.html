Dietary Intake Can Predict and Protect Against Changes in Bone Metabolism during Space Flight and Recovery

Record ID: ProK
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This study proposed that a diet with a decreased ratio of animal protein to potassium will lead to decreased bone resorption and bone mineral loss during space flight. The study goals were to validate the hypothesis that modification of dietary intake patterns can minimize bone loss and postflight recovery time by determining if the ratio of acid (animal protein) to base (potassium) precursors in the diet is correlated with markers of bone formation and bone resorption during space flight. Specifically, the researchers wanted to determine the extent to which urinary calcium excretion predicts changes in bone metabolism from acid and base precursors in the diet. They also wanted to determine if the ratio of acid to base precursors in the diet is related to the length of time required for recovery from bone mineral loss after space flight.

APPROACH:
Seventeen astronauts completed the inflight and postflight protocols. Samples were returned from the International Space Station (ISS) on Shuttle flights in 2011 on STS-133, 134, and 135, and on SpaceX missions 1-5. This study consisted of 4-day controlled diets with a high or low animal protein–to-potassium (APro:K) ratio. High and low diets were given to crewmembers before and during space flight. Each crewmember had one high and one low diet session before flight and two high and two low sessions during flight, in addition to a 4-day session around flight day 30, when crewmembers consumed their typical in-flight intake. At the end of each session, blood and urine samples were collected.

RESULTS:
Calcium, total protein, energy, and sodium were maintained in each crewmember's preflight and in-flight controlled diets. Relative to preflight values, N-telopeptide (NTX) and urinary calcium were higher during space flight, and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP) was higher toward the end of flight. Both diets did not affect NTX, BSAP, or urinary calcium. Dietary sulfur and age were significantly associated with changes in NTX. Dietary sodium and flight day were significantly associated with urinary calcium during flight.

Please refer to the publication below titled, Dietary Acid Load and Bone Turnover during Long-duration Spaceflight and Bed Rest for more detailed results.
