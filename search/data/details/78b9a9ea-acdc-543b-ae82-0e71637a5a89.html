Evaluation of Thermoregulation During Space Flight

Record ID: 3.2.2
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Impaired thermoregulation may have significant impact during space flight operations such as extravehicular activities (EVA), entry, landing and egress. Impaired thermoregulation, as represented by an increase in core (intestinal) temperature in response to a given thermal load, has been consistently observed in subjects confined to bed rest or immersed in water (Greenleaf, 1980; Greenleaf, 1985; Fortney, 1987). Further, these changes were observed during exercise following these simulated microgravity exposures (Greenleaf, 1980; Greenleaf, 1985; Fortney, 1987). Similar changes were expected to occur during space flight.

This impairment of temperature regulation may be manifested as decreased exercise capacity and increased orthostatic intolerance; it may also be manifested as higher levels of core body temperature for a given level of work (oxygen consumption, Vo2) both during and after space flight. The higher core temperatures may be due to altered cutaneous vasodilatory reflex (delayed onset of vasodilatation and decreased skin perfusion for a given level of core temperature), increased sweating threshold, and decreased sweating sensitivity, while heat production might not be altered. The purpose of this preliminary experiment was to assess human core (intestinal) temperature and thermoregulatory responses to exercise after adaptation to long-duration spaceflight. Specific objectives of this experiment were to:

1) Determine whether overall thermoregulation is impaired during space flight by comparing core temperature (Tin) during two submaximal levels of aerobic exercise preflight, inflight, and postflight;

2) Determine whether the elevated core temperature is caused by altered heat production, as calculated by indirect calorimetry;

3) Determine whether the elevated core temperature is caused by impaired heat loss, as assessed by the skin blood flow/core temperature relationship (where changes in skin blood flow are measured pre- and postflight by laser Doppler flowmetry, and estimated during all tests by calculations of tissue conductance) and by the sweating response. Local sweat response/core temperature relationship was measured pre- and postflight using a dew point hygrometer, and total body sweat loss is estimated during all tests by measuring body weight loss.

It was anticipated that, for a given absolute level of work, body temperatures would increase to a greater degree due to (1) a smaller production and/or decreased efficiency of evaporation of sweat, and (2) decreased convective heat loss from the surface of the skin.

APPROACH:
This investigation examined body temperatures (skin and core), heat production (indirect calorimetry), and evaporative heat loss during 2 standard levels of exercise (40% and 65% of preflight VO2peak).

Submaximal Cycle Exercise Test (before and after flight) - Graded maximal cycle exercise testing was first conducted to volitional fatigue on 2 crewmembers in the supine position. These sessions occurred 2-3 days before the preflight submaximal exercise tests, repeated during the last 5 days of the mission, and 6 days after landing (R+6). The graded maximal cycle exercise test protocol consisted of:

  • 5 minutes of supine rest on the ergometer
  • 2 minutes of warm-up at 50 Watts (W)
  • 5 minutes of pedaling at 100 W
  • 5 minutes of pedaling at 125 W
  • 5 minutes of pedaling at 150 W

    After the 150-W segment of the protocol, exercise intensity was increased by 25 W-per-minute until the subject could no longer maintain the 75-rpm pedaling frequency. Cool down was conducted at 50 W for 3-5 minutes after maximal exercise. During each maximal test, oxygen (O2) uptake and carbon dioxide (CO2) production were measured and averaged over 30-s intervals using a gas analysis system. Heart rate was continuously monitored with a standard electrocardiogram (ECG). Blood pressure was measured during the last 30 seconds of each exercise level using manual sphygmomanometry. The highest level of O2 uptake, averaged over a 60-s period, was designated peak oxygen consumption (VO2peak). Exercise intensities requiring 40% and 65% of the preflight peak value were used during the submaximal tests.

    The supine submaximal cycle tests were conducted 145-146 days before flight (L-145) and on R+5. All testing was performed at room temperature (22.6-23.3°C; 56-64% humidity) and at the same time of day to mitigate circadian effects. Postflight sessions occurred during the summer (July, 1995), but the crewmembers were almost exclusively confined to air-conditioned buildings. The submaximal exercise tests consisted of a continuous protocol of the following:

  • 20 minutes of quiet rest
  • 20 minutes of exercise at 40% preflight VO2peak (50 or 60 W)
  • 20 minutes of exercise at 65% preflight VO2peak (100 or 110 W)
  • 10 minutes of quiet resting recovery

    Heart rates during submaximal exercise were monitored with a heart watch. Blood pressure was measured twice before exercise and every 10 minutes during exercise with a manual sphygmomanometer. Skin temperatures were measured continuously with four external skin thermistors (forearm, calf, thigh, chest) and recorded with a data logger. Core temperature was measured using a small ingestible telemetry thermal sensor pill that was ingested 6 hours before exercise. Temperature data was sampled every 30 seconds and stored in a receiver.

    Skin blood flow velocity was measured continuously with a laser Doppler skin blood flow monitor using an integrated probe attached to the forearm with a local skin-heating (38°C) collar. Local sweating rate at the upper quadrant of the chest was measured continuously with a dew point hygrometer sweat system. The dew point sensor was ventilated (500-800 ml· min-1) with ambient air from the room. Subjects towel dried after each exercise session and were weighed in the same dry gym shorts before and after exercise. All measurement devices were calibrated before each exercise session.

    Submaximal Exercise Test (during flight): No submaximal data was collected during flight due to the late arrival of the Spektr module and scheduling problems after the module arrived.

    RESULTS:
    These results should be viewed as preliminary results only, since the low number of subjects did not allow for statistical analysis of the data. However, the results do suggest that thermoregulation was impaired following long-duration space flight.

    Maximal Exercise: The preflight maximal exercise supine cycle VO2peak values for the subjects were 32.6 and 29.5 ml• kg-1• min-1 for Subjects 1 and 2, respectively. Due to the limited active muscle mass with this form of exercise, these supine cycle values were each approximately 20% lower than the measured upright treadmill VO2peak values. During flight, Subject 1 was reported to have performed approximately 70% of the standard prescribed Russian countermeasure program (which consists of approximately 2 hours each day of aerobic and resistive exercises), while Subject 2 was reported to have performed less than 70% of the prescribed countermeasures. Near the end of the mission, on flight day 112, Subject 1 had a cycle VO2peak of 27.2 ml• kg-1• min-1(reduced 17% from preflight); for Subject 2, the value was 20.8 ml• kg-1• min-1(reduced 29%). After flight, neither crewmember could complete the exercise protocol, stopping after 28-29 minutes. Postflight, cycle VO2peak on R+6 was further reduced to 23.5 ml• kg-1• min-1 for Subject 1 (reduced 28% from preflight). For Subject 2, no value was obtained because the medical monitor would not allow the postflight maximal exercise test to be performed because of the subject's delayed recovery from the mission.

    Submaximal Exercise: Both crewmembers were able to complete the preflight submaximal exercise protocol; final exercise heart rates were only 137 and 123 bpm for Subjects 1 and 2, respectively. On R+5, Subjects 1 and 2 could not complete 11 and 12 minutes of the 65% VO2peak level, respectively. In both cases, the medical monitor terminated the test shortly after the exercise heart rate exceeded the preflight response. In each case, the subject appeared fatigued and was having difficulty maintaining the pedaling frequency.

    Blood pressure responses to exercise were similar before and after flight. Oxygen uptake on R+5 was similar to preflight values, except at the last sampling time for Subject 1, where the lower value was most likely due to his difficulty maintaining the pedaling frequency. The Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER) value was higher at most sampling times on R+5.

    A consistent difference was not seen between pre- and postflight supine resting skin temperatures at any measurement site. Resting intestinal temperature was similar preflight and at R+5. During postflight exercise, the core temperature, (Tin, ingestible telemetry pill) at test termination was similar (37.8 degrees C for both subjects despite a 29% shorter exercise time and a 40% reduction in the total cumulative work (W• min-1).

    The sweating and skin blood flow responses for the two subjects were altered postflight. The slope of the sweating/Tin relationship (mg• min-1• cm-2• °C-1) was reduced from 4.5 preflight to 2.1 postflight for Subject 1 and from 11 to 3.6 for Subject 2. The slope of the skin blood flow/Tin relationship (percent change Perfusion Unit• °C-1) was also reduced from 346 preflight to 214 postflight for Subject 1 and from 214 to 143 for Subject 2. Tin thresholds for the onset of sweating and vasodilation were not altered in a consistent manner after flight. For sweating, Tin thresholds (°C) were 37.4 preflight and 37.6 postflight for Subject 1 and 36.6 and 36.6 for Subject 2. For skin blood flow, Tin thresholds were 37.3 preflight and 37.5 postflight for Subject 1 and 37.6 preflight and 37.7 postflight for Subject 2. Total body sweat (weight) loss during preflight exercise was 636 g vs. 272 g postflight for Subject 1 and 455 g vs. 45 g postflight for Subject 2. Subject 2 had just reached the sweating threshold postflight when the session was terminated. Body weight on R+5 was reduced in both crewmembers from the preflight value--for Subject 1 by 0.8 kg and for Subject 2 by 2.3 kg.

    In conclusion, these preliminary findings suggest alteration of heat loss responses in two crewmembers following a 115-day space flight. The sensitivity of the heat loss responses was reduced after long-duration space flight, resulting in a faster rate of increase in core temperature.
