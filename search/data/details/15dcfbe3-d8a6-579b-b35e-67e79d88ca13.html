Eye-Hand Coordination

Record ID: DSO 418
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objective of this experiment was to evaluate changes in the perception of motion levels in crewmembers during space flight and immediately postflight. APPROACH:
A hand tracking task of a moving point on recording papers was made preflight, in flight, and postflight to note any changes in sensitivity. A modified servo recorder, self-contained, weighing approximately five pounds was used by a mission specialist during approximately 3 - 15 minute sessions in flight and 2 - 15 minute sessions pre- and postflight. Data was recorded on board and returned for analysis.

The tracings were scored and consisted of sweep, peak, and reaction times (before and after the trial) for the sine and ramp data. The vertical distance between the signal and the response at the peak was measured in decimeters. Peak differences were converted to centimeters and are referred to as "peak". Overshoots were recorded as negative values while undershoots were positive for the peak measurements. The horizontal distance between the signal and the response, which is referred to as "sweep" was measured on the axis and at half the amplitude of the signal. In the sweep measurements, leads were recorded as negative values and lags were positive. The sweep measurements on the axis were selected and defined as the lag response. These lag responses were converted to time measured in milliseconds and are referred to as "time-lag."

The horizontal distance between the signal and the response was measured at the beginning of the wave pattern and at the end of the wave. These lag responses were converted to reaction times in milliseconds and are referred to as "RT-Before and RT-After.”

Unequal sub-samples among the frequencies within the replications exist for the peak and time-lag measurements. Sub-samples for frequencies 2, 5, and 10 were approximately 4, 10, and 20 respectively. Only one sample for each frequency within the replications is present for the RT-Before and RT-After measurements for the sine and ramp waves. There were approximately 18 to 20 measurements of reaction time in the square wave pattern.

Several problems were encountered while recording the data. Sometimes the pen used to record the subject's tracing stopped writing and at other times the pen tore the paper. These individual points were recorded as missing. For some measurements, whole sessions were questionable and these data were not used in these analyses.

RESULTS:
The peak differences generally deceased over flights (time) for both sine and ramp waves, while the same pattern was seen only for the half amplitude factor on the time-lag data. The full amplitude factor showed very mixed results for the three flights. Generally, the performance was better (smaller peak difference and less lag) for the half amplitude than for the full amplitude and generally the performance improved as the frequency decreased.

No significant flight or replication effects were detected from the before and after reaction time data of the sine and ramp waves as well as the reaction time from the square signal. The data suggest that the subjects improved in performance over time, although the limited replication data did not necessarily support this, and that the tracking in space (in flight) essentially did not alter the crewmembers' performance.
