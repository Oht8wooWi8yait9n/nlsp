Chromosome Analyses of Gemini Astronauts

Record ID: GEMCYTO
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to ionizing radiation was a known hazard during the Gemini program. Although the U.S. Mercury and Gemini crews, as well as Soviet crews, received only small doses of radiation during their missions, scientists were certain that future high altitude orbital flights and ultimately the lunar missions would subject crews to appreciable exposures. In the 1960s, relatively little was known about the radiation response of man, and therefore much less was known about flight crews and what their responses would be if exposed to various doses under various circumstances. It was assumed that radiation responses on the ground would be identical to radiation exposure in spaceflight. In earlier suborbital and orbital flights, biological materials were flown, and had results of radiation exposure several magnitudes higher than expected, given the relatively low doses they were exposed to during flight. This led to the hypotheses that (1) a synergism might exist between radiation and some other parameter of spaceflight, such as weightlessness or vibration, or (2) that weightlessness itself might induce effects more commonly associated with radiation exposure.

In an attempt to answer these hypotheses, several inflight experiments were carried out during the Gemini program. Chromosomal aberration production in human peripheral leukocytes irradiated in vitro during the flights was studied. This measurement had been demonstrated to be a sensitive method of biological radiation dose estimation, and had been successfully used in a number of cases of accidental whold body irradiation.

APPROACH:
This study was first conducted on Gemini 3. Preflight and postflight peripheral leukocyte chromosome analyses were made of blood samples obtained from the Gemini 3 crew. These measurements had two purposes: first, to serve as a control of the experiment entitled "Synergistic Effect of Zero-g and Radiation on White Blood Cells" (experiment ID S004), and secondly, to examine the effect of spaceflight on human chromosomes. Subsequently, preflight and postflight chromosome analyses were made on all other Gemini flight crews (except Gemini 8, where postflight samples could not be taken because of the mission''s early termination).

Leukocyte samples were obtained from blood drawn during the last preflight medical exam and the first postflight medical exam. For most flights, postflight samples were flown to and processed at Cape Kennedy; the samples for Gemini 3 and 11 were processed aboard the recovery vessel along with the samples for the S004 experiment. Short term leukocyte cultures were prepared, and the leukocytes were then separated from the heparinized blood by centrifugation. The cells were then placed in culture medium: 10 ml of Medium 199 with 30% AB+ inactivated human serum, 5000 units penicillin, 5mg streptomycin, and 0.5 ml phytohemagglutinin M (Difco) per culture. The cultures were then incubated at approximately 36 degrees Celsius in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air. Colchicine was added after 60 hours of incubation to a final concentration of 10 to the -4 M, and cells were fixed after 66 hours. Cells then suitable for chromosome analysis were scored for chromatid- and chromosome-type aberrations.

It should be mentioned that on the Gemini 4, 5 and 7 flights, small quantities of various radioisotopes were injected into the crewmen preflight and postflight for other studies of plasma volume and red cell mass:

  • RISA (125I-iodinated human serum albumin) was used on Gemini 4 and 5

  • 51Cr-labeled red cells were used on Gemini 5 and 7

  • DFP (tritiated di-isopropyl fluorophosphonate) was used on Gemini 7.

    Preflight and postflight blood samples for this chromosome study were obtained before any of these isotopes were injected; on Gemini 4, however, one postflight sample from one crewman did not produce satisfactory cultures, so a second sample had to be obtained 1 week after recovery (after postflight isotopes had been injected.) While the estimated total-body radiation doses from the decay of these isotopes was very small (less than 88 mrem per man for the Gemini 5 flight, for instance), even minute radiation doses like these could cause some chromosome breakage. More complete details can be found in the experiment "Bioassay of Body Fluid" (experiment ID M005). Furthermore, for the missions where RISA was used, a control subject was injected with 2.5 micro-c of RISA and blood samples were taken for chromosomal analysis at times corresponding to the flight crew sampling times. No controls were done for the 51 chromium or DFP procedures.

    RESULTS:
    No large chromosomal effects of spaceflight were demonstrated. Postflight effects of the magnitude seen in biological materials flown on pre-Gemini flights were not seen on any of the Gemini flights. Some classes of aberrations did show a slight increase after some of the flights, but could not be correlated with flight duration, isotope injection or extravehicular activity (EVA). In particular, postflight increases of chromatid- and chromosome-type deletions are significant by a simple Chi-square test. The results were quite puzzling, in that chromatid-type aberrations occurred; these aberrations are only induced in cells that have already started DNA synthesis. However, the researchers used phytohemagglutinin-stimulated cultures, which were in the pre-DNA-synthesis stage. Only chromosome-type aberrations can be induced in such cells. The investigators offered a possible explanation for the origin of the chromatid-types: either the cells or the serum, some of which is always carried over into the culture with the cells, contain some chemical substance capable of breaking chromosomes during or after DNA synthesis. This, however, does not explain why only some crewmembers showed the increases.

    These experiments correlated with the results of the Gemini 3 and 11 in vitro experiments, in that investigators were unable to demonstrate any dramatic spaceflight effects on chromosome aberrations. None of the observations with the leukocyte chromosome system suggested any special genetic hazards associated with spaceflight. The leukocyte system was recommended to be a feasible biological dosimeter for future missions.
