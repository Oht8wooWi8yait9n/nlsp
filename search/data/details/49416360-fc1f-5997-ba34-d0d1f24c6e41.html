Torso Rotation Experiment

Record ID: E410
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The neurological motor strategy of voluntarily keeping the head fixed on the torso as if wearing a rigid neck brace has been termed "torso rotation", since the upper body must turn to reposition the head. In most subjects, continuous torso rotation on the ground, or in 1-g, gradually leads to motion sickness, most likely because of excessive suppression of the vestibular system, which consists of the organs used for balance. A motor strategy similar to torso rotation is often adopted during weightlessness. While this can be a means of reducing head movements to combat motion sickness, it often precedes the onset of motion sickness symptoms. When experiencing motion sickness, an attempt to minimize head movements by moving in this manner might aggravate rather than alleviate the symptoms.

The main objective of this experiment was to determine if torso rotation in weightlessness leads to motion sickness. Should motor strategies similar to torso rotation be identified as a contributor to space motion sickness, it would then be relatively easy to train crewmembers to avoid such strategies, or to preadapt them by mimicking the provocative movements under controlled laboratory conditions. In addition, excessive vestibular suppression, similar to that caused by torso rotation, may be a major contributor to many kinds of motion sickness, including that experienced in cars, buses, planes, and boats. If so, this information may provide a new perspective on the problem and may suggest methods of preventing the disorder.

APPROACH:
The Torsion Rotation Experiment (TRE) is an experiment to monitor eye, head, and upper torso movements of space flight crew members during routine in-flight activities for evidence of egocentric motor strategies, that is, concentrating on a body frame of reference rather than on the external world. Data had to be obtained as early as possible before the onset of motion sickness to allow a correlation between the degree of suppression and the subsequent severity of motion sickness symptoms. Ideally, eye, head and torso rotation about all three axes (pitch, roll and yaw) should have been measured, but in reality, pitch and roll eye movements could not be determined with sufficient accuracy during space flight. As a result, off-line analysis will identify periods of activity dominated by yaw rotation, which will then be studied for evidence of unusual gaze slip during coordinated eye-head movements. At the same time, head rotation was compared to torso rotation to assess overall motor strategy. The hardware used for this experiment consisted of surface electrodes to record eye movements, a sensor package to monitor head rotations, and a second set of transducers mounted on the subject's back to detect upper body rotations. A microprocessor in the torso unit acquired all data and stored the information in removable modules.

RESULTS:
Preflight
Following periods during which the subject was performing pure yaw-axis head movements, with all calibration maneuvers, saccades, artifacts and periods of inactivity excluded. It was then possible to determine if the eyes were being stabilizing relative to the outside world or relative to the body during self-generated head rotations by plotting eye velocity relative to head velocity on a point by-point basis. If the subject's eyes were always stabilized relative to the outside world, eye velocity (relative to the head) and head velocity (relative to space) would be equal but in opposite directions. By contrast, if the eyes were always stabilized relative to the body, only head velocity would change. In real life, one might expect a tendency to move back and forth between the two strategies (arrows), with a change in weighting towards the latter in the presence of increased vestibular suppression.

Inflight
Maximum head and eye velocities are less than those seen pre-flight. One subject was no longer stabilizing his eyes relative to the outside world, as demonstrated by the fact that only 11 % of the 2419 data points now lie in sectors 3 and 4. However, 69% are now in sectors 1 and 2, indicating a great deal of suppression of the vestibular system in general and the vestibulo-ocular reflex in particular. These results are typical of all 4 subjects on Flight Day 1. Over a period of days, Subjects 2, 3 and 4 tended to return to a more ground-like eye stabilization strategy, but Subject 1 did not. All subjects increased their maximum head velocities after Flight Day 1.

Postflight
The data are similar in every respect to those obtained pre-flight from the same subject, indicating a rapid return to a normal pattern of eye stabilization. This was true of all subjects, although the strategy adopted by Subject 1 was still a hit unusual, as had been noted pre-flight.
