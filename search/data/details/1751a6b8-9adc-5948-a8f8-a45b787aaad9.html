Computational Models of the Cardiovascular System and Its Response to Microgravity

Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Experimental studies of the cardiovascular system in humans, and even in laboratory animals, are necessarily limited in scope because of restrictions on the types of measurements that can be made. Often, the true parameter of interest cannot be measured directly so it must be inferred from other measures. Even when appropriate measurements can be made, the cause of a particular observation may not be evident because of the complexity of the interactions between the numerous components of the system. These and other issues can often be addressed more effectively with the aid of a computational model that simulates the critical components and behaviors of the cardiovascular system. Models depend upon experiments for refinement and specification of their parameters, but also illuminate and enhance the interpretation of experimental results. Investigators view the experiments and computational models as highly synergistic in that the value of one is greatly enhanced by the existence of the other. It could be argued that these are not merely advantages, but essential aspects of a study of orthostatic intolerance.

The primary objectives of this project were to develop a general, modular model of cardiovascular function that contains the essential features associated with the effects of gravity, and to use this model to examine the short-term effects of changes in posture before and after exposure to the microgravity environment. The objective of the currently funded project was to extend a previously developed computational model of the cardiovascular system and to use this model to investigate the short-term (0--5 minutes) beat-to-beat hemodynamic response of the cardiovascular system to abrupt orthostatic stress, such as head-up tilt (HUT) or lower body negative pressure (LBNP). The project aimed at facilitating the understanding of the physiology and treatment (prevention) of orthostatic intolerance in post-flight astronauts.

Specific Aims:
1. Enhance the current version of the cardiovascular simulation to better represent the short-term effects of abrupt orthostatic stress.
2. Verify the model and use it to investigate and evaluate various hypotheses of orthostatic intolerance, and to predict the effects of countermeasures. This objective required extensive collection and archiving of experimental data from collaborators.
3. Complete, document, and disseminate to other investigators a form of the model implemented in JAVA. 4. Apply the cardiovascular model to the clinical problem of intelligent patient monitoring with particular emphasis on establishing an enhanced research database of multiparameter hemodynamic data from intensive care patients.

RESULTS:
Transient Response: To understand better the transient hemodynamic events elicited during transitions from the supine to the head-up posture (or vice-versa), investigators previously conducted a small clinical study in which ten normal healthy volunteers underwent an active change in posture (stand-up), a slow head-up tilt (HUT to 75 degrees over 55 seconds), and a fast head-up tilt (HUT to 75 degrees over 2 seconds). Arterial blood pressure and heart rate were recorded continuously. Simulation results were obtained by matching the model's response to the changes in mean arterial pressure and heart rate at the onset of rapid tilt. Once matched to these two time series, the remaining six transient responses (HR and MAP response at the conclusion of rapid tilt, HR and MAP response at the beginning of slow tilt, and HR and MAP response at conclusion of slow tilt) were then predicted using the same parameter settings. The simulations are capable of reproducing all major features of the transient response within the limits of experimental observations. Furthermore, the simulations are consistent in that matching the model's response to one stress (rapid tilt) allows investigators to predict the outcome of a different stress (slow tilt).

Parameter Estimation: Major effort was allocated to implementing an algorithm that allows for parameter estimation by minimizing an error criterion between model output and hemodynamic time series from astronauts and bed rest subjects. While other error criteria can be envisioned and easily tested, investigators chose to use a least-squares error criterion to measure the distance between the time series. The model is quite non-linear with a large number of parameters that can be optimized in order to fit a given data set. In fact, when optimizing over all parameters it is very likely that the resultant parameter estimates are very sensitive to small perturbations of the data, a phenomenon that is typical of ill-conditioned problems. To circumvent this problem, investigators implemented a subset selection algorithm for gradient-based, non-linear least squares parameter estimation. Subset selection allows for partitioning of the model parameters into well-conditioned and ill-conditioned parameters. The former are likely to be estimated reliably from the given data streams whereas parameter estimates of the latter are likely to be unreliable, and their presence makes the parameter estimation problem very sensitive.
