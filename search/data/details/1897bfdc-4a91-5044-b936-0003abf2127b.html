Hypobaric Hypoxia in Space Data Mining

Record ID: 10_2_Data_Mining
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Extravehicular activity (EVA) is a vital component of human space exploration. The pressure differential between the internal cabin of a spacecraft and a space suit pressurized to 4.3 psia introduces the risk of decompression sickness (DCS). The risk of DCS is currently reduced with lengthy denitrogenation protocols. To reduce both the denitrogenation time and the risk of DCS during EVA, the option of an intermediate habitat pressure of 8.2 psia with 34% oxygen (herein referred to as 8.2/34) has been proposed for NASA’s exploration missions. However, the proposed atmosphere creates partial pressure of inspired oxygen inspired oxygen (PIO2) of 128 mm Hg, which is equivalent to breathing air at approximately 4,000 feet altitude. Coincidentally, one of the denitrogenation protocols used during the Shuttle Program was a staged protocol that exposed astronauts to 10.2 psia and 26.5% oxygen (10.2/26.5). The Shuttle-staged protocol resulted in a mildly hypoxic PIO2 of 127 mm Hg, almost identical to that of the proposed 8.2/34 environment. Terrestrial data suggests that this hypoxic dose is acceptable for unimpeded mission operations; however, the interaction between adaptation to microgravity, the space flight environment, and mild hypobaric hypoxia is uncertain.

This study had the following specific aims:

  1. Determine accurate environmental exposure doses and durations for crewmembers during the shuttle program including hypoxia and elevated carbon dioxide (CO2).
  2. Identify and evaluate any available flight data from the Shuttle program that may aid in clarifying the physiologic and health effects of exposure to mild hypobaric hypoxia in microgravity.
  3. Review terrestrial hypoxia literature with respect to very mild hypoxia at altitudes of 6000 feet or less.

APPROACH:
Retrospective environmental data were examined to determine shuttle mission time at 70.3 kPa (10.2 psia) and partial pressure of oxygen (ppO2) and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (ppCO2) values for missions that performed a Staged pre-breathe protocol. Private medical conferences and post-flight debriefs were examined and data related to 14 possible hypoxia symptoms were compared with equivalent data collected during normoxic Space Shuttle operations at 101.3 kPa (14.7 psia) using logistic regression.

RESULTS:
Over the Space Shuttle program, there were 134.1 days (788.8 person-days adjusted for crew size) under the 70.3 kPa (10.2 psia) Staged condition (average 3.17 ± 2.2 days per mission, maximum 8.1 days) and 258.81 days at 101.3kPa (14.7 psia) and 2192.95 total person-days of normoxic operations. An average of 4.31 potentially hypoxia-related symptoms per mission day was documented under Staged conditions compared with 4.08 per mission day during normoxic operations. Logistic regression analysis found none of the hypoxia signs and symptoms to be significantly associated with the 70.3 kPa (10.2 psia) condition versus the 14.7 psia condition.

Terrestrial experience indicates that chronic exposure to a PIO2 of 876 kPa (137 mmHg) is well-tolerated by healthy humans. Shuttle experience indicates that similar levels of hypoxia in space at a pressure of 70.3 kPa (10.2 psia) resulted in no increased reporting of possible hypoxia related symptoms, and no obvious crewmember performance issues related to completion of mission objectives. However, these results may not be representative of how chronic mild hypoxia interacts with physiologic changes due to microgravity over long duration missions.
