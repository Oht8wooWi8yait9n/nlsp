Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
One medical objective of the Apollo program was to continue the investigation of biomedical changes incident to space flight and to detect, document and understand changes occurring during space flight. On the Apollo 7 through 17 missions, the objective of the endocrine, electrolyte and fluid volume changes study was to evaluate the biochemical changes in Apollo crewmembers. The areas studied were balance of fluid and electrolytes, regulation of calcium metabolism, adaptation to the environment, and regulation of metabolic processes. These studies, like others of the Apollo program, were designed mainly to ensure crew heath and well-being.

During the Apollo Soyuz Test Project (ASTP), the Biochemistry and Endocrinology investigation continued the studies begun during earlier programs. In combination with information from other medical disciplines, data from this investigation allowed for objective assessment of the individual crewmember's health. Preflight data provided baseline information for the detection and identification of postflight physiological changes that may have occurred as a result of exposure to the space environment. The results of the biochemistry and endocrinology studies were not only helpful in the diagnosis of abnormalities, but also provided rationale for accurate remedial procedures.

APPROACH:
Blood samples and 24-hour urine samples were collected pre- and postflight; no samples were collected inflight. Both sample types were collected 30, 15 and 5 days before flight (L-30, 15 and 5). Postflight, blood samples were drawn as soon after recovery as possible (on R+0) as well as 28 days after recovery (R+28). The preflight blood samples were taken in the morning after bladder emptying and prior to food intake. The crew consumed diets of their choosing pre- and postflight, but followed the ASTP diet inflight. Fluids were available when desired.

The blood samples (plasma or serum) were analyzed for glucose, cholesterol, serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, alkaline phosphatase, calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), inorganic phosphate, bilirubin total, creatinine, creatine phosphokinase, lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), osmolality, sodium (Na), potassium (K), chloride (Cl), triglycerides, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), angiotensin I, aldosterone, and cortisol. A limited amount of plasma was available from the immunology studies on days R+1, 8, and 13 and were analyzed for cortisol and aldosterone.

The 24-hour urine samples were analyzed for specific gravity, osmolality, Na, K, Cl, Ca, Mg, inorganic phosphate, uric acid, creatinine, aldosterone, antidiuretic hormone (ADH), cortisol, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and amino acids.

Body fluid compartments were determined on subjects B and C on L-15 days and immediately postflight. These compartments included plasma volume (iodine 125 method), total body water (tritium method), extracellular fluid (sulfur-35 method), and total body exchangeable potassium (potassium 42 method).

Because of the crew's inhalation of toxic fumes during reentry, and concern for the crew's health, the postflight protocol deviated from the originally approved schedule. Additional testing was conducted to try to identify the components of the inhaled gases which affected the crewmen. This was done by analyzing postflight blood samples for the presence of hydrazine and 1-methylhydrazine. Also, the effects of the toxic gas (nitrogen dioxide) inhalation were evaluated by measurements of various forms of urinary hydroxylysine.

RESULTS:
Results from the ASTP crewmembers were similar to findings from previous space flight crews who flew missions of comparable duration. The results of the blood biochemistry analysis showed postflight decreases below preflight values for glucose in subjects B and C, for cholesterol in subjects A and B, and for uric acid in subjects A and C. Increases above preflight values were observed in SGOT, SGPT, BUN, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, LDH, and angiotensin I.

The component isoenzymes of LDH were examined to ascertain the effect of the toxic gas inhalation on the normal isoenzyme distribution. The LDH isoenzyme values show nearly equal distribution of the total LDH increase through all five bands on R+0. By day R+28, the elevation was shifted more toward LDH isoenzyme 1 for subjects A and B. Subject C's results were still more elevated in bands 2, 4, and 5. However, the changes observed in the LDH isoenzyme patterns of the ASTP crewmen did not reflect damage in tissue other than lung.

The postflight 24-hour urine sample analysis showed a decrease in Na, K, and Cl in all three crewmen. Hydrogen ion, cortisol, and aldosterone were all elevated after flight. The body compartments results showed decreases in plasma volume, total body water, extracellular fluid, and total body exchangeable K. The postflight urine results and the body compartment results indicated that body fluids and electrolytes were decreased on return to normal gravity and that a process of conservation by the body had been initiated. This process was shown in particular by the results of angiotensin I and aldosterone measurements.

Collagen Breakdown and Nitrogen Dioxide Inhalation

Measurements of urinary hydroxylysine glycosides indicated that considerable collagen degradation occurred during the reentry phase of the mission. Nitrogen dioxide, a recognized pulmonary irritant, was inhaled during reentry. (Nitrogen dioxide, a reddish brown gas, available commercially, under pressure, as a brown liquid, is in fact an equilibrium mixture of nitrogen dioxide and the colorless nitrogen tetroxide). The first hours after splashdown were marked by crew complaints of tightness of the chest, retrosternal burning sensations, the inability to inhale deeply, and nonproductive coughing. Oxygen was administered to each crewman. Since the crew showed clinical and roentgenographic signs of diffuse chemical pneumonitis, it was likely that collagen degradation occurred in the pulmonary parenchyma.

Similar changes of the urinary excretion of hydroxylysine were not observed in the various astronauts who participated in Apollo or Skylab missions, even though many lasted considerably longer that the Apollo-Soyuz mission. The well-known properties of nitrogen dioxide and it's ability to affect enzymatic activities, cell membrane structures, and structural proteins of the pulmonary parenchyma suggest that the increased urinary excretion of hydroxylysine metabolites might have been related to the acute pulmonary lesions caused by the inhalation of the toxic gas.

Identification of Toxic Gas Components

Attempts to identify the components of the gas inhaled during entry were done by analyzing blood samples for hydrazine and 1-methylhydrazine. Test results were negative. The absence of these compounds was also confirmed by the lack of consistent change in serum cholesterol and triglycerides.
