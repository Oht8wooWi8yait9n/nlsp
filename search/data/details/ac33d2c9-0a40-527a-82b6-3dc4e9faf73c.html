Use of a Molecular Marker of Learning and Memory to Assess Effects of 56Fe Irradiation on Hippocampus-dependent Cognition and Neurogenesis

Record ID: NNX08BA07G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Particulate irradiation in the space environment may pose a significant risk to space flight crews during a mission or upon return to Earth. This may involve the central nervous system, where low radiation doses can induce cognitive or behavioral impairments. Understanding the cellular and molecular basis of cognitive injury will facilitate the establishment of risk estimates and may provide a foundation for the establishment of effective countermeasures or treatments for such injury. Using an animal model, we will determine the extent to which low doses of 56Fe irradiation affect cognitive function and will determine if such changes are associated with the alteration of a gene that is associated with learning and memory, or with the production and maturation of newly born neurons.

APPROACH:
The overall goals of this project were to determine: (1) how low doses of 56Fe ions affected behavioral performance; (2) if specific cellular (e.g. neurogenesis) and molecular factors (immediate early gene Arc) might play a role in cognition; and (3) if those endpoints were affected by inflammatory changes. The experimental methods chosen to accomplish these goals provided a means by which the effects of low radiation doses could be quantified both in terms of behavioral performance as well as specific cellular and molecular endpoints that are associated with learning and memory.

The proposed measures of behavioral performance, which were also used to induce Arc expression, involved a fear conditioning paradigm with 2 versions: one that required the hippocampus (context version) and one that did not require the hippocampus (cued version). The version that required the hippocampus consisted of two types of behavioral paradigms. The first involved no tone shock pairing (-/-) and represented the ability of an animal to recognize an environment previously encountered. Animals that recognized that environment would explore it less, a behavior that is referred to as spatial habituation learning. The second paradigm involved tone shock pairing (+/+) and represented a Pavlovian type of response; that is, animals received a mild shock the first day, and when subsequently placed in the same environment, they would freeze more if they remembered having received an adverse stimulus in that environment the day before. For each version used, thirty minutes after the end of the final testing session on day 2, tissues were collected and prepared for microscopic analyses.

RESULTS:
Investigators completed all analyses related to behavior, neurogenesis, Arc protein expression, and measures of the endogenous inflammatory cells in the brain, the microglia. These endpoints were assessed after whole body irradiation with either 0, 0.25 Gy, 0.5 Gy or 1.0 Gy of 56Fe. All animals underwent cognitive testing 3 months after irradiation using a fear conditioning training- and testing-paradigm. The three-month time point was selected because that time has been previously used to show cognitive deficits after photon or HZE irradiation, as well as changes in Arc expression. Investigators quantified the effects of whole body 56Fe irradiation (600 MeV, 0.5 or 1 Gy) on hippocampus-dependent and hippocampus-independent cognitive performance and determined if those effects were associated with changes in Arc expression in the enclosed and free blades of the dentate gyrus. Whole body 56Fe irradiation impacted contextual but not cued fear freezing and the percentage of Arc-positive cells in the enclosed and free blades. In mice tested for contextual freezing, there was a correlation between Arc-positive cells in the enclosed and free blades. In addition, in mice irradiated with 0.5 Gy, contextual freezing in the absence of aversive stimuli correlated with the percentage of Arc-positive cells in the enclosed blade. In mice tested for cued freezing, there was no correlation between Arc-positive cells in the enclosed and free blades. In contrast, cued freezing in the presence or absence of aversive stimuli correlated with Arc-positive cells in the free blade. These data indicate that there are blade dependent effects after particulate irradiation. More studies are required to address the physiologic significance of such findings.

One of the microenvironmental factors that may influence how irradiation affects cognition is inflammation. Investigators addressed this issue by irradiating animals deficient in a specific chemokine receptor: chemokine receptor-2 (CCR2). CCR2 mediates the recruitment of infiltrating and resident microglia to sites of CNS inflammation, and is upregulated by 56Fe irradiation. CCR2 knock out (KO) and wild-type mice were used to compare effects of 56Fe radiation (600 MeV, 0, 0.25 Gy) on hippocampal function using contextual fear conditioning involving tone shock pairing during training (+/+) and exposure to the same environment without tone shock pairings (-/-). In the -/- condition, irradiation increased freezing and hippocampal Arc protein levels in WT but not CCR2 KO mice, suggesting that a lack of CCR2 was associated with a poorer outcome. In contrast, in the +/+ condition, irradiation reduced freezing but there was no genotype difference. There were no significant correlations between the number of Arc-positive cells in the dentate gyrus and freezing in either genotype but in CCR2 KO mice, there was a trend towards a negative correlation between freezing and Arc-positive cells in the enclosed and free blades. In the +/+ condition, BrdU alone and BrdU/NeuN were reduced in the free blade but not the enclosed blade of CCR2 KO. CCR2 status had no influence on total numbers of newly born activated microglia before or after irradiation, indicating that other mechanisms are involved in the genotype-dependent radiation response.

The investigators’ standard markers of neurogenesis, microglia, and the immediate early gene Arc have been shown to be good predictors of radiation response after relatively high doses of photons or heavy ions. However, in their current studies they were not particularly responsive to low dose irradiation with iron ions. Given those findings they wanted to look for other markers that might track with cognition and provide predictive value after low dose exposures. The formation of long-term memory relies on modulation of synaptic connections in response to neuronal input. This plasticity requires coordinated activity-dependent synthesis of specific mRNAs and proteins that facilitate molecular and structural changes at the synapse. Dendritic spines are bulbous membrane projections of mixed morphology, that form the postsynaptic specializations of the vast majority of excitatory synapses in the central nervous system (CNS) and their structure and density are important factors in synaptic function. Spine morphology can predict both spine stability and synaptic strength, and changes in dendritic spine density or structural reorganization of spines is thought to be important for cognitive processes such as learning and memory.

In their new study of dendritic spines, investigators irradiated young adult male C57BL6 mice with 0 or 0.5 Gy (whole body) of 56Fe ions. Within each radiation treatment group, animals were randomly assigned into cohorts that received cognitive testing using a no shock/no tone paradigm or no cognitive testing, i.e. caged controls. Half of the mice from each specific treatment group were used for the determination of dendritic spine density/morphology and the other half were used for measurement of Arc and other cellular markers. Dendritic spines were visualized using a well-established Golgi staining paradigm. In the CA1 and dentate gyrus of sham-irradiated mice, the behavioral testing enhanced the spine density as compared to behaviorally naïve mice. In contrast, behavioral testing did not affect spine density in irradiated mice. These intriguing data indicate that irradiated mice did not benefit from the environmental enrichment (i.e. behavioral testing) as sham-irradiated mice did.

Finally, investigators initiated and completed a mixed beam study to determine how the brain responds when it is confronted by two different types of irradiation. Mice either received a single whole body exposure of 1.0 Gy protons (n = 24), a single whole body dose of 0.05 Gy 56Fe (n = 24), a single whole body exposure of 1.0 Gy protons immediately before a single whole body dose of 0.5 Gy 56Fe (n = 24) or no irradiation (n =24). Two months following irradiation, all animals receives a daily i.p. injection of bromodeoxyuridine (100 mg/kg) for 7 days, and 1 month later mice were either cognitively tested for novel object recognition or acted as caged controls. Thirty minutes after the onset of the last session of the cognitive testing, animals were sacrificed and their brains bisected into the two hemispheres and then frozen. Tissues were either analyzed for cellular markers of neurogenesis, the immediate early gene Arc, or for molecular/biochemical assays for markers of inflammation/oxidative stress. No cellular, molecular, or biochemical changes were observed after either of the single doses or the combined treatment. This suggests either that the low doses used did not induce any significant alterations, or the specific endpoints chosen for study were not sensitive to minor changes induced by the relatively low doses used.

The data highlights the complexity of assessing how low doses of 56Fe affect hippocampal function. Those data suggest that even a relatively low dose of 56Fe can induce behavioral changes that involve the hippocampus, but the cellular/molecular/biochemical mechanisms responsible are still not yet known.
