Individual Variations in Measures of Muscle and Bone in Ambulatory Subjects<br>

Record ID: ARCbedrest068
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Muscle strength has been shown to predict bone mineral density (BMD) in women, but there is limited data in healthy non-athletic men. The purpose of this study was to determine the relationship of muscle strength and bone mineral density in men, and to define the effect of exercise patterns in non-athletes on BMD and muscle strength. A secondary objective was to test a newly developed instrument, the Mechanical Response Tissue Analyzer, to determine a mechanical property of bone related to its strength (EI or bending stiffness) non-invasively.

APPROACH:
Fifty healthy men, aged 28 to 51 years were recruited for an ambulatory study and gave informed consent. Volunteers were instructed in the daily recording of physical activities and outfitted with a step meter to be worn at all times except during bathing, sleeping and recreational exercise (softball games, etc.). The exercise log was reviewed each week for accuracy with an exercise physiologist. A five-day diet record was also provided with instructions to enter all foods eaten for four week-days and at least one weekend day. Each subject had a series of bone density measurements (Hologic QDR 1000) and a series of strength tests using isotonic weight equipment (Universal and Nautilus). Bending stiffness of the right ulna and right tibia was estimated with a new instrument (MRTA) on 3 separate occasions and on the left ulna and tibia, on one occasion. A single measurement of bone density of the wrist was carried out with a Norland densitometer and the calcaneal bone density by a single energy densitometer, (Osteon, Inc). The right calcaneus was measured three times and the left, twice.

RESULTS:
We obtained the first data on bending stiffness in the ulna and tibia of adult males with our non-invasive instrument and found the left tibia to be stronger than the right. The ulnas of right-handed men showed lower values for EI in the left ulna than in left-handed men whose left and right ulnas were equal. EI was related to the mineral density of the right ulna, and both right and left tibias. EI was also related to the width of the ulna and the strength of the biceps. We concluded that the measurement of EI was reproducible, and was influenced by arm strength.

We found that BMD at all sites was related to the muscle strength in the back and the biceps (recorded in pounds). Body weight correlated with tibia and whole body BMD. Comparison of exercisers and non-exercisers walking mileage showed no difference in miles per day (3.9 miles a day), age, body weight, dietary protein or fat intakes in these two groups. Kcal/d were greater in exercisers than non-exercises due to intakes of carbohydrate. BMD and muscle strength were significantly greater in the exercisers than in the non-exercisers, indicating that hours spent doing recreational exercise is a better predictor of BMD than daily walking. Calcaneal BMD was not related to walking steps, but could be correlated with the amount of time engaged in high load exercise (i.e. loads of more than two body weights).
