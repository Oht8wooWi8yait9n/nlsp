Influence of Microgravity and Extravehicular Activities on Pulmonary Oxygen Exchange

Record ID: DSO 494
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

Early in human space exploration, there were concerns that exposure to microgravity would lead to hypoxemia since cardiopulmonary physiology was disrupted. Any potential disruptions did not impact performance or operational success in short-duration flights, so detailed investigation into pulmonary gas exchange were devised when longer duration flights were possible.

Research continued on Skylab with a normoxic atmosphere of 70% oxygen (O2) at 5 psia and an inspired O2 partial pressure (PIO2) of 148 mmHg. The study found no indication that pulmonary gas exchange in microgravity was impeded. Studies on the Shuttle resulted in the implementation of the shuttle staged denitrogenation protocol in 1984. This protocol allowed for the safe and effective extravehicular activity (EVA) by reducing the risk of decompression sickness (DCS) to an acceptable level in the spacesuit without lengthy in-suit denitrogenation (prebreathe) time.

This experiment furthered the investigations from Skylab and Shuttle and provided additional data on the effects of microgravity and extra vehicular activity (EVA) on impairment of oxygen exchange in the lung.

APPROACH:

Four male astronauts on STS-69 (1995) and four on STS-72 (1996) were exposed on Earth (baseline, taken between 5 and 45 days before launch) to a 20-minute seated acute sequential hypoxic challenge by breathing for 4 minutes at 18.0%, 14.9%, 13.5%, 12.9%, and 12.2% oxygen–balance nitrogen. A Novametrix CO2SMO Model 7100 recorded hemoglobin (Hb) oxygen saturation through finger pulse oximetry (SpO2, %). Measurements were taken at baseline, in-flight, the day of (R+0) and 2 d after (R+2) return to Earth. The same baseline process was followed postflight for R+0 and R+2, with astronauts resting and seated at the KSC in Titusville, FL. In-flight measurements were taken between 17 to 95 hours into the flight. The astronauts breathed the Shuttle atmosphere at 10.2 psia 26.5% O2, which was an equivalent partial pressure of oxygen (PIO2) of 128 mmHg as the 18.0% O2 mixture at sea level for 10 minutes. The Novametrix device was also used for in-flight measurements.

RESULTS:

The persistent increase in pulmonary diffusion capacity (DLCO) and membrane diffusing capacity (Dm) is evidence that pulmonary edema did not occur in microgravity. In addition, gravity imposed a degree of matching between ventilation and perfusion. These increases in pulmonary diffusing capacity and pulmonary capillary blood volume, rapidly revert to preflight levels on return to 1G This results in more uniform filling of the pulmonary capillary bed and an attendant increase in the surface area available for gas exchange. So an otherwise normal lung with no change in the apparent range of ventilation-perfusion (VA/Q ) in microgravity has no impediment to gas transfer. The study shows that exposure to and recovery from microgravity did not significantly alter the gas exchange process that dictates the oxygen saturation (SpO2) response to mild hypoxia in astronauts.

In summary, the findings of the study determined:


  1. There was no difference in astronaut oxygen saturation (SpO2) (about 97%) after breathing 18% O2 for 4 minutes at 760 mmHg before and after spaceflight and breathing 26.5% O2 at 527 mmHg for 10 minutes after days in microgravity both at a partial pressure of oxygen (PIO2) of about 128 mmHg.

  2. There was no difference in astronaut oxygen saturation (SO2) between baseline, R+0, or R+2 during the acute, sequential hypoxic challenge.

  3. There was no acclimatization to mild hypoxia during spaceflight that alters oxygen saturation (SpO2) levels upon return to 1 G.

In conclusion, the multitude of physiological changes associated with microgravity and during recovery from microgravity did not affect astronaut oxygen saturation (SpO2) under hypoxic conditions.
