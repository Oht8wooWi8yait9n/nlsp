Clinical Trial of the Efficacy of Melatonin as a Countermeasure for Entrainment to the Sleep/Wake Schedules Required during Shuttle Missions

Record ID: NAG91035
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Shuttle astronauts are typically exposed to "sleep cycle shifting" due to mission-dependent orbital mechanics and mission requirements. This is equivalent to imposition of a non-24 hour 'day' and is likely to result in misalignment of the endogenous circadian timing system with respect to the desired sleep-wake cycle, even among crewmembers who begin the mission with their sleep-wake cycle well synchronized to mission elapsed time (MET) on the day of launch. Such misalignment is known to produce sleep disturbances, daytime sleepiness, reduced attention, negative mood, slower reaction times, gastrointestinal disorders and impaired daytime alertness. Although appropriately scheduled exposure to bright light has been shown to be capable of resetting the phase of the human circadian pacemaker, existing Shuttle technology precludes use of such bright light delivery systems aboard the Shuttle. Several lines of evidence, including the discovery of melatonin receptors on the suprachiasmatic nucleus in the human hypothalamus and the results of animal and human studies, indicate that melatonin administration may be effective in generating phase shifts of the circadian pacemaker. The current study evaluated the ability of melatonin to entrain the human circadian pacemaker in sighted humans to a shorter-than-24-hour day length.

The current research was designed to test the following hypotheses:

  1. the human circadian pacemaker will be unable to maintain synchronization with a sleep-wake cycle of 23.5 hours, typical of that required on Shuttle missions;
  2. that such loss of synchronization will result in sleep-onset insomnia, decreased sleep efficiency, and decreased daytime alertness and performance resulting from imposition of the 23.5-hour day; and
  3. that a daily dose of melatonin (5 mg), administered one hour before bedtime, will be sufficient to entrain the circadian pacemaker to a 23.5-hour scheduled sleep-wake cycle.

To test the hypotheses, researchers conducted long-term (50-day) inpatient trials utilizing methodologies that allowed direct measurement of endogenous circadian phase and amplitude before, during and after extended duration exposure to a shorter-than-24-hour day length (i.e., similar to that occurring on many space shuttle flights). Each trial took place in an environment with low light intensities similar to that measured aboard the space shuttle on STS-90. It was anticipated that when subjects were scheduled to a 23.5-hour day and room lighting conditions were maintained to 8 lux maximum, that the endogenous circadian pacemaker would oscillate with a period that was different (24.1 to 24.2 hours) from the period of the imposed sleep-wake/work cycle. Failure to entrain the human circadian pacemaker to the 23.5-hour day was expected to be associated with disturbed sleep, and impaired waking alertness and performance. It was also expected that a daily dose of 5 mg of melatonin, one hour prior to scheduled sleep, would entrain the human circadian pacemaker to the 23.5-hour day. The melatonin countermeasure was expected to maintain normal sleep and circadian patterns, and normal waking alertness and performance.

APPROACH:
Five healthy male subjects (age: mean ± SD, 34 ± 7.25; range, 26-44) were studied. All subjects were required to pass a rigorous health screening including medical history, physical and psychological exams, blood and urine chemistries and an electrocardiogram. Toxicology screens for drug use, including alcohol and caffeine verified that participants were drug-free upon admission to the laboratory.

Blood was sampled for melatonin and cortisol during circadian phase estimations (constant routines) and 28-hour sampling windows throughout the intervening study days between constant routines. (In general, during a constant routine protocol, subjects remain under constant conditions, awake, engaged in light, mainly sedentary activity, and consume identical food and fluid every hour.) Core body temperature, waking electroencephalogram (EEG) and polysomnography were recorded throughout most of the protocol. Subject's performance, alertness and mood were assessed every 2 hours during wakefulness using valid, reliable standardized measures, which are sensitive to circadian misalignment and homeostatic sleep drive.

Ceiling-mounted fluorescent lamps with a 4100 K color temperature produced a spectrum of white light. Clear polycarbonate lenses filtered 99.9% of the light in the UV range. Light-dark cycles during the 23.5-hour day were of very dim light (< 8 lux maximum at ~183 cm with the sensor aimed toward the light fixtures) during scheduled wakefulness and darkness during scheduled sleep. Light intensity was measured with a photometer. The < 8 lux maximum light intensity was very similar to the light intensity measured on the middeck of the Space Shuttle during research on the Neurolab STS-90 mission.

Subjects were assigned to one of two cells: A or B. The protocol was divided into 9 segments:

CELL A

Segment A1 (Ambulatory Baseline) consisted of a minimum of 7 days, during which wrist activity and light levels were recorded using ambulatory-recording devices while the subjects were at home on a normal routine maintaining a sleep log.

Segment A2 (Adaptation) began with admission to the Intensive Physiologic Monitoring (IPM) Unit of the Brigham & Women's Hospital on Experimental Day 1 and ended on the morning of Experimental Day 4. Physiologic and neurobehavioral monitoring commenced upon admission on Experimental Day 1 and continued throughout the duration of the study. Subjects continued to sleep and wake at their regularly scheduled times for three normal scheduled nights. A placebo, was administered (single blind) one hour prior to lights out on nights 2-3 to determine if .

Segment A3 (Endogenous Circadian Phase Assessment-ECPA) began on the morning of Experimental Day 4 and ended on the evening of Experimental Day 5. Upon waking on Experimental Day 4, the subjects were placed on a constant routine protocol for 40 hours after which time subjects underwent an 8-hour recovery sleep. The constant routine protocol, as is true with the rest of the protocol from this point forward, was conducted in dim room light. Blood was sampled for melatonin and cortisol until the morning of Experimental Day 5. The constant routine was used to assess the phase and amplitude of the endogenous circadian pacemaker under strictly controlled conditions.

Segment A4 consisted of eighteen successive 23.5-hour days, which began on the morning of Experimental Day 6 and ended on the evening of Experimental Day 23. Each day consisted of 15.67 hours of scheduled wakefulness and 7.83 hours of scheduled sleep. Melatonin (5.0 mg) was administered orally, double blind, one hour prior to lights out each night. Light exposure was maintained at < 8 lux maximum during scheduled wakefulness. In addition to the blood sampled during the constant routines, one 28-hour window of blood sampling took place during Experimental Days 14-15. During this window, blood was sampled for melatonin and for cortisol.

Segment A5 (ECPA Reassessment) consisted of a repetition of the constant routine and recovery sleep that was used in the protocol for subjects in Segment A3.

Segment A6 (Adaptation) consisted of a repetition of the adaptation that was used in the protocol for subjects in Segment A2.

Segment A7 (ECPA Reassessment) consisted of a repetition of the constant routine and recovery sleep that was used in the protocol for subjects in Segments A3 and A5. In addition, this segment was used to reestablish a normal phase relationship between sleep and the phase of the endogenous circadian pacemaker.

Segment A8 consisted of a repetition of the eighteen 23.5-hour-day segment that was used in the protocol for subjects in Segment A4. Placebo was administered, double blind, one hour prior to lights out each night.

Segment A9 (ECPA Reassessment) consisted of a repetition of the constant routine and recovery sleep that was used in the protocol for subjects in Segment A3, A5 and A7.

CELL B

The procedures for this cell were identical to that of Cell A except that in Segment B4 subjects received placebo instead of melatonin one hour prior to each sleep episode and in Segment B8 subjects received melatonin instead of a placebo one hour prior to each sleep episode.

In order to determine whether assessment of endogenous melatonin levels provided a method to assess the status of circadian phase and period, researchers assessed the potential impact of the sleep-wake cycle and associated light-dark cycle on the endogenous circadian rhythm of plasma melatonin. Researchers performed a detailed analysis of the melatonin phase during a 20-hour forced desynchrony protocol.

In this protocol, twelve young healthy subjects (5F, 7M) aged 24.1 ± 3.70 years (mean ± SD) were studied in an environment free of time cues for 18-27 days. Following three 24-hour baseline days (16 hour wake / 8 hour sleep), the subjects were scheduled to 24 cycles of a 20-hour flight day (FD) protocol (13.33 hour wake/6.67 hour sleep). Ambient light intensities were <15 lux during all scheduled wake times and <0.03 lux during all scheduled sleep episodes. Melatonin data were fitted with a non-orthogonal spectral analysis (NOSA) program, which simultaneously fit the imposed (20-hour, 2 harmonics) and endogenous (near 24-hour, 6 harmonics) components (and their interactions) of melatonin in order to calculate the subjects' intrinsic circadian periods t (tau). Each subject's melatonin data were then folded by their fitted t. For each circadian day researchers calculated: 1) duration of melatonin peak (DMP, time between upward and downward crossings of the mean); 2) circadian phase of melatonin peak (MIDPT, midpoint of upward and downward crossings of the mean); and 3) area under melatonin curve (AUC, estimated using the trapezoidal rule, z-scored for each subject). Linear regressions were fitted to the AUC and DMP data from the entire FD protocol to assess any overall change in the mean levels of melatonin secretion during the experiment. To determine whether the timing of the 20-hour sleep/wake schedule systematically modulated the phase or amount of melatonin secretion, each subject's AUC, DMP and MIDPT data were folded by the beat period (mean ± SD = 117.27 ± 0.13 hour) of their &964; (mean ± SD = 24.12 ± 3.10 hour) and the 20-hour FD protocol. For each variable, sine curves were then fitted to the data from all 10 subjects, and a Student's t-test was used to determine whether the fitted amplitude of the sinusoid was significantly different from 0.0.

RESULTS:
Hypothesis 1: Entrainment Studies of the Human Circadian Pacemaker to a 23.5 hour day.
Most subjects who received the placebo treatment failed to entrain to the scheduled 23.5-hour day, whereas most subjects given 5.0 mg of melatonin one hour prior to each bedtime maintained normal phase relationships between the timing of their core body temperature rhythms and the timing of the scheduled sleep-wake cycle. Two of three subjects in the melatonin condition maintained phase angles in the normal range with low levels during sleep suggesting that they entrained to the 23.5-hour day.

Hypothesis 2: Impaired Neurobehavioral Performance During Circadian Misalignment.
The neurobehavioral function during the entrainment studies to the 23.5-hour day with and without melatonin (n=3) was also examined. Daily performance averages were computed for subjects who completed both melatonin and placebo conditions. Researchers assessed vigilance performance every two hours during wakefulness using the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT). The primary outcome measures analyzed were median reaction time and lapses (response times > 500 ms). Results of these analyses showed that reaction time performance and lapses in attention were similar in the melatonin and placebo conditions. Researchers also assessed working memory, cognitive speed and accuracy trade-offs using the Addition Performance Task and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test. Both tasks showed significant interaction effects (P < 0.05) between Drug Condition and Day such that performance continued to improve (i.e., learning occurred) in the melatonin but not the placebo condition. Subjective Sleepiness on the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale was significantly lower (less sleepiness) near the end of the protocol for the melatonin compared to the placebo condition (P < 0.05).

Hypothesis 3: Progress on the relationship between melatonin and circadian entrainment.
The AUC (p<0.20) and the DMP (p<0.15) did not show any significant changes in their mean levels over the course of the FD protocol. The DMP also showed no systematic alteration in relation to the 20-hour FD regimen. However, the AUC (p<0.0001) and the MIDPT (p<0.0001) manifested systematic modulations in relation to the 20-hour FD protocol. When sleep episodes were scheduled near circadian phase 240°, MIDPTs occurred approximately one hour later in the circadian day than when sleep was scheduled to occur near circadian phase 60°. When sleep episodes were scheduled near circadian phase 180°, the AUC was approximately 1.5 standard deviation larger than when sleep was scheduled to occur near circadian phase 0°.

These data demonstrated that the quantity of melatonin secreted and the phase of the midpoint of the melatonin curve are significantly affected by the timing of the sleep/wake, light/dark, upright/supine conditions during a 20-hour FD protocol. The data also demonstrated that the endogenous melatonin rhythm was robust and provided an accurate estimate of the period of the pacemaker under the current space-flight conditions of dim light.

The current results suggest that:

  1. additional research is necessary to evaluate the impact of non-24-hour sleep-wake/work schedules on circadian entrainment, sleep and performance during ground-based simulations and space-flight conditions;
  2. a dose of 5.0 mg of melatonin taken daily one hour prior to bedtime appears to be well tolerated and may be able to entrain the human circadian pacemaker to a shorter-than-24-hour day in some but not all individuals. Thus, additional research is needed to determine the effective dose necessary to entrain all individuals to non-24-hour day lengths. A flight-based test of daily administration of a larger dose of melatonin (=5.0 mg per day) as a countermeasure to circadian misalignment during space flight is warranted since melatonin appears to hold promise as an entraining agent at this higher dose;
  3. individual differences, likely due to differences in the intrinsic circadian period, make some individuals more vulnerable to circadian misalignment. These individual differences in vulnerability to circadian misalignment suggest that robust countermeasures need to be developed to ensure entrainment of all astronauts to a variety of imposed sleep-wake schedules during space flight.
  4. some sleep disturbances in space are likely related to a failure to maintain entrainment of the human circadian pacemaker to the imposed schedule, especially for missions with schedules that deviate from the 24-hour day; and
  5. measurement of the endogenous melatonin rhythm can be used to assess the status of the circadian time keeping system in humans under the current dim lighting conditions of space flight.
