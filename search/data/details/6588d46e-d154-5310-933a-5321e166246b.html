Response of Thyroid to Space-like Radiation Fields

Record ID: NAG91452
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Glandular tissues are sensitive to carcinogens and ionizing radiation; thus, they are common sites for tumor development. Thyroid tissue is a glandular tissue that can grow into unhindered three-dimensional shapes and can be used as a realistic model to investigate how other tissue types respond to ionizing radiation on different levels. It is important to understand the effects of ionizing radiation on the body's tissues to ensure the protection of crewmembers during long-duration space flight. A mission to Mars is of particular concern, because astronauts would be subjected to more types of chronic low-dose and acute high-dose radiation as they travel away from the protection of Earth's atmosphere.

The objective of this study was to characterize the pattern of radiation regulated gene expression in thyroid tissue using low-dose ionizing radiation. Tissue models were subjected to various doses of low, medium and high linear energy transfer (LET). This radiation exposure protocol enabled the investigator to determine which gene profiles reflect the critical changes in structure and function of tissue that lead to changes in cells. The resulting information will improve radiation exposure risk assessment for long-duration space flight.

APPROACH:
In a simulated low Earth orbit model, thyroid tissue models, or analogs, were chronically exposed to gamma rays delivered at a low-dose rate. The thyroid tissue analog will thus become "primed" by the low-dose exposure, after which it was subjected to galactic cosmic ray models of low, medium and high linear energy transfer (LET). The "priming" effect was induced to test the investigator's hypothesis that low-dose chronic exposure invokes cell and tissue level homeostatic mechanisms that cause structural and functional changes reflected in their gene expression profiles. It is believed that after thyroid tissue cells transition to the "primed" state, they will exhibit an altered response to an acute radiation exposure and will show an increased resistance to changes that lead to cellular disease relative to "unprimed" cells.

RESULTS:
These studies indicate that protons and HZE particles (carbon and iron) have unique signatures and result in more dramatic responses to low dose radiation than gamma rays. In fact the data results showed that protons have the greater response, in terms of the number of modulated genes, than carbon or iron ions. In the studies of acute responses and those of the challenge exposures indicated that protons behave more like HZE particles than they do gamma-rays or low LET radiation. The findings of several of experiments reveal protons and carbon were more affecting of gene expression than gamma-rays or iron ions. Suggesting that they have the potential to cause greater effects than would be predicted based on their intermediate LET’s influence.

The investigators three-dimensional tissue analog model represents an intermediate state between the isolated follicles found in flask-grown cultures and the highly organized multifollicular assembly of the intact thyroid gland. The identification of differences in gene expression found when comparing FRTL-5 cells grown in flasks with those grown in bioreactors supports the biological differences measured previously using these culture conditions. The results indicate that the tissue analogs and flask-grown follicles have substantially dissimilar responses to radiation stress. Thus, the method of culturing (flasks vs. bioreactors) dramatically impacts how these cells respond to ionizing radiation. It is likely that cells in the bioreactor cultures and the flask cultures represent different microenvironments that are controlled by different regulatory factors, and in turn respond differently to ionizing radiation. From this perspective, it will still be necessary to determine which of these gene expression patterns represent the best approximation of the radiation response in vivo. However, the previous reports of normal tissue-like properties of engineered organoids suggest that our bioreactor-generated thyroid tissue will exhibit radiation-induced changes in gene expression that more closely approximates the in vivo condition. Thereby providing a reasonable approximation to in vivo and thus reducing the need for animals in ascertaining risk. In conclusion, the results support that at low doses of radiation the gene expression response of tissues is significantly less than would be predicted from cells grown in vitro. Therefore, risk based on in vitro data will lead to an overestimation of the risk of exposure to low dose (< 1Gy) radiation. Secondly, the responses to relatively high doses resulted in gene expression patterns that were not significantly different whether the cells were grown in flasks or as tissue analogs. This suggests that extrapolation of data from high dose; high dose rate experiments are less dependent on higher order cellular integration than lower dose responses.
