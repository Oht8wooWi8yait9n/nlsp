The Response of Single Human Cells to Zero Gravity

Record ID: S015
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Studies with centrifuges demonstrated that gravitational acceleration has a significant influence on cell biology systems. The purpose of this experiment was to determine the effects of zero-gravity on living human cells during and subsequent to the Skylab 3 mission. A strain of diploid human embryonic lung cells, WI-38, was chosen for this purpose. In general, this experiment was designed to detect the effects of zero-gravity on cell growth rates and on cell structure as observed by light microscopy, transmission electron microscopy and scanning electron microscopy, and histochemistry. Studies of the effects of zero-gravity on the cell function and the cell cycle were performed by time lapse motion picture photography and microspectrophotometry. Subsequent study of the returned living cells included karotyping, G- and C- banding, and analyses of the culture media. Some of the living cells returned were stored by deep freeze techniques for possible future experiments.

APPROACH:
The Woodlawn Wanderer 9 was used inflight to support the growth of human embryonic lung cells. Cell cultures were maintained with the proper replacement of media with new fresh media. The cells were kept at a constant temperature of 36 degrees Celsius and at a constant pressure of one atmosphere in a hermetically sealed compartment. Two phase-contrast time-lapse motion pictures of living cells for 28 days was used to collect data on the cell cycle and mitosis. An automated system fixed the cells at predetermined intervals for postflight examination under the light microscope, scanning electron microscope, and transmission electron microscope. Cultures were maintained at approximately 22 degrees Celsius after the first 12 days of the mission for the return of intact cells for subsequent subculture. The cell cultures brought back to ground for subculture were analyzed for chromosomal aberrations, and the cell culture media was analyzed to determine chemical composition.

Cellular growth rates were determined by cell counting procedures accomplished with the aid of a phase-contrast contrast microscope using a grid reticle in a 10X magnification eyepiece. Postflight, cell counting was performed with the 15X eyepiece due to the fact that the high density of the cell population made it difficult to determine single cells. Time lapse films were analyzed with a projector which permitted projection 1-8, 16, and 24 frames/second. Cells were counted every 3 hours. Cell multiplication was determined by adding the number of observed mitoses and mitotic activity, which was calculated from the average cell density. The rate of cell migration was determined by covering the projection screen with paper and tracing the location of the nucleus of individual cells every hour.

Whole coverslips with attached cell monolayers in 4 percent glutaraldehyde fixative with cacodylate buffer were processed for transmission electron microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and phase contrast microscopy. Scanning microspectrophotometry was performed on selected areas of the nuclei using a scanning microscope photometer having a 1-micron measuring at 1 micron intervals at 540 nanometers. Subcultures were incubated overnight with fresh growth medium, then harvested with standard colchimid arrest and hypotonic treatment procedures. Banding analysis was performed on air dried slides. Five cells from control cultures were analyzed for G-band pattern and 10 cells were analyzed for C-band pattern. At least 10 cells of the flight culture were analyzed for G-band pattern and 20 cells were analyzed for C-band pattern.

RESULTS:
Ten cultures were fixed during the first 12 days of flight. Growth curves, DNA microspectrophometery, phase microscopy, and ultrastructural studies of the fixed cells revealed no effects of a zero-gravity environment on the ten cultures. Two cultures were photographed by means of phase time-lapse cinematography during the first 27 days of the flight. Analysis of the films revealed no differences in mitotic index, cell cycle, and migration between the flight and control cells. No differences between the flight and control cell cultures were observed in the karotype and chromosome banding. Minor unexplained differences have been found in the biochemical constituents of the used flight and control media.
