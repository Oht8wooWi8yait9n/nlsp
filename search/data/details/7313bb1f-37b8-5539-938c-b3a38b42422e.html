Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
During space flight, space motion sickness (SMS) and perceptual disturbances have led to reductions in performance efficiency and sense of well-being. During entry and immediately after landing, such disturbances could have a serious impact on the ability of the commander to land the Orbiter, as well as on the abilities of all crewmembers to egress from the Orbiter, particularly in a non-nominal condition or following extended stays in microgravity. An understanding of spatial orientation and motion perception is essential for developing countermeasures for SMS and perceptual disturbances during space flight and upon return to Earth. Countermeasures for optimal performance in-flight and a successful return to Earth require the development of preflight and in-flight training to help astronauts acquire and maintain a dual adaptive state.

This study was intended to enhance the understanding of adaptation to space flight and readaptation after flight on various aspects of human spatial orientation, neurosensory, sensory-motor, physiological, and perceptual functions and on autonomic gastric functions associated with space motion sickness (SMS). This experiment correlated the postflight recovery of postural equilibrium control, head and gaze stability, readaptation changes in vestibular function, and examined the relationship between voluntary oculomotor performance and passive vestibulo-ocular responses following space flight. Changes in stomach nerve response to food consumption over the first few days of flight were to be observed and analyzed, and evaluated when SMS symptoms occurred. The investigators were also interested in how nerve activity correlated with the symptoms before, during, and after their occurrence.

APPROACH:
Protocol A
Voluntary Head Movement (VHM)
Standard electro-oculography (EOG) techniques recorded horizontal and vertical eye position. A head mounted tri-axial rate sensor measured head velocity in the pitch, yaw and roll planes. The BOG and rate sensor signals were collected with the Superpocket physiological monitoring system and recorded on a TEAC cassette recorder. A goggle assembly controlled visual conditions via an electronic shutter, changing instantly from a clear state to an opaque state according to the experiment protocol. A cruciform target and four additional visual targets were mounted on the middeck locker for on-orbit data collection and on a screen during baseline data collection. A microcassette recorder delivered a sinusoidally-modulated audio tone during one portion of the VHM protocol. Tasks performed in-flight were tested pre- and postflight as well. Subjects were asked to report perceptual sensations associated with each stimulus.

VHM Inflight - Four of the six inflight experiments were designed to investigate voluntary oculomotor function. Two additional experiments were performed pre- and postflight only investigated this function. The protocols involved a combination of eye and/or head movements to acquire targets along a visual display. Calibrations of eye and head signals were performed. Each task was repeated three times in both the horizontal and vertical planes. The tasks are described below:

  • Head Calibration - the subject directed a head-mounted laser spot to the central target and moved their head to pre-selected targets in both the horizontal and vertical planes.
  • Eye Calibration - with head fixed, subject acquired a successive sequence of target positions in the horizontal and vertical planes, visually fixating on each target for two seconds.
  • Target Acquisition - a cruciform target arrangement, located 34 inches from the subject's eyes was used to display a series of targets in both the horizontal and vertical planes. Subjects acquired each target with both a head and eye movement.
  • Gaze Stabilization - the subject first visually fixated on a wall-fixed target with the head in a central position. When the goggles became opaque and vision was occluded the subject rotated the head while maintaining ocular fixation on the just-seen wall-fixed target. When the goggles became clear the subject refixated the target with the eyes only. The subject then rotated the head back to center, keeping eyes on the target.
  • Spontaneous/Gaze Nystagmus - was measured with the crewmember fixating on a wall target, and then imagining the same wall-fixed target with vision occluded, and then with the eyes closed. Gaze nystagmus, or the effect of a change in eye position was evaluated by having the crewmember fixate on imaginary 40 degree targets (vision occluded) in both the vertical and horizontal planes for 20 seconds per imaginary target. Measurement of eye movements continued for an additional 20 seconds when the eye position was returned to straight ahead gaze.
  • Sinusoidal Head Oscillations -
    With Vision: Subjects attempted to maintain visual fixation on a wall-fixed target while smoothly oscillating his/her head in yaw or pitch in cadence with an audio tone that is sinusoidally modulated to sweep over the bandwidth of interest (chirp).
    Without Vision: Subject repeated the chirp with vision occluded while attempting to maintain ocular fixation on the remembered location of the target. The subject repeated this entire procedure in each plane for each condition.

    VHM Pre/Postflight

  • Non-Predictable Target Tracking - for these trials, the target was initially stationary in the center of the field of view. At an unpredictable time, the target began to move at constant velocity, either rightward, leftward, upward, or downward. The onset time of target motion, direction of motion, target velocity, and final target displacement was randomized.
    Predictable Target Tracking - the target oscillated at 0.3 Hz. For each trial, tracking was performed for at least six cycles.
  • Positional Nystagmus - spontaneous eye movements were collected with the crewmember in seven different static positions:
    1. Seated upright
    2. Supine
    3. Supine with head turned to the right
    4. Supine with head turned to the left
    5. Supine with head and neck extended over the edge of the examination table (head and neck will be supported by test operator)
    6. Body and head turned to the right side
    7. Body and head turned to the left side

    Optokinetic Nystagmus (OKN)
    A binocular Optokinetic Stimulator provided a full visual field optokinetic stimulus during the OKN protocol. Standard electro-oculography (EOG) techniques recorded horizontal and vertical eye position. The EOG signals were collected with the Superpocket physiological monitoring system and recorded on a TEAC cassette recorder. Tasks performed inflight were tested pre and postflight as well. Subjects were asked to report perceptual sensations associated with each stimulus.

    Postural Equilibrium (pre/postflight)
    Measurements pertaining to postural equilibrium were collected using standard electromyography (EMG) techniques on the upper thigh and lower calf segments of the subject's left leg, sway-bars situated at the shoulders and hips, and force transducers situated in the foot plate of the posture platform. In addition, overhead and full field video was recorded through the entire test session. The subject's primary task was to maintain a normal upright stance.

    Head and Gaze Stability During Locomotion (pre/postflight)
    Whole body movement during locomotion on a treadmill and during a jump task was videotaped and the locations of retroreflective markers placed on specific locations on the body were analyzed with the aid of a computerized motion analysis system. In addition, eye movement and leg muscle activity during these tasks were recorded using standard electro-oculography (EOG) and electromyography (EMG), respectively.

    Path Integration ((pre/postflight)
    To examine the cognitive processing of spatial orientation information, whole body movement during locomotion along a previously memorized path (parallelogram or square) were videotaped and the locations of retroreflective markers placed on specific locations on the body were analyzed with the aid of a computerized motion analysis system.

    Protocol B
    This experiment marked the first attempt to obtain measurements of the stomach muscle activity in humans during space flight. In this study, myoelectric activity of the stomach was examined following a meal. Gastric activity associated with space motion sickness was measured using EGG in which electrical impulses associated with stomach secretion and movements were recorded. To perform this EGG, three electrodes were situated on the abdomen.

    Each subject participated in two preflight training sessions which occurred at 12 months prior to launch (L-12) and at 10 months prior to launch (L-10). To compare subject inflight EGG data that were associated with eating to eating under normal ground conditions, there were two baseline data collection sessions. These sessions occurred after both preflight training sessions. During one baseline data collection session, subjects were given fluids and during the other session, they were given a small meal. Subjects fasted for 6.5 hours before a data collection session. When the small meal was consumed, data collection began 20 minutes before the 5 to 10-minute meal and continued for three hours following the meal. To ensure proper and consistent placement of EGG electrodes on the abdomen inflight, a permanent marker was used to mark the correct locations after a meal had been consumed within eight days prior to flight.

    The EGG data were collected using a portable recording instrument designed for space flight. The electrodes amplified the signal at its source and the main part of the recording instrument amplified it further. A one Mb RAM card held twenty-four hours of data. The hardware was lightweight and worn on a belt. It was capable of registering input from the crewmember regarding food or water consumption and SMS symptoms. There were five events that were coded by number: eating, drinking, stomach awareness/discomfort, nausea and vomiting. When a crewmember experienced one of these events, he pressed the event marker with the correct code number and the event with its corresponding Mission Elapsed Time (MET) was recorded. Insertion of event markers did not disrupt data collection or logging.

    The EGG equipment was donned as early as possible in the flight. Continuous data collection occurred for at least the first three flight days. The electrodes, RAM card and battery were replaced every 24 hours.

    RESULTS:
    Protocol A
    Results for this portion of the experiment aren’t available at this time.

    Protocol B
    Crewmembers responded normally to ingestion of a meal preflight, showing an increase in the amplitude and power of the normal three gastric cycles per minute. An abnormal decrease in the dominant power occurred on Flight Day (FD) 1 for all subjects but the response returned to normal on FD3. Individual differences were observed and one crewmember actually showed a slight abnormal preflight response to the meal. All three crewmembers experienced moderate to severe loss of appetite on FD1 and two of them showed mild loss of appetite on FD3. When appetite returned and SMS symptoms resolved, the gastric responses after a meal returned to normal.

    Two nausea events were recorded by one crewmember and one vomiting event was recorded by another crewmember. Percent spectral power was assessed to determine the regularity of normal gastric slow waves. The percent of time that normal waves are recorded by the EGG and the percent of time that abnormal (faster or slower) waves are recorded is the percent spectral power. EGG data showed that in the first episode of nausea 60% of spectral power was associated with deviations in normal contraction cycles before nausea was reported. Forty percent of the power was associated with fewer contractions per minute and 20% was associated with higher contractions per minute. The percent of spectral power associated with these alterations of rhythmic contractions increased to approximately 75% for 15 minutes following the report of nausea, then decreased to about 45%, which was noted at 45 minutes following the nausea report.

    In the second report of nausea, the percent spectral power associated with altered stomach contractions was high (75-80%) throughout the entire period of EGG recording. The researchers speculated that the data indicated that this second report of nausea lasted longer than the first. Also of note was that the first nausea report data corresponded to slower gastric contractions two to three times more than faster ones throughout the entire recording period. In the second episode, data was associated with faster frequency contractions during the time following the nausea report. Other studies have shown that faster frequency contractions are associated with motion sickness as compared to slower frequency contractions.

    Similar to the nausea, the EGG data showed altered stomach contraction rhythms before and up to 30 minutes following the vomiting episode. For this entire time, 75 to 80% of the spectral power was associated with abnormal rhythms. Forty-five minutes after the report the gastric activity was normal. One interesting point is that this crewmember specifically stated that he had no symptoms prior to vomiting, which is a fairly common characteristic of SMS. For both nausea and the vomiting episodes, crewmembers had altered rhythmic gastric contractions at least 30 minutes prior to reporting the symptom.

    The abnormally fast neural myoelectric waves that are detected by EGG have been associated in other studies with decreased spontaneous movement of the stomach and the absence of stomach contractions. Findings in this study indicate that spontaneous stomach movement may be decreased during both mild and severe symptoms of SMS, which occur during the first few days of space flight. The perturbations of this movement may affect absorption of nutrients and oral medications for both astronauts and motion sickness victims on Earth. Since astronauts must treat several physiologic abnormalities that occur in space, it is important that drug absorption and bioavailability be preserved for pharmaceutical effectiveness. Also, crewmembers must maintain adequate nutrition for physically and mentally demanding work, much of which is critical to safety of the crew or the vehicle.

    This investigation utilized a noninvasive technique to successfully record human stomach myoelectric activity in space over a long period. Future work should involve confirmation of these findings and further characterization of the altered stomach rhythms in relation to the presence of nausea and vomiting, especially in regards to the precise timing of the EGG data and the subjective reports of the SMS symptoms. This technique could potentially be utilized for monitoring stomach muscle and nerve conditions as an early warning before SMS symptoms appear. It could also potentially assist flight surgeons in making decisions regarding medication administration and timing. However, in order to function in such a manner, the data would have to be available real-time or near real-time.
