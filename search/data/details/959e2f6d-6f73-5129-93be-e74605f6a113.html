Are Medications Involved in Vision and Intracranial Pressure Changes Seen in Spaceflight?

Record ID: VIIP_Pharm
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
It has recently been reported that intracranial pressure (ICP) and/or vision changes, have occurred in a number of long-duration astronauts. Some crewmembers have experienced changes in their vision after long-duration space flight on the International Space Station (ISS). These impairments include visual performance decrements, development of cotton-wool spots or choroidal folds, optic-disc edema, optic nerve sheath distention, and/or posterior globe flattening with varying degrees of severity and permanence. These changes are now used to define the visual impairment/intracranial pressure (VIIP) syndrome.

The reasons for these potentially debilitating medical issues are currently unknown. The potential role of administered medications has not yet been examined, but it is known that many medications can have side effects that are similar to VIIP symptoms. Some medications raise blood pressure, which can affect intracranial pressure. Many medications that act in the central nervous system can affect intracranial pressures and/or vision. About 40% of the medications in the ISS kit are known to cause side effects involving changes in blood pressure, intracranial pressure and/or vision. For this reason, investigators aimed to study the potential relationship between ISS medications and their risk of causing or exacerbating VIIP-like symptoms.

APPROACH:
This study employed a two-fold strategy to evaluate the role of medications in crewmembers' visual problems. First, the NASA Life Sciences Data Archive (LSDA) and Lifetime Surveillance of Astronaut Health (LSAH) data repositories were queried for crew health information regarding in-flight use of medications by all crewmembers who received sufficient testing to be assigned a Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) severity score. Use of medications was correlated in affected crew compared to the control group (CPG = 0) to assess the role of these medications in vision and ICP changes seen in space flight. Secondly, the FDA Adverse Event Reports were used a terrestrial source of data. These are publicly available and represent hundreds of thousands of cases in each quarterly data set. The quarterly reports were compiled into a database format and queried for data of interest. This resource represents a huge database; incorporating it into the study was an attempt to compensate for the extremely small sample number in the NASA data archive. For this data mining study, data associated with adverse events involving vision changes and intracranial pressure changes was examined.

RESULTS:
Medication use during space flight missions was similar to that during the Space Shuttle flights and in adult medicine, with the exception that usage of sleep aids was about 10 times higher during space flight missions.
