Effect of Microgravity on the Cornea of a Refractive Surgery Subject

Record ID: Visual_Acuity
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
NASA recently approved Photorefractive Keratectomy (PRK) and Laser Assisted in situ Keratomileusis (LASIK) refractive surgery for astronauts and astronaut candidates. The current microgravity environment encountered by astronauts (and therefore their corneas) involves exposure to hypobaria (pertaining to gaseous pressure lower than normal) and hyperbaria (pertaining to or utilizing gaseous pressure greater than normal) as well as variations in oxygen concentrations. The specific effects of hyperbaria and hyperoxia (an increased amount of oxygen in tissues and organs) on LASIK and PRK corneas are unknown. The effect of the combination of factors such as hypobaria (4.3 psi) coupled with 100% oxygen is also unknown. However, since any refractive change during exposure to hypobaria, hyperbaria and hyperoxia is based upon slow metabolic changes within the corneal tissue it is fully expected that any refractive changes will be slow and subtle in nature. The effect of microgravity on the cornea of refractive surgery subjects is also unknown. Physiologic changes such as a rise in intraocular pressure and possible choroidal (vascular layer of the eye) volume changes may occur in an eye exposed to microgravity. However, it is not expected that these physiologic changes will have an effect over and above that which would be experienced by a normal cornea.

The current microgravity environment encountered by astronauts and their eyes involves exposure to low pressure and high pressure as well as variations in oxygen concentrations. The main goal of this study is to establish the normal response of the eye to microgravity exposure and whether changes may take place in the eye during exposure to microgravity.

APPROACH:
This study involved the participation of a single subject during a short-duration (approximately 10-12 days) space flight. Corneal shape, visual acuity, refraction, and intraocular pressure were measured for changes from the preflight assessment. Linear regression was used to assess trends in the time course of these changes. Most of these tests are routinely performed during annual eye exams. Additional tests that are part of this study involved routine tests for subjects prior to eye surgery such as LASIK surgery. There were no invasive tests as part of this study. The only medications that were used in this study were cycloplegic (dilates the pupils) and topical anesthetic drops.

The subject participated in the assessment of vision acuity using eye charts and a vision questionnaire on flight days (FD) 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8. The subject set up the eye charts and read letters to check vision, used lenses to see if the visibility of the chart improved, completed the vision questionnaire, and recorded answers on the paper provided. Postflight, the subject participated in the same testing as during the preflight phase. Postflight sessions were scheduled for R+1, R+14 and if clinically advised at R+30 days.

All training and pre-flight baseline data collection took place at Johnson Space Center and Coastal Eye Associates in Houston, TX. Postflight data collection took place at the Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Centre (GCTC) in Star City Russia, Johnson Space Center, and Coastal Eye Associates.

RESULTS:
Although the astronaut’s eyes were subjected to a wide spectrum of physiologic changes, no measurable changes in distance or near visual acuity, no hyperopic shift in refraction, or change in the amplitude of accommodation were documented during launch, 12 days of microgravity, and reentry. This information suggests that the subject’s post-PRK cornea was not significantly affected by these conditions. The astronaut did experience some ocular symptoms, most notably tired eyes reported as “slight but not troublesome” to “a little troublesome” during the first half of the flight. He also noted irritated and painful eyes to a “slight but not troublesome” level, and tearing and itchiness to a “perhaps very slightly” level. He reported no dry eyes, light sensitivity, visual discomfort, or headaches related to vision. These symptoms are probably best interpreted as very low level and possibly more relate to sleep deprivation than any space-related ophthalmic changes or any refractive surgery–specific issue. The astronaut reported facial edema and pressure in his face, eyes, and sinuses within minutes of reaching microgravity. These symptoms are commonly reported and are thought to be caused by cephalad microgravity fluid shifts.
