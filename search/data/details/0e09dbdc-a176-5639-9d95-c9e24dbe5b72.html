Summary / Description

NASA’s Artemis campaign is a series of missions designed to return humans to the Moon in collaboration with international and commercial partners. These missions aim to establish a sustainable presence on the lunar surface and lay the groundwork for future crewed missions to Mars.

Artemis II will be the first crewed mission of the Artemis campaign. Its primary objective is to test the Orion spacecraft with astronauts onboard, including evaluations of the Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) and rehearsals of critical operations for future Artemis missions. The mission will launch from Launch Complex 39B atop NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS) Block 1 rocket.

The Orion capsule, named Integrity, will carry four astronauts:
  • Reid Wiseman (NASA) – Commander, on his second spaceflight
  • Victor Glover (NASA) – Pilot, also on his second spaceflight
  • Christina Koch (NASA) – Mission Specialist, on her second spaceflight
  • Jeremy Hansen (CSA) – Mission Specialist, making his first spaceflight

Launch preparations are ongoing at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center. The launch stack was assembled within the Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB) with Integrity and the SLS rocket being mated to each other along with other preparatory tasks. On the morning of January 17, 2026 at 07:04 EST (12:04 UTC) Crawler Transporter 2 began the task of transporting the Artemis II stack to launch pad 39B. The crawler transporter moved the approximately 11-million-pound stack a little over 4 miles at a maximum speed of 0.82 miles per hour, arriving at pad at 18:42 EST (23:42 UTC).

At the pad the stack underwent final preparations for launch including a wet dress rehearsal (WDR) where launch teams simulated a countdown including loading cryogenic propellants into the rocket.

The first WDR began with call to stations on February 1, 2026 and continued through to the early hours of February 3, 2026. The WDR identified several open work items to address in the rocket and ground systems.

After implementing the necessary fixes on the rocket, ground teams conducted a second WDR, beginning with the call to stations on February 17, 2026, and successfully concluding on February 19, 2026. Following the successful completion of this second rehearsal, mission managers tentatively set March 6, 2026, as the target date for the first operational launch attempt of Artemis II. However, an issue with the helium purge system in the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) later arose, requiring the rocket to be rolled back to the VAB to allow ground teams access to investigate and resolve the problem.
Once repairs where completed in the VAB ground teams prepared the rocket for roll out back to LC-39B. Roll out commenced on March 20, 2026 at 00:20 EDT (04:20 UTC), with the stack arriving at the pad at 11:21 EDT (15:21 UTC).

Mission managers are currently targeting a launch window on April 1, 2026 that will open at 18:24 EDT (22:24 UTC) and last for approximately 120 minutes. Launch Period 19 has additional launch opportunity windows daily through April 6, 2026.

Once launched, the SLS Block 1 rocket will place Integrity into a high earth orbit, where the crew will conduct a 23.5-hour checkout of Orion’s systems to verify readiness for deep space operations. During this phase, the crew will separate from the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS), the upper stage of the SLS, and perform proximity operations demonstrations to evaluate the handling characteristics of the Orion spacecraft and its European Service Module (ESM).

Following the completion of the proximity operations demonstrations, Orion’s main engine will perform a trans-lunar injection (TLI) burn, placing Integrity on a lunar free-return trajectory. The spacecraft will then coast for approximately four days toward the Moon.

At the Moon, Integrity will conduct a lunar flyby, passing between 5,000 and 9,000 nautical miles (9,260 to 16,668 kilometers) from the lunar surface. The exact flyby distance will depend on the actual launch date. During the flyby, the crew will conduct scientific observations, including imaging of the Moon’s far side.

At the mission’s apogee, its farthest point from Earth, the Artemis II crew will have traveled approximately 280,000 miles (450,616 kilometers) from Earth. This will be the longest distance humans have ever traveled from Earth, surpassing the record set in 1970 by Apollo 13.

Continuing along its free-return trajectory, Integrity will begin a four-day coast back to Earth. Prior to entry interface, the crew will redon their pressure suits in preparation for reentry. The Orion capsule will then perform a splashdown in the Eastern Pacific Ocean, off the coast of San Diego, California.

Awaiting the crew in the recovery zone will be NASA’s ground recovery forces, working in partnership with the United States Navy aboard a San Antonio-class amphibious transport dock ship. The joint Navy-NASA team will recover the crew and transport them back to NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, marking the conclusion of the mission.

In addition to the various technical test objectives for the Orion spacecraft, the crew will participate in science for the Human Research Program aimed at examining human health and performance during deep space exploration missions. The data collected from these experiments will support future missions in the Artemis campaign and future human exploration of Mars.
