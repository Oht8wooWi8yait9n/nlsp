Lignin Formation and Effects of Microgravity: a New Approach

Record ID: 9307247
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

The focus of this experiment was to establish the effect of the microgravity environment on the ability of plants to form a reinforcement tissue known as compression wood. On Earth, woody plants produce this distinctive reinforcement tissue when their stems are bent contrary to their normal orientation. The compression wood formation helps restore the stem to its upright position, which contributes to the plants survival, but it has an adverse effect on wood quality and texture.

There were two main objectives of this study. The first one was to determine whether compression wood formation occurs in microgravity, and to establish the time-course of its [potential] induction at both 1g and in microgravity, respectively. The second objective was to develop methodology to determine the rate-limiting step(s) involved in formation of the monomeric lignin precursors from phenylalanine, including how the corresponding monolignols are transported to the plasma membrane and how the various metabolites and enzymes are organized at the subcellular level.

APPROACH:

This experiment flew on the STS-78 mission on the Space Shuttle Columbia, which was launched on June 22, 1996 for a 17-day mission. Conifer seedlings, Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) and Loblolly pine (Pinus taeda), were placed in the Plant Growth Unit (PGU) containing six Plant Growth Chambers (PGCs) in an orientation that favors compression wood formation in Earths gravity. Four plants were grown in each Plant Growth Chambers (PGC). A total of six PGCs were flown.

After two days on orbit, the crew opened the PGCs and bent the plants into a 45 degree orientation. The crew also performed a daily status check of PGU systems, and photographed the PGCs. During the flight, two of the chambers were opened for plant fixation, at which time the plants were harvested, sectioned, chemically fixed with 2.5% gluteraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde for postflight analysis. This harvesting occurred on days 10 and 13 of the mission. The remaining four chambers were harvested, sectioned and fixed with 2.5% gluteraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde upon recovery. Ground controls were conducted on a 24-hour delay in the Orbiter Environmental Simulator at environmental conditions (temperature, % relative humidity, and CO2) similar to those experienced by the flight samples.

After samples were returned to the investigators lab, electron and light microscopic study of the samples defined the time and place of compression wood formation and the extent to which it forms. Chemical and biochemical analysis complemented the study, enabling the investigator to measure the effects of microgravity and compression wood formation and define the regulatory enzymes and genes involved.

RESULTS:

This space flight experiment, while answering the question of whether compression wood formation occurred in microgravity, was not conducted without difficulty. Although the bending experiments were initiated in space, the Space Shuttle cabin temperatures were higher than any recorded previously (more than 29 degrees C). This condition had a deleterious effect on the growth of several specimens. The experiments had to be repeated several times postflight (under simulated flight conditions in the Orbiter Environmental Simulator) to confirm that the difficulties experienced were, in fact, due to high temperature.

From the specimens surviving in good condition, both sets of plants (i.e., microgravity and 1g) were essentially identical, all forming compression wood when orientated in a 45 degree orientation, and forming no compression wood when placed in a vertical orientation.

It was expected that compression wood would not be formed if the gravitational vector was removed as in microgravity. Thus, to account for its formation in space, either the small amount of gravity in microgravity is still large enough to ensure that the organisms can still respond to it, or, more likely, the effect of mechanical loading by harnessing overrides the gravitactic responses (i.e., due to overlapping signal transduction, perception and response mechanisms). Therefore, even in microgravity, the plants make appropriate corrections to alleviate the stress gradient introduced by bending, thereby forming compression wood.

It is now established that woody gymnosperm plants, such as Douglas fir, when placed in off-vertical configuration in microgravity, relative to the light source, respond by forming compression wood. This is proposed to be due to the influence of an internal stress gradient within the plant. It now needs to be established if woody angiosperms form comparable compression wood under these conditions. This finding would add to the steady growing body of knowledge associated with general phenylpropanoid metabolism and cell wall development. It also needs to be proven whether compression wood would occur if plants were able to realign their stems without the influence of harnessing, e.g., by phototropic responses over longer time periods (months, years.) This will be an important experiment to conduct on the International Space Station.
