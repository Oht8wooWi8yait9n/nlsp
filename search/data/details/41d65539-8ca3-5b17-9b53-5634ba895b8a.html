Protocol/Hardware Test of Spacelab-3 Autogenic Feedback Test

Record ID: DSO 442
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
As astronauts move about in space, they make twisting and turning movements. The extent and duration of movements depends on the size of the area they inhabit. It is important for crewmembers to wear garments and hardware that allow them to move freely in zero gravity so they can perform mission related activities. The purpose of this experiment was to evaluate the Autogenic Feedback Test (AFT) hardware and Shuttle garments for crewmembers in various locations in the Space Shuttle.

APPROACH:
Participants in this study donned the Autogenic Feedback Test (AFT) hardware and Shuttle garments while the flight crew received their weather briefing. A maximum of 45 minutes to one hour was needed for four crewmembers as only two Ground Support Equipment units were available for "check-out.”

Crewmembers participating in this study used a Urine Collection Device (UCD) modification for the AFT garments used during launch. The material of the flap may "bunch-up" in the crewmember's back causing discomfort while seated in the launch chair. Further, the snaps on this flap prevented the garment from "riding-up" while the crewmember sat in launch configuration. Lastly, this modification saved time post-insertion in that the crewmember did not have to snap the flap into place while in zero-g (again, to prevent the garment from riding up).

The feedback display was carried to the orbiter by close-out crew and handed to the crewmembers after seat ingress. The close-out crew turned the power switch of the Autogenic Feedback System (AFS) on before exiting the orbiter. It was recommended that the crewmembers follow the AFS activation cue card (mounted before each seat). However, after the unit is in "check-out mode" only, one additional button push is required to start the tape recorder. It was strongly recommended that this final button push not occur until the L-l0 minute hold. Because each tape operates for seven hours, if all crewmembers activated the recorder at the same time (i.e., L-l0 minutes), it was possible to accurately time-line the cassette change on the first mission day.

After the final button press, the crewmembers removed the display from their wrists and placed them in Flight Data Flight Book (FDFB) pouches mounted on the sides of each launch chair. This location was chosen for its convenience and because the high vibration environment of launch might make the display fall off the wrist mounts.

Feedback Display
It was recommended that upon orbital insertion all crewmembers participating in this study remove the feedback display from the FDFB pouch on their launch chairs. Treatment subjects re-attached the display to their cable harnesses at this time. Control subjects placed the display in a pocket. It became apparent that crewmembers should keep this display with them at all times while on shift. The display must be available for treatment subjects (i.e., feedback) and is used by all crewmembers to input "events" to the recorder, (i.e., indicating the onset of symptom episodes if and when they occur). This display may also be kept in the AFS pouch pocket used for holding the belt.

Middeck Procedures
After consultation among the investigators, it was determined to be imperative to obtain continuous in-flight physiological monitoring. The first mission day was particularly important in this regard. Therefore, the requirement for reconnecting the AFS to the crewmember's cable harness within one hour post-insertion was maintained. The crewmember indicated that tugging the under seat pouches (containing the AFS) may produce unnecessary head movements. The crewmember recommended that this may be avoided by unbolting the launch chairs from the deck, leaving them in the air (or velcroing them to the wall), and then removing the pouches. This procedure would also save time in reconfiguration activities performed post insertion. Further, if this procedure were done one chair at a time with crewmembers assisting one another, it could reduce the time required.

Aft flight Deck Procedures
This procedure must be considerably modified to accommodate Mission Specialist 1 (MS-1), located on the flight deck. Because this crewmember is responsible for payload activation and must perform many of his tasks in the confined area of the flight deck, it may not be possible for him to attach his AFS unit for up to five hours post insertion. This would result in a significant loss of critical data to this experiment. Nominally, MS-1 remains in his launch seat until the completion of OMS-2 (approximately 42 minutes). Instead, it was recommended that he brace himself in the hatch to the right of his seat such that his head is level with the underseat pouches, remove the AFS from the pouch, and connect it to his cable harness during this time. Because of his location, this activity should not disturb other critical flight tasks underway. Further, the recommended changes to the AFS pouch/belt assembly should facilitate his attachment of the AFS and enable his unimpeded performance of mission-related activities in the flight deck.

RESULTS:
Modification to AFS Pouch/Belt Assembly
There was no Spacelab flown on this mission and the subject crewmember was required to make numerous translations from the middeck to the flight deck. Because the hatch connecting the two decks was significantly narrower than the "tunnel" leading to Spacelab, the AFS unit tended to "hang-up" on obstructions. This necessitated his making twisting and turning motions as well as unnecessary head movements. After discussion of this matter with the crewmember postflight, it was agreed that this problem could be avoided by making the AFS pouch detachable from the belt while remaining connected to the cable harness. In this way, the AFS could be held above the crewmember's head (or between his legs) during translation. The crewmember recommended that the shoulder strap be used in-flight because it stabilizes the AFS unit. The size of the AFS unit did not impede his movement or performance of mission related activities while he was in a relatively open volume of space (middeck and flight deck), as he was able to slide the unit (on the belt) to his back, side, or front, if needed. It was also recommended that a square of Velcro be placed on the AFS unit itself to facilitate battery change-out procedures.

Use of Diagnostic Log Book or Voice Cassette Recorder
It was essential to the science of this investigation that crewmembers provide a subjective report of their malaise levels during time-lined and symptom-contingent episodes in flight. The crewmember used a voice microcassette recorder to perform these tasks. He did use the diagnostic book as a "cue card" so that his reports used the standardized terminology of the diagnostic scale. It is recommended that the use of a written log or taped report be made a crew option. It was observed that one crewmember (during an early SL-3 simulation), attached his voice recorder with velcro to his flight suit (left side of chest) so that it would be convenient for verbal reports.

Use of Foot Restraints
Investigators discovered that post-sleep donning required 45 minutes. Further questioning revealed that this was largely due to a difficulty with the BSR cable snap lead and the failure to use foot restraints during donning. Although this crewmember (and two of the PSs from SL-3) participated in a class on the KC-135 in which the investigators documented the fact that use of foot restraints shortened donning time from 45 to 15 minutes, this procedure was not performed in flight. Further, the use of restraints reduces unnecessary head movements which might be made while "floating as you dress."

Modificaton of Bio Signal Recorder (BSR) Electrode Snap Leads
The snaps on the disposable BSR electrodes worn on the crewmember's wrist tended to tear off the electrodes and remain mated to the cable harness. This tended to slow down donning procedures as the crewmember had to find an implement (i.e., screw driver) to remove the snaps. For SL-3, the engineers will replace these BSR snap leads on the harness so that this does not occur.

Pre-Stringing the Cable Harness
The crewmember discovered that donning time could be reduced by attaching the cable harness to the AFT garment before putting it on. Appropriate changes to the FDFB will be made.

Proposed Modification to AFS
On the second mission day, the crewmember noted that the AFT garment felt uncomfortably tight, particularly in the abdominal region. He emphasized in his debriefing that he did not know if this was unique to himself or if others were likely to also feel discomfort caused by the garment. A possible solution to prevent the recurrence of the problem on SL-3 is to modify the AFT garment by inserting a second zipper on the right side (alternatively, the existing zipper could be modified to open from the bottom, rather than the top). This will loosen the fabric around the abdominal region and will also result in the loss of the abdominal respiration signal.

De-Orbit Prep Procedures
It was strongly recommended that a deorbit prep checklist be written and followed carefully. The crewmember took great care in his preparations for reentry. This essentially amounted to performing post-insertion procedures in reverse (i.e., mounting the AFS under the middeck seat, and performing AFS activation). However, it was discovered that although the AFS worked as is should, no reentry tape was made. It is believed that the crewmember inadvertently failed to make the final button press (which starts the tape running) before he removed his wrist display. In summary, the results of this mission were extremely valuable to the SL-3 experiment.
