Frames of Reference for Sensorimotor Transformations

Record ID: 9401701
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This experiment addresses the fundamental question of reference frames used by the central nervous system (CNS) to interpret sensory information and to control movements. By studying the perception and coordination of visual and haptic stimuli in space, researchers can better understand the role of gravity in interpreting and calibrating these two sensory modalities on earth.

Gravity plays a potential role in calibrating different essential reference frames. The CNS can sense the gravitational force, and thus define a vertical direction, in a variety of ways. Mechanisms in the inner ear permit the measurement of gravity in a head-centered reference frame. The force of gravity acting on an outstretched limb permits the perception of vertical in an arm-centered coordinate frame, while visual cues in the environment (walls, falling objects) can be used to define vertical in retinotopic coordinates. The constant direction of gravity can potentially be used to align these different reference frames. This experiment is composed of a series of psychophysical tests specifically addressing the role of gravity in defining, calibrating, and aligning different sensory and motor reference frames.

A. Hypotheses

  1. Gravity provides a potential reference direction for the interpretation of sensory information. In microgravity, we might expect to see a shift from allocentric interpretations of visual and kinesthetic information to an egocentric reference frame: head/retinal for visual data and body/joint for motor and kinesthetic information.
  2. Gravity serves to align visual, kinesthetic, and haptic reference frames. In microgravity we might expect to see a reduction in the capacity to coordinate visual and haptic information.
  3. Interpretation of kinesthetic sensation is based on internal models of the limb and of the environment, including a model of the constant force of gravity acting on the body. We can expect that initially in orbit, the discrepancy between the internal models appropriate for a 1-G environment and the actual 0-G conditions will introduce specific biases in the perception of proprioceptive and haptic stimuli. We might further find that these biases disappear as the internal models are adapted to novel conditions.

B. Specific Objectives

  1. Test the hypothesis that gravity can be used to align visual and motor frames of reference.
  2. Identify the role of gravity in defining the reference frames used to code visual, proprioceptive, and haptic information. By extension, measure the adaptation of the perceptual system to an environment lacking gravity.
  3. Collect fundamental baseline data on the capacities of the human visual, proprioceptive, and haptic perceptual systems under conditions of microgravity.

C. Functional Objectives
This experiment consists of a series of psychophysical tests designed to measure the subject's ability to measure and align visual and haptic stimuli.

  1. Visual Orientation – Test the accuracy of alignment of two visual stimuli (lines).
  2. Haptic Orientation – Test the ability to discriminate between two directions of hand displacements in the fronto-parallel plane.
  3. Haptic/Visual Coordination – Test the ability to align a visual line with the orientation of the haptically presented axis.
  4. Visual Magnitude – Test for perceptual differences between the lengths of visually presented horizontal and vertical lines.
  5. Haptic Magnitude – Test for perceptual differences between the length and width of a haptically presented rectangular "hole".

APPROACH:
The subject was seated in a body restraint chair, and secured with belts around the shoulders and hips. The feet were placed in foot restraints installed in front of the chair. The subject observed a flat video screen through a 60 cm long, cylindrical tunnel. The face was placed inside a mask at one end of the tunnel, and secured lightly with a Velcro strap that passed behind the head. The subjects manipulated the ROBOTOP joystick mounted below and to the right (right-handed subject) or left (left-handed subject) of the video monitor. The subject was required to adjust the length or the orientation of a ‘variable’ stimulus to match the length or orientation of a fixed ‘reference’ stimulus. Preflight, inflight, and postflight psychophysical test sessions were identical.

Subjects were presented with two stimuli (such as lines on the screen) sequentially. One of these stimuli, the reference, was fixed for the duration of a single trial. The second, the variable stimulus, was under the control of the subject via the ROBOTOP joystick and a control knob. The task for the subject was to adjust the variable stimulus such that it matched the reference. The characteristic of the reference stimuli was randomly varied, as was the starting value for the variable parameter. Different starting points were used for trials with the same reference value. Using this technique, two effects were measured. Differences between the reference stimulus and the average final value of the variable stimulus, for a given reference, indicated a bias in the sensing of the desired parameter and/or a bias in the mapping from one direction/modality to another. The standard deviation of values for a given reference will indicate the accuracy with which the subject can control and reproduce the desired output.

To effectively measure the biases in perception, it was important that the subject not know about eventual errors that he or she might make. Feedback about errors would give an instant clue as to how the response should be corrected. However, it was very difficult to remain focused on the experiment task if there was no feedback about performance. To this end a "score" was devised for the subjects. This score represented the variability of responses to a given reference stimulus. If the subject was alert and performing well, he or she would produce approximately the same response for a given reference stimulus, and the score would be small. If, on the other hand, the subject was distracted or not concentrating well, it was expected that responses to a given stimulus would be more variable, thus producing a larger score. It was, therefore, desirable to have as low a score as possible. This score measured precision and not accuracy. The score was intended purely for the use of each individual subject in order to provide feedback about the work that they were performing. These scores were not reported to anyone, nor were they used in any way to rank subjects.

RESULTS:
Data was collected on flight days 25, 27, 28, 53, and 54, but the sessions scheduled for flight day 120 and 121 was canceled due to equipment failure. The experiment was not performed on NASA 5. Data from the NASA 4 study have been drawn from a single subject, so care must be taken in interpreting these results. Nevertheless, the following observations can be made from the performance of these tasks by this subject. Some of the results have been confirmed by the Russian experiment "Orientation", which followed the same protocol as this experiment and was performed on two subjects from the Mir 23 crew.

Visual Orientation
The oblique effect for the comparison of visual orientation is apparent in the plots of response variability. The variability of responses is lower for vertical (90°) and horizontal (0°) stimuli, compared to the other orientations. The data suggest that variability may increase more in microgravity for the oblique angles than for the canonical horizontal and vertical references. This observation is confirmed in the average of the NASA 4 and Mir 23 subjects, particularly around the horizontal direction. Note that an anomaly in the onboard hardware created a vertical reference line on the video monitor that may have aided the subjects in aligning the vertical stimuli. This may explain why variability at +115.5 degrees was not increased in microgravity. If confirmed, these results suggest that in the absence of the gravitational reference frame, visual perception of orientation is perturbed, with a greater bias towards a body-aligned reference frame for ‘vertical’ and ‘horizontal’ lines.

Haptic Orientation
Responses are somewhat more variable for the haptic perception of orientation. There is no clear evidence for an oblique effect in this test of the human haptic system, nor is there an obvious effect of microgravity on either the biases or the variability of responses. This latter observation is confirmed in the average across the 3 subjects which shows no clear preference for 0° or 90° stimuli nor an obvious effect of microgravity on the average variability.

Haptic-Visual Coordination
Subjects are understandably less precise in matching the orientation of a visual stimulus to the direction of a haptically-perceived reference, due to the sensorimotor transformations involved. Both bias and variable error are significantly greater for the haptic-visual coordination task. A strong oblique effect can be observed in the measures of variable error, with significantly less variability for horizontal and vertical stimuli. These observations are confirmed in the average across the 3 subjects. Note that the effect is much stronger in the haptic-visual task than would be expected from the results of either the visual or haptic perception tasks alone. Thus, it is in the transformation of orientation from visual to haptic space that the horizontal and vertical directions become particularly salient. The lack of a systematic change in the patterns of responses due to weightlessness suggests that it is a body-centered coordinate system that mediates transformations between the two sensory modalities.

Visual Magnitude
When adjusting the length of visually presented lines, the subject consistently produced a vertical response length that was shorter than the horizontal reference. These responses are consistent with the well-known visual illusion for horizontal and vertical lines. The comparison of 0-G versus 1-G data does not show systematic increases in either the magnitude of the illusion or in the variability of responses. These results support the hypothesis that the visual illusion arises from differences in retinal receptive fields as opposed to differences in a gravitationally-linked reference frame.

Haptic Magnitude
The NASA4 subject showed similar differences for haptic perception of lengths as is normally seen for visually perceived lines. When asked to produce a square hole, the subject systematically responded with a rectangle that was shorter than it was wide. Furthermore, the magnitude of the illusion increased in microgravity, with no consistent change in variability. Thus, haptic perception of form appears to be affected by the gravitational environment or background levels of force applied to the limb.

This interesting observation is confirmed by the results of the Mir 23 study. Subjects do not consistently produce short or tall rectangles in the place of squares for stimuli presented in this posture and workspace region. Nevertheless, all subjects produced rectangles that were shorter in microgravity than on the ground. Furthermore, one can observe a ‘rebound’ effect in the post-flight data. Early post-flight responses are even taller than those produced pre-flight, while in 2 out of 3 subjects, later post-flight tests produce results closer to pre-flight values. The time-course of these effects tends to indicate a gradual adaptation of haptic perception that is influenced by changes in the gravitational environment.

Discussion
From the haptic magnitude experiment, it appears that the action of gravity on the arm influences the perception of hand displacements in a way that is different for horizontal vs. vertical movements. In related experiments, the researchers have observed that gravity affects both the haptic perception of applied forces and the control of arm movements. Results from these three experiments suggest that haptic perception involves mechanisms that include information about the intended action, as opposed to a passive interpretation of sensory information. The human nervous system requires knowledge about the effects of gravity on the sensory and motor apparatus to correctly interpret and coordinate sensorimotor information.

Gravity plays a role in the interpretation of visual stimuli and in the perception of limb position. It was therefore hypothesized that gravity might play a role in aligning visual and proprioceptive or haptic reference frames. Despite the observed effects of gravity on haptic perception, one can see that a gravitational reference is not essential to the alignment of visual and haptic reference frames. The oblique effect seen for the transformation of information between these two modalities persists in microgravity. Thus, the reference frame used to coordinate these two modalities appears to be linked to the axis of the trunk or head.

The coordination of visual and haptic sensory modalities might nevertheless be aided by the presence of gravity's pull. In these initial experiments, the body axis was fixed with respect to the head and with respect to the floor of the Mir station. Cognitive information about the subject's orientation with respect to these reference frames may allow the subject to substitute the body axis for a gravitational reference frame when the gravitational reference is removed. However, much of the work to be performed in 0-G is not carried out with the body fixed in such a rigid fashion, most notably during extra-vehicular activity (EVA). This experiment was designed to test the hypothesis that gravity is the unique or dominant reference frame for visuo-haptic coordination, and the results tend to reject this hypothesis. It is well known, nevertheless, that the nervous system can adapt and substitute alternate reference frames when adequate sensory cues are not available. Indeed, the cognitive perception of ‘vertical’ combines sensory information from visual, vestibular, and haptic cues, and the effects of gravity on this perception are most easily observed when these cues are no longer aligned. Additional experiments involving head and whole-body tilt are planned, both on the ground and in microgravity, to distinguish between the different possible body reference frames for visuo-haptic coordination and to see if gravity influences the interpretation of visual and/or haptic sensation when the egocentric and allocentric reference frames are no longer aligned.

These data, interpreted in the context of current theories on sensorimotor integration, should be useful for understanding the basic operating principles of the human nervous system. Such understanding is crucial for developing diagnostic tools and eventual therapies for those afflicted with deficits for sensorimotor function. Information about difficulties in sensorimotor coordination imposed by the microgravity environment may also aid in the design of more effective tools and interfaces for the astronaut community.
