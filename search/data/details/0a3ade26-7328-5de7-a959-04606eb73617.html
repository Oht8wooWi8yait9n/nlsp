Influence of Sensory Integration on the Neural Processing of Gravito-Inertial Cues

Record ID: NNJ04HB01G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Our sense of spatial orientation results from a complex set of neural processes of sensory integration; these processes utilize information from many different physiological systems. Evidence suggests that extended exposure to microgravity yields adaptive changes in these neural processes. For the most part, these adaptive changes appear functionally relevant for space flight but yield significant transient problems when astronauts return to gravitational environments (Earth, Mars, Moon, etc.). In-flight countermeasures that reinforce the normal sensory interactions encountered in a gravitational environment (e.g., rotations about any axis not aligned with gravity are accompanied by changes in the relative orientation of gravity) will almost certainly help astronauts function adequately when they transition to a gravitational environment. This will be important to pilot astronauts landing spacecraft as well as to crewmembers trying to rapidly exit a spacecraft. Furthermore, artificial gravity has been proposed as a countermeasure for numerous problems associated with space flight (including cardiovascular deconditioning, bone decalcification, etc.). One undesired side effect of artificial gravity will be additional alterations in these processes of sensory integration. For all of these reasons, it is essential that we understand the predominant sensory interactions that are altered by exposure to microgravity. Specifically, investigators proposed to investigate how rotational signals from the semicircular canals influence how humans process the ambiguous gravito-inertial force measured by the otolith 0rgans. Investigators performed a series of ground experiments that sought a better understanding of sensory interactions between the semicircular canals and graviceptors. They proposed to perform these investigations using several different measurement techniques including measures of reflexive eye responses as well as psychophysical measures of human perception.

This project was originally a flight experiment that was scaled back to be a ground study shortly after the space shuttle accident. After this change in plans, our project goals were altered. Our new goal was to use psychophysics and eye movement measurements to investigate how humans utilize semicircular canal cues to help estimate the relative orientation of gravity here on Earth. Investigators proposed a ground study to measure human responses (perceptual and eye movement) during combined canal-otolith stimulation on Earth. Their goal was to develop new tests that will allow them to investigate how canalotolith interactions adapt to micro-gravity exposure.

APPROACH:
Subjects were tested using protocols in which the subjects a) tilted, b) translated, or c) tilted and translated simultaneously. Investigators expected the human responses to be consistent with those previously measured in monkeys, with canal cues essential for accurate estimation of gravity and linear acceleration.

RESULTS:
1. Investigators developed a new hypothesis designed to explain space flight adaptation, the disorientation experienced in microgravity, and the motion sickness that sometimes occurs.
2. The team successfully developed a new testing paradigm that allowed them to investigate human tilt and translation responses. The new testing paradigm is ready to be used as part of a preflight/postflight investigation to study how astronauts adapt to the micro-gravity environment.
3. The team successfully completed an extensive set of human studies in which they found that the mechanism used to evoke human translational VOR responses is different than the mechanism used to evoke monkey translational VOR responses.
4. These same studies showed that human perception and human action (i.e., the VOR) employ qualitatively different neural mechanisms, which emphasizes the importance of studying perception to complement our existing extensive understanding of the VOR.
