Assessment of Pharmaceutical Stability in Space Using Ground-based Simulation of Spacecraft Environmental Factors

Record ID: BRC04-JSC01
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Therapeutic efficacy and safety of pharmaceuticals flown on the space shuttle and International Space Station (ISS) remain critical issues for successful NASA medical operations. Stability of drugs on the ground is known to be dependent on a number of environmental factors including but not limited to exposure to heat, light, and humidity, as well as other storage conditions. Some medications used in space flight have been used in doses far higher than those used on the ground or have produced unusual side effects, both of which can be explained by alterations in drug stability. Drugs are stored in manners that are not conventional on the ground, and outside the normal stability-rated storage conditions manufacturers report to the FDA. In addition, objects in space are exposed to background cosmic radiation and to radiation from Solar Particle Events (SEPs) both of which contain ionizing radiation or heavy ions. The radiation dose inside spacecraft, however, is higher than without due to production of daughter ions and this increase can cause a reduction in the stability of drugs.

Preliminary studies indicated significant differences in potency of some pharmaceutical preparations flown on space flights compared to ground-based controls. It is hypothesized that space flight environment, especially radiation, affects stability of pharmaceuticals contained in the space shuttle and ISS medical kits. The objectives of this investigation are to 1) determine chemical and physical stability parameters of select pharmaceuticals contained in the shuttle and ISS medical kits along with matching ground-based controls; 2) characterize degradation profiles of the unstable formulations after exposure to particulate radiation using synchrotron light and radiation sources; and 3) develop mathematical models that can predict shelf life of pharmaceuticals during space flight.

APPROACH:
Select dosage forms from the medical kits returned to Earth after a space flight were analyzed and compared to matching formulations stored under controlled conditions on Earth. Disintegration, dissolution rates and concentrations of active drug and degradation products of these dosage forms were determined. Effects of high-energy radioactive particles on the stability of these pharmaceuticals were examined by exposing the formulations to synchrotron light and radiation sources at the National Space Radiation Laboratory (NSRL). Predictive degradation models were constructed from these data to estimate the degree and magnitude of loss of pharmaceutical activity during space flights.

This experiment was divided into two separate but related parts, a flight payload experiment and a ground experiment conducted at the NSRL, both of which were initiated in 2006. In the flight portion of the experiment a payload of several identically packed medication kits containing 35 dosage forms comprised of 29 prescription or over the counter medications was launched onboard STS-121 on July 4, 2006, and transported to the ISS. Over a planned period of 18 months, individual packed medication kits were returned from the ISS and medications analyzed for total drug content and dissolution rate. On the ground, drugs are regulated by the FDA to deliver within five percent of the labelled claim of the dose for a predetermined time period which is not to exceed two years.

RESULTS:
The returned kits revealed stability decrements for several drugs. Several medications were found to have less than 95 percent of their label claim when flown on STS-121: clavulanate (in Augmentin), levothyroxin, and risedronate. Medications flown onboard STS-121 were also compared to identically packed kits which were left in controlled storage on the ground at Kennedy Space Center. Medications which showed a greater than five percent difference in active drug content compared to KSC controls were furosemide and cilastatin (in Primaxin). Similarly, in the ground-based study, researchers found decrements in overall drug content when exposed to iron (Fe) and proton (p+) beams ranging from 0.1 to 50 Gray, doses which although higher than terminal doses to humans, are suitable for accelerated stability testing as defined by the FDA. Some drugs failed to meet the expected 95% of dose in this scenario: acetaminophen, cavulanate (in Augmentin), ciprofloxacin (in ophthalmic solution), mupirocin, phenazopyridine, and promethazine. Compared to controls, two drugs had a greater than five percent reduction in content, clavulanate (in Augmentin) and phenazopyridine.

In addition to drug content delivered, other factors such as dissolution rate can be affected by environmental factors. Results from exposure to space flight on STS-121 indicates dissolution rates for several drugs were different from the rates of ground controls; the drugs with reduced release rates after exposure to space flight were azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, levothyroxin, folate (in a multivitamin), and niacin (in a multivitamin). Several drugs exposed to radiation exposures at NSRL also had reduced dissolution rates: amoxicillin and clavulanate, trimethoprim & sulfmethoxazole, ciprofloxacin tablets, and ibuprofen. Three drugs had reduced release rates when exposed to Fe beams but not p+ beams: levothyroxin, mupirocin, and riboflavin.

Analysis of drug content and dissolution rates in future payload returns, and in further exposures at NSRL, is required to ascertain the true effect of space flight on drugs.
