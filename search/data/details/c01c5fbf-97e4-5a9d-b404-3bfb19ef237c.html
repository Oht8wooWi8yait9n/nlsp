Influence of Weightlessness on Baroreflex Function

Record ID: DSO 467
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to weightlessness alters postflight cardiovascular responses to exercise and standing. Although these changes are probably initiated during flight, they are not known to impair cardiovascular performance in space. However, functional problems become apparent upon landing, when most astronauts have reduced exercise capacity and orthostatic intolerance. Loss of plasma volume during weightlessness may be partially responsible for orthostatic intolerance.

Orthostatic intolerance is a predictable, but a poorly understood consequence of space travel. Because arterial baroreceptors modulate abrupt pressure transients, including those provoked by standing, the hypothesis that space flight impairs baroreflex mechanisms was tested.

APPROACH:
Vagally-medicated carotid baroreceptor-cardiac reflex responses (provoked by neck pressure changes) in the supine position and heart rate and blood pressure in the supine and standing positions were studied in 16 U.S. astronauts (15 male and one female) before and after four to five day Space Shuttle missions. Preflight studies were performed nominally 10 and five days before launch; in practice, preflight data were collected between 13 and seven days, and again between seven and five days before launch. Postflight data were collected on landing (R+0), on R+3, and again on R+8.

The electrocardiogram, respiration (abdominal bellows connected to a strain-gauge pressure transducer), and neck chamber pressure were recorded by FM tape and strip chart recorders. The electrocardiogram and respiration were recorded during the last five minutes of a 20-minute rest period prior to any intervention. Resting vagal-cardiac activity was estimated from average R-R intervals and their standard deviations. Blood pressure was measured manually with a sphygmomanometer before barorector testing.

RESULTS:
On landing day, astronauts’ resting R-R intervals and standard deviations, and the slope, range, and position of their resting R-R intervals on the carotid transmural pressure-sinus mode response relation (operational point) were reduced relative to preflight values. Reductions of operational points (indicating reduced hypotensive buffering capacity) were related directly and significantly to reductions of postflight systolic pressure responses to standing. After landing day, baroreflex slopes and ranges were subnormal and remained so for the duration of the study (eight to 10 days). These results suggest that short-duration space flight leads to significant, functionally relevant impairment of human baroreflex mechanisms.
