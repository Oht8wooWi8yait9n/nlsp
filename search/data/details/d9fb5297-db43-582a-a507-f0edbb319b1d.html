Bioluminescent Monitoring of Opportunistic Pathogens in the Spacecraft Environment

Record ID: NAG91424
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The specific research objective of this proposal was to insert a quorum sensing gene (luxI) into bacteriophage to intrinsically generate autoinducer molecules upon target cell infection and subsequent amplification of lux-based bioluminescent signaling mechanisms. Using quorum sensing molecules as a means of detection allows for sensing of biological agents at very low level concentrations. As opposed to chemical detection systems, where typical toxic thresholds of part-per-billion to part-per-million still equate to >1014 molecules available for detection, a quorum sensing biological detection system may be capable of sensing down to one individual biological agent. This technology provides a unique mechanism to provide a measurable amount of bioluminescence from these low event numbers by using the production of autoinducer molecules to produce amplification of individual targeted biological agents, thus avoiding dependence upon target cell growth. The sensors are capable of functioning as a network of autonomous detectors for continuous on-line, near real-time monitoring in a low-power, low-mass, low-maintenance, redundant framework.

APPROACH:
To assist in the detection of E. coli, a recombinant bacteriophage reporter was developed that uses quorum sensing (luxI/luxR) signaling and luxCDABE-based bioluminescent bioreporter sensing to specifically and autonomously respond to serotype E. coli. The bacteriophage reporter, derived from phage PP01, was tested in artificially contaminated foodstuffs including apple juice, tap water, ground beef, and spinach leaves.

RESULTS:
Testing of these reporter phage systems indicates detection limits approaching 10e4 CFU/ml without sample preincubation and down to 10 CFU/ml with sample preincubation for E. coli O157:H7 within a 96-well plate assay format. Testing in artificially contaminated apple juice demonstrated detection limits down to 10e4 CFU/ml in less than 8 hours.

For general E. coli detection using strain XL1-Blue as a target, identification in pure culture occurred within 1.5 – 5 hours at high cell densities (10e8 – 10e5 CFU/ml) and at lower cell densities (<10 CFU/ml) with requisite preincubation within a total assay time of approximately 10 hours. When tested against artificially contaminated lettuce, the assay detected original inoculums between 10e8 – 10e5 CFU/ml within 2.6 – 12 hours, respectively.

While the overall assay limits fall short of the 1 – 10 CFU/ml detection limits typically desired in food matrices, their limits of detection are close to that of existing immunoassays and fluorescent-phage based assays, and the sensitivity of these assays may be increased through additional genetic modifications of the phage (i.e., linkage of luxI to multiple phage promoters) or through better sample concentration techniques (i.e., immunomagnetic separation). Since no ancillary reagents or manipulations are required other than incubation and light measurement, these assays remain highly amenable to automated high-throughput monitoring using any number of available photomultiplier-based luminescent readers, including miniaturization in a BBIC format. Phage-based sensing also ensures only the detection of live cells, thereby overcoming the false positive detection of dead cells often inherent in other assaying methods such as ELISA and PCR. Further manipulations of phage reporter genetic mechanisms and advances in miniaturized electronic optics will clearly impact future monitoring and detection strategies in the food industry.
