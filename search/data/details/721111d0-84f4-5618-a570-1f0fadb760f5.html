Influence of Sensory Integration on the Neural Processing of Gravito-Inertial Cues

Record ID: 99-E370
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Human spatial orientation is the result of complex neural processes, integrating sensory information from many different physiological systems. Results from previous space flights show that extended exposure to microgravity causes adaptive changes in these neural processes. These adaptive changes cause significant transient problems when astronauts return to a gravitational environment. Thus, it is important to understand the predominant sensory interactions that are altered by the exposure to microgravity.

The main focus of this investigation is to understand how the processing of sensory cues from the otolith organs of the inner ear is influenced by other sensory cues, particularly visual motion cues and cues from the semicircular canals. It is important to understand these sensory interactions because all natural movements involve combinations of sensory input form the otoliths and these sensory systems. It is hypothesized that the post-flight data will demonstrate a reduced ability to use rotational cues to estimate the relative orientation of gravity. These findings would confirm the influence of relatively uninvestigated mechanisms affecting the neurophysiologic adaptation to a microgravity environment during spaceflight and readaptation to a 1-G environment upon return to Earth.

The study is designed to provide a comprehensive assessment of the influence of rotational cues on the ability of humans to estimate gravity ("down") and linear acceleration. Crewmembers returning from long-and short duration space flight will be tested (1) for their ability to keep themselves in an upright position using stimuli in the roll and tilt axis, (2) their perceptions of tilt, and (3) eye movement responses induced by rapid roll tilts.

The inability to accurately estimate the direction of gravity causes disorientation and imbalance. This can lead to severe problems during and following landing, like piloting errors (e.g., pilot induced oscillations) that could lead to undesirable flight incidents and the inability to get away from the landing site during emergency egress after landing. This study will document the extent to which the inability to use rotational cues to estimate the direction of gravity contributes to post flight instability and disorientation. If the hypotheses are correct, the study will provide baseline data to allow us to begin to investigate and implement an appropriate in-flight countermeasure to alleviate this specific aspect of post flight disorientation and instability.

APPROACH:
As this investigation has been discontinued, the methodology for this experiment is not available at this time.

RESULTS:
