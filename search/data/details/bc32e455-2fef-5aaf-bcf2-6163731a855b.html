Gastrointestinal Absorption and Pharmacokinetics in Astronauts

Record ID: DSO 632
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Weightlessness during space flight induces a wide range of physiological and biochemical changes in crewmembers, such as disruption of gastrointestinal (GI) function and physiology, changes in liver function, alterations in organ blood flow, and hormonal and metabolic perturbations. Any of these changes can influence the pharmacokinetic behavior and pharmacodynamic consequences of medications administered to crewmembers during space flight. As mission durations increase, the effects of spaceflight-induced physiologic changes and the resulting pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic deviations will also increase. These effects must be countered in order to maintain optimum health and performance during flight, as well as to initiate re-adaptation to gravity upon return to Earth.

Comparison of results from the "Pharmacokinetics of Acetaminophen (DSO 458)" experiment (7 subjects - Tylenol tablets) and the "Gastrointestinal Function During Extended Duration Space Flight (DSO 622)" experiment (4 subjects - Tylenol syrup) indicated that absorption and bioavailability of oral liquid dosage may be better and more reliable than that of an equivalent solid dosage form. Medication uptake may also improve in a fed state during which a low fiber breakfast was ingested, than during a fasted state, lasting 8 to 10 hours, as a means of compensating for the expected GI decrements during space flight.

Additionally, unusually high breath levels of hydrogen (Shuttle data) and methane (Mir data) were observed, in addition to the reported regular use of countermeasure (lactobacillus) by the Russian program, which indicated changes in GI microbes. This warranted the need to examine the microbial physiology of the GI tract. Decreased GI motility--as indicated from the limited results from the Extended Duration Orbiter Medical Project and the Shuttle-Mir Science Program experiment "Gastrointestinal Function During Extended Duration Space Flight (DSO 622)"--will affect drug absorption and bioavailability of oral medications and thus compromise therapeutic effectiveness during space flights. This effect will be compounded by anticipated changes in the hepatic metabolism of drugs and other xenobiotics.

The purpose of this experiment was to determine changes in the GI function and physiology and examine the pharmacokinetics of orally administered pharmaceutical probe acetaminophen. Estimating clinically significant organ functional changes and resultant pharmacokinetic consequences will lead to the design and development of effective pharmacologic countermeasures and therapeutic adjustments.

APPROACH:
This investigation was comprised of three tests: lactulose and acetaminophen test, glucose breath hydrogen test, and 3-C Urea Helicobacter test. Each of these tests was to be conducted both before and after flight; no data collection was scheduled during flight. During pre- and postflight sessions, a specified breakfast was to be provided, followed by an oral ingestion of acetaminophen. Timed breath and saliva samples were collected, as were void-by-void urine samples. Food, fluid, exercise and medications were monitored during the urine and saliva collections. The rate of acetaminophen absorption and metabolism would be determined by analysis of the urine samples and timed saliva samples.

Lactulose and Acetaminophen Tests: Participants were asked to avoid eating foods that are likely to generate hydrogen (such as cabbage, beans, dairy products) for twenty-four hours before the test. After approximately an 8-hour fast (sleep period), a saliva sample, a urine sample, and two consecutive breath samples, 5 minutes apart, were collected from the subjects. The subjects then ate a low fiber breakfast, followed by ingestion of 650 mg of acetaminophen (650 mg). After acetaminophen ingestion, participants drank orange juice (or an agreed-upon fluid) containing 20 g of lactulose.

Subjects were to record intake of food, fluids, medications, and exercise during the 24-hour protocol period. They were not to eat or exercise for 4 hours following ingestion of medication; however, drinking water was allowed. After ingesting the acetaminophen and drinking the lactulose/orange juice mixture, the following samples were collected from the subjects:

1) 12 additional saliva samples
2) 16 additional breath samples (per the prescribe schedule)
3) Urine voids for 24 hours (as separate samples)

Glucose Breath Hydrogen Test: After an overnight fast, a breath sample was collected. Participants then consumed a dose of glucose dissolved in water. Breath samples were collected every 15 minutes for 120 minutes after dose consumption.

Urea Helicobacter Test: After overnight fasting, a baseline breath sample was obtained. Subjects then consumed a liquid test meal containing 13-C urea. Breath samples were collected every 10 minutes for 30 minutes post-ingestion.

A ground-based protocol of DSO 632 was also performed. Recent data collected on GI function (DSO 632) in astronauts indicates five out of the six subjects tested exhibited active colonization of H. pylori in their stomach. H. pylori is a bacterial pathogen suspected to promote incidence of gastric and duodenal ulcer disease. The objective of this addendum was to screen and establish population data for the incidence of H. pylori in the local Johnson Space Center (JSC) population in reference to the astronaut population. The investigators aimed to screen a total of 50 subjects from ground support personnel and from astronaut core (about 30 and 20 each, respectively). The investigators wanted to screen astronauts that have flown before and those who have not flown. Crewmembers from international partners were also encouraged to participate in the screening. Subjects could self administer at their convenience. This simple, safe and noninvasive breath test known as Breath Tek -UBiT, is a clinical test administered by health care providers to diagnose pathology associated with H. pylori incidence. The test was administered in the morning after an overnight fast. The total time required for the implementation of this protocol was 30 minutes for briefing before the test (at subjects' convenience) and approximately 20 minutes for the test itself. Test protocol includes collection of two breath samples, one before and another 15 minutes after consumption of a test beverage provided by the vendor. Samples were analyzed using vendor-provided instrumentation. A GI symptoms checklist was provided to subjects having results in the positive range to assess any incidence of pathology associated with H. pylori. A copy of the test results was provided to the subjects along with instructions for action if positive test results were observed.

RESULTS:
Results indicated significant changes in GI motility and absorption of APAP during both short and long-duration space flights; difference between early, mid and late flight days (n=2) was not significant suggesting that the GI functional changes manifest early in flight and remain consistent throughout the mission.

Results for the ground-based portion of this study aren't available.
