The Genetic Basis of the Loss of Musculo-Skeletal Tissue during Weightlessness: Towards the Identification of Individuals that are at Greatest Risk

Record ID: NAG91499
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
One of the main physiologic hurdles to human presence in space on long-duration missions is the loss of bone and muscle mass that accompanies microgravity. Although bone and muscle have been proven to be lost at considerable rates during extended stays in microgravity, there appears to be considerable variation in loss among individuals. Some astronauts lose large amounts of tissue, while others are nearly unaffected. This finding has also been observed in ground-based bed rest studies on humans, as well as ground-based research on mice. Individual variability in bone and muscle loss may be due to genetic variations that cause differences in the mechanical sensitivity of the muscles and skeleton.

The objective of this study was to better understand the genetic basis of the sensitivity of the muscle and skeleton to the loss of one-G mechanical stimuli by identifying the location of genetic traits responsible for the difference in sensitivity to loading. The results of this research may be used to help identify astronauts that have the greatest need for pharmacologic or biomechanical countermeasures, as well as to discover new drugs that work to curtail bone and muscle loss caused by disuse.

APPROACH:
A genetically heterogeneous mouse population was created by double crossing two inbred strains of mice with very distinct skeletal responses to the loss of functional weightbearing. More than 400 of these mice were subjected to hindlimb unloading and reambulation. Longitudinal changes in the morphology and mechanical properties of these mice were determined by in vivo microCT and finite element modeling.

RESULTS:
Similar to data from astronauts and bed rest patients, changes in bone morphology during disuse and re-ambulation were associated with large variations in this genetically heterogeneous mouse population. The loss of bone quantity and quality during disuse was particularly dramatic in trabecular bone. Even though most mice were able to recover at least some of the tissue mass that was lost during disuse, this recovery occurred by re-thickening of individual trabeculae while the connectedness of the structure further deteriorated. The amount of tissue recovery was neither associated with any baseline bone morphology parameter nor with the rates of tissue loss induced by disuse. These data indicate the strong influence of genetic variations on the changes in bone morphology and strength induced by rapid changes in its mechanical milieu, emphasize that is critical to preserve bone’s micro-architecture before individual trabecular connections are lost, and suggest that the genes modulating changes in morphology during disuse are, at least in part, distinct from those regulating tissue recovery. The weak correlations between bone and muscle loss and recovery further suggest that there is little overlap between the mechanosensory elements of muscle and bone.
