Automated Microfluidic Devices for Monitoring Biological Systems in Space

Record ID: NNJ05HB38G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to the unique environmental conditions found in space (i.e., low gravity, potentially toxic chemicals in enclosed spaces, and ionizing radiation) raises the prospects for significant biological consequences arising from radiation and chemical-induced damage at the genetic level compounded by the potentially synergistic effects of microgravity. To better understand these effects, a collaborative effort between the Ramsey group at The University of North Carolina and NASA's Johnson Space Center has been established to develop robust biosensers for space applications. These efforts seek to integrate the lab-on-a-chip technology developed at ORNL with space bioreactors developed at NASA.

The objective of this study was to develop an enabling biosensor technology that can be effectively utilized for 1) the routine monitoring and detection of a wide variety of molecular compounds and 2) the identification of characteristic chemical signatures produced by living organisms as a result of specific environmental stresses. This technology will integrate the lab-on-a-chip devices (microchips) with space bioreactors. The first steps in realizing this goal will be 1) to develop a prototype microfluidic device capable of operating in reduced gravity and 2) to develop assays to detect changes/mutations in genes in response to environmental stressors.

APPROACH:
The lab-on-a-chip technology was used both to monitor the performance of the bioreactor and to detect and quantitate DNA changes in cells cultured in the bioreactor. By coupling the reporter organisms to an analytical measuring instrument, specific biomolecules can be detected and monitored as an indicator of either bioreactor performance on the International Space Station (ISS) or the effect of environmental insults at planetary surfaces. This biosensor technology will be developed and tested for low-gravity operation and will be adaptable to a wide range of cell types. The entire system will be automated so that no human intervention will be required while on orbit. In addition, because of the compact nature of the lab-on-a-chip devices they can be integrated with the present bioreactor systems with minimal increases in space, power, and mass allotment.

RESULTS:
This study focused on developing a microfluidic-based system capable of sampling cell culture effluent under microgravity conditions. Over the period of the study investigators successfully designed, built, and tested a portable microfluidic system capable of performing rapid electrophoretic separations under a variety of gravitational conditions. This instrument was successfully flown multiple times on NASA’s microgravity research aircraft. A second generation of the lab-on-a-chip instrument was designed with an integrated interface to a NASA flight-certified cell culture bag. This interface was successfully tested on earth and in the microgravity research aircraft using cell media that was spiked with both fluorescently labeled amino acids and green fluorescent protein (GFP).

The aim in this part of the research was to demonstrate the ability of microfabricated devices to repeatedly and reproducibly sample microliter volumes from flight certified cell culture bags and perform capillary electrophoretic separations in 0, lunar, Martian, and hypergravity (1.8g).
