A Multi-faceted Approach to Examine Team Adaptation and Resilience within Isolated, Confined, and Extreme Environments

Record ID: NNX16AM17G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Long-duration exploration mission (LDEM) crews worked and lived together in an isolated, confined, extreme (ICE) environment for a period of up to 30 months. Physical and psychological stressors are inherent in such a mission, triggered by living in a small habitat, in continual close proximity to others, separated from family and friends, while carrying out a dangerous mission with a high workload, and doing so without real- time support from mission control. In addition to on-going, low-grade chronic stressors, a crew on a LDEM was likely to experience several unanticipated acute stressors, including perhaps one or more emergencies. Therefore, a successful LDEM crew would need to self-adapt as a mission progresses, working through and recovering from various challenges, and sustaining their resilience.
Each LDEM crewmember needed to adapt and maintain personal resilience. But, equally important, the crew also needed to adapt and sustain their resilience as a team. Researchers have traditionally examined adaptation and resilience from an individual perspective, but to ensure that LDEM crews were ready for the challenges they would face, there was a need to better understand how adaption and resilience would operate at the team level. Doing so would allow for the development of validated countermeasures that can be deployed prior to and at appropriate times during a mission, increasing a LDEM crew’s ability to handle the stressors associated with ICE environments and enabling them to adjust when unexpected challenges emerge. This study addresses both the fundamental need to learn ore about team adaptation and resilience, as well as the need to develop and test potential countermeasures.
APPROACH:
Investigators looked at data that was previously collected during NASA’s Human Exploration Research Analog (HERA) missions. They indexed the nature of different episodic events, the teams confronted, and associated them with members’ adaptation and resilience by using computer aided text analysis (CATA) of members’ communications. The investigator team had developed a CATA scoring mechanism for multiple dimensions of team processes and resilience which would yield multi-dimensional networks per event. They also explored the construct validity of the data yielded by the sociometric badges as a potential alternative or complementary source of network data.
After reviewing the archived HERA data, investigators gathered data from 36 to 45 Saturation Dive Teams (SAT) over two to three years from three organizations, each with their own organizational culture. Each crew’s 28-day mission runs in parallel with two other crews who are also diving in rotating 8-hour shifts. The three crews worked on the same mission but did not dive or live together simultaneously - each crew lived in their own separate and isolated hyperbaric chamber. Approximately half of the SAT teams received one or more countermeasure(s) and half served as controls for testing the efficacy of the intervention(s).

RESULTS:
Behavioral Health and Performance has conducted literature reviews and operational assessments exploring threats to crew cohesion and performance. They highlight how future-long duration exploration mission (LDEM) crews will, by necessity, have greater autonomy and control than prior crews. This research helped to address the risk to future space exploration crews by enhancing NASA’s understanding of the ‘’key threats, indicators and life cycle of the team for autonomous, long duration and/or distance exploration missions.’’
The investigator team’s work with crews within three LDSM analog environments demonstrated how a diary protocol approach can be used to measure and yield rich, qualitative data about taskwork and teamwork challenges over time. Some evidence of ‘’survey fatigue’’ over time was noted, but participants did not report any resistance or reluctance to complete them. The acceptance may be because the focus of the diary was perceived as very relevant to them. Overall, the diary approach yielded rich qualitative and quantitative data and enabled the collection of longitudinal data in all 3 analog settings.
The findings from the experiment also confirmed that the ICE teams experienced both task-related and non-task related challenges. Crews in some settings may experience more task related challenges than non-task related challenges while crews in other settings may experience the reverse. However, per the findings from the experiment, all crews would benefit from learning how to deal with both types of challenges. The challenges appeared to fall into 4 primary categories:
1.       Personal Needs
2.       Living Environment
3.       Interpersonal
4.       Taskwork
A close examination of the HERA campaigns 5 and 6 crew data suggested that challenges occurred throughout the mission, but different types of challenges were more predominant at different mission phases. Crews adapted less effectively to more disruptive challenges, particularly task-related ones. They reported handling recurring tasks and interpersonal challenges less effectively. This highlighted the importance of dealing with challenges, and in particular interpersonal challenges, when they arose, rather than allowing them to fester or drain a team’s adaptability.
Across the three analogs, a total of 19 types of responses or adaptations to challenges were identified. These fell into seven response categories. Adapting Strategies and Adapting Actions were the two most deployed response categories, but the pattern of responses differed across analogs. The differences that were exhibited made sense, because the types of challenges experienced, and the nature of the mission varied across the three analogs.
In general, crews reported that they handled challenges more effectively when the adaptation processes, they deployed aligned with the nature of the challenge, but other adaptation processes worked at time times as well. The team also discovered that making any type of adjustment helped with Personal challenges, which suggests that it is unhealthy to ignore Personal challenges, and that doing “something” is better than doing nothing in those instances. The team also found that when teams handle challenges more effectively, they report higher levels of resilience, maintain a better mood, and believe they are completing their mission goals and tasks more effectively. In short, it pays to prepare crews to handle and adapt to challenges constructively.
A key focus of this research was to test whether it is possible to inoculate crews against future challenges so they can navigate them more effectively. It was seen that crews that participated in a structured adaptation inoculation exercise were less adversely impacted by challenges that emerged over the course of a mission. In sum, this short, simple, team-led countermeasure showed meaningful results across a 6-week mission. Participants believed that future space crews would benefit from this type of exercise.
