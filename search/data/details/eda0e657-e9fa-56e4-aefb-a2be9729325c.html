Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A significant fraction of waste material (plant residue, greywater and human waste) generated during space missions and on the earth, contains recoverable energy. The specific objective of the project was to optimize an approach that will demonstrate a Plant-based Anaerobic-aerobic Bioreactor Linked Operation (PABLO) that efficiently converts plant residuals and greywater into fuel grade methane (CH4), electricity, and biosolids that will be suitable for plant and fungal culture. This effort will optimize the potential for CH4 production by aerobically treating anaerobically recalcitrant waste materials - biosolids before they are used as a plant growth material. These steps will breakdown the resistant materials, freeing soluble carbon and stabilizing the materials. In a second part of the project we modified the PABLO system to a system concerned with converting human waste in to CH4 and biosolids. The Waste-ABLO (WABLO) approach is for the conversion of human waste into CH4 and biosolid residuals that are condensed but not used in the production of food.

In both the space based and Earth-based systems the starting point was waste materials. The major difference is in the space-based system we dealt primarily with dried residues that will be rewetted with greywater. In the earth-based system, the residues were wet or dry. Outputs from the system include: CH4, CO2 and solids. In this system, the residuals were treated with an aerobic process to and returned to the anaerobic system. The residuals that settle in the system were removed and land applied (earth) or used in confined growth systems (Mars).

The project was divided into three tasks:
Task 1: The objective was to use an anaerobic-aerobic bioreactor system to optimize production of methane from plant-based waste materials and for the recovery of high-quality biosolids.
Task 2: Methods for the optimization of the PABLO biosolids for use in fungal and plant cultivation were investigated. This required an evaluation of the makeup the aerobic reactor’s microbial population to maximize the formation of soluble carbon for use in the anaerobic portion of PABLO.
Task 3: The objective was to use a bioreactor system to optimize production of methane from human waste materials

RESULTS:
For the first set of biological methane production (BMP) experiments, corn residue was milled and added to a phosphate buffer. Anaerobic digester sludge was used as an inoculum. Digesters were incubated at 23oC and periodically checked for gas and acid production over a period of time.

Corn residue in a phosphate buffer showed poor methane production over a 45 day time period. The maximum amount of methane produced was 0.09mLs after 32 days. Investigators also found this trend of low methane production to be true for several other crop residues that were tested. Low methane production resulted from the lignin matrix surrounding cellulose carbon sources, preventing the anaerobic bacteria from accesses these sources. Because of these findings, an efficient and simple pretreatment method should be used on crop residues to reduce the lignin content. There are several methods of pretreatment that could be employed to break down lignin including gamma-ray irradiation, alkali treatment, or aerobic composting. All of these methods would either require hauling large amounts of chemicals or supplies, or consume valuable resources such as oxygen.

Lignin degrading fungi appear to be a promising method of pretreatment for space missions. The mushroom species Pleurotus ostreatus, or Oyster mushroom, is edible, easy to grow, and is able to selectively degrade lignin, leaving the more soluble forms of carbon available for digestion by anaerobic microorganisms. This species also grows well in a high carbon dioxide environment during its longest growth stage, so oxygen consumption could be minimized with careful planning. The oyster mushroom requires a lot of water to sustain good growth; however, investigators have found that this mushroom species is able to grow well when using a mimicked hygiene graywater solution in place of regular water.

The process of growing the Oyster mushroom is quite simple. The mycelium is first grown on a grain, such as wheat or rye, to establish the fungi. Plant materials to be degraded are then pasteurized. Once cooled, the grain grown mycelium are then transferred and mixed with the pasteurized plant materials and grown under high humidity conditions. While the mycelium is being established, they grow best under a higher carbon dioxide concentration. Oyster mushrooms will begin to appear after about 18 days and grow to fruition in just 3-5 days. Graywater can be used throughout the mushroom growing process to conserve drinking water.

After one harvest of mushrooms grown on pasteurized corn residue, graywater was leached through the remaining residue and collected for methane production studies. This time, only the leached graywater and anaerobic sludge was used. No buffer was added. The digesters were incubated at 35oC. Methane production was greatly increased over digested corn residue in buffer, with a maximum production value of 8.3mLs after 35 days.

Milled plant residue alone will not produce enough methane to use a fuel source. A pretreatment method should be used to break down the lignin. The Oyster mushroom is an efficient and simple pretreatment method for degrading the lignin found in plants. This method opens up useable forms of carbon to the anaerobic bacteria and greatly increases the amount of methane produced. Using graywater throughout the methane production process will conserve valuable drinking quality water while recycling another waste material created by the crew on space missions. Degrading plant materials anaerobically is showing great promise for both long-term space missions and here on Earth.

Of the three residues tested, miscanthus produced the least amount of methane during all stages. More methane was produced after pasteurization and at the pinhead stage, but there was little difference between post-pasteurization and the pinhead stage. Corn residue produced more methane after pasteurization and after the pinhead stage, reaching a maximum methane production of 8.6mL and 8.3mL respectively. Tomato residue reached maximum methane production of all treatments at 9.5mL at the pinhead stage. Uninoculated controls were used for all treatments and consistently showed less methane production than inoculated treatments. This suggests that fungi are an effective method of pretreatment for production of methane from crop residues. Further research is needed to optimize this system. Greater methane production may be possible by extending the amount of time the bottles are incubated for. The bottles used for the experiments were small and only held a limited amount of gas. A different gas collection system should be developed so that pressure does not build up in the bottles.
