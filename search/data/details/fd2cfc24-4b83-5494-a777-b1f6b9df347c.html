Molecular Characterization of Choroid Plexus and Hippocampal Damage and Degenerative CNS Risks from Space Radiation

Record ID: NNJ14HP06I
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A major goal of the NASA space radiation research program is to enable human exploration of space without exceeding limits for immediate and persistent risks to the central nervous system (CNS) from space radiation. This research plan builds on the mounting evidence of the radiation-sensitivity of the hippocampus, which is important for memory and performance, and the choroid plexus (CP), which produces cerebral spinal fluid and maintains the blood-brain barrier. Using transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomics, and immunohistochemical analyses of CNS tissue from rodents exposed to simulated space radiation, investigators have identified molecular and cellular changes associated with oxidative stress, neuro-inflammation, vasculature damage, changes in the protein composition of cerebral spinal fluid, and amyloid deposits such as those found in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). They propose new research to investigate the low-fluence responses and post-exposure-time dependence of these findings, to identify the molecular mechanisms of inter-individual variations in behavioral responses after radiation exposure, and to validate the predicted mechanisms using tissue of irradiated and behaviorally characterized rodents. The specific aims are to: (1) Investigate the cellular and functional abnormalities in the hippocampus and CP of HZE-irradiated rats that are predicted by their ‘omics findings with the emphasis on defects associated with AD. (2) Investigate hippocampal and CP radiation damage responses associated with inter-individual variation in radiation-induced neurocognitive deficits, using N-glycoproteomic, metabolomics, and in situ analyses, with emphasis on tissue defects associated with AD. (3) Characterize the radiation sensitivity of persistent CP epithelial and endothelial damage in AD mouse models and examine their associations with neuro-inflammation and other defects associated with AD. This research is foundational to establish quantitative mechanism-based predictors for neuro-cognitive risks and onset of AD symptoms in individuals exposed to high-linear energy transfer (LET) space radiation.

APPROACH:
This project continues to provide NASA with new mechanistic understanding of the risks for impaired neurocognitive performance and individual susceptibility for neurological diseases after exposure to HZE radiation. The approach used an outbred rat model and: (a) employs state-of-the-art ‘omics and tissue technologies to investigate CNS damage response functions in animals exposed to low-fluence HZE particles (i.e., <1 hit per cell), (b) links the type and magnitude of molecular defects with neurocognitive performance, and (c) validates the predicted damage responses in brain tissue. This project focuses on radiation damage to the hippocampus (site of spatial and performance memories) and the choroid plexus (CP, small cauliflower-like tissue in the ventricles adjacent to the hippocampus that produces cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and maintains blood-brain homeostasis). This research distinguishes between two broad categories of risk: (1) mechanisms related to exposure dose, and (2) mechanisms of inter-individual variations in risks for neurocognitive performance, and neurological diseases (i.e., Alzheimer Disease). The working model is that neurocognitive damage after radiation involves direct cell damage to specific CNS target cells as well as indirect damage mediated by the irradiated microenvironment, such as abnormal CSF produced by an abnormal choroid plexus. The underlying hypothesis for this project is that exposure to space-relevant doses of HZE radiation (less than one ion per cell) induces abnormal molecular signaling and cellular damage in CP and hippocampus that determine risks and individual susceptibilities for neurocognitive deficits and neurological diseases such as AD.

RESULTS:
This experiment is in progress. However, preliminary results are available.

Investigators confirmed that the CP is radiation sensitive with significant cellular and molecular damage after exposure to X-ray, 56Fe, or 12C radiation. They have discovered a time-course of molecular signaling changes in the CNS after exposure using transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomics analyses of several brain regions (hippocampus, CP, CSF, and cortex) collected after whole body exposures, usually in individuals also tested on one or more behavioral assays. Persistent changes in gene and protein expression were associated with increased neuroinflammation, increased oxidative stress as well as certain neurological diseases and neurocognitive dysfunctions. These experiments distinguish between radiation effects and normal aging.

Cranial exposure of young adult rats to heavy particle ionizing radiation (HZE, 56Fe) causes neurocognitive deficits that persist to very old age, and the magnitude of the deficits are variable across animals. Beginning at 4 months and increasing in severity over 2 years after exposures, investigators observed histological changes of the CP including occluded endothelial vessels, increases in the frequency of amyloid deposits throughout the CNS, and decreased epithelial expression of proteins that normally prevent amyloid fibril formation in the CNS. They also found increased frequencies of amyloid plaques in exposed animals but noted that the increase in plaque frequency was delayed 4-9 months after exposure. These findings suggest that higher doses of space radiation may increase the risks for Alzheimer-related pathologies in the brain, long after the exposure. Ongoing studies are in progress to understand the effects at space relevant doses of exposure.

Investigators also found that exposure to simulated space radiation diminished the ability of the CP to produce normal cerebral spinal fluid. Animals exposed to 10 and 100cGy 56Fe showed changes in many proteins associated with various neurological diseases, psychological disorders, neuronal cell death and survival. They also discovered molecular signaling changes that are associated with inter-individual variation in behavioral responses to HZE radiation, related to memory deficits and increased anxiety in rats.

In summary, the research in a rat model identified persistent histological and molecular defects in the CNS tissues (CP, CSF, and hippocampus) that are different from normal aging and are associated with long-term neurocognitive dysfunction related to memory deficits, increased anxiety, as well as increased plaque formation long after HZE exposures.
