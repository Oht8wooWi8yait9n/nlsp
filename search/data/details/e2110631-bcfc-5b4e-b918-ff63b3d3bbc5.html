Tissue-Specific Acute and Late Molecular Surveillance of Particle Radiation Effects

Record ID: NNX07AV20G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The space atmosphere is comprised of wide spectrum of highly energetic and charged particle radiation. Exposure to this type of radiation in the galactocosmic environment or unplanned solar particle events (SPE) may pose a potential health hazard to humans in long-term manned space explorations. A large database of information is available on the mutagenic potential of particle radiation on cultured cells; however, limited information is available for multicellular tissue responses. This is primarily due to constraints in the selection of adequate markers in the appropriate tissue systems. Transgenic rodent mutation model systems are widely accepted by regulatory agencies as an appropriate in vivo systems to access the potential carcinogenicity of a variety of test articles. In these systems, multiple copies of the inert reporter gene are integrated into every cell of the genome, making it possible to use it as a tool to measure, both quantitatively and qualitatively, mutations in every tissue of the animal. This experiment uses a transgenic mouse mutation model system to evaluate the immediate and long-term mutagenic potential of simulated space radiation beams in different tissue. Knowledge gained in this ground-based work is directed at understanding how tissue and organ respond to exposures both on the lunar surface as well as in deep space. Information gathered from this research will aid in reducing the uncertainties associated with predicting risks both in the estimation of risk to astronaut health and the design of appropriate countermeasures to ameliorate any potential negative effects in space flights.

APPROACH:
Animals were exposed to a range of low dose-rate simulated solar particle event (sSPE) radiation at the NASA-sponsored Research Laboratory (NSRL) at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL). Peripheral blood was harvested from animals from 1 to 12 days after total body irradiation (TBI) to quantify the level of circulating reticulocytes (RET) and micronucleated reticulocytes (MN-RET) as an early indicator of radiation-induced genotoxicity. Bone marrow lymphocytes and hippocampal tissues from each animal were collected at 12 days and up to two months, to evaluate dose-dependent late effects after sSPE exposure.

RESULTS:
This project demonstrated that the lacZ model system is indeed an efficient and effective model system that can be used to evaluate tissue-specific genomic damage as a function of particle radiation quality. In addition to measuring reporter transgene modifications, aspects of the in vivo system were studied by using a multi-pronged approach to evaluate particle radiation induced perturbations in different organ systems, including cytogenetic damage and recovery in reticulocytes (RETs) populations and lymphocytes and alterations in gene expression profiles in the CNS and the liver tissue. Through this effort, temporally-regulated dose, dose-rate and radiation quality- and dosing regimen-dependent of changes in (i) mutation in the reporter transgene, (ii) gene expression profiles in micro-dissected hippocampus sections from the brain and liver tissues, (iii) persistence of total chromosome/chromatid aberrations as well as chromosome-specific lesions, and (iv) dynamic early changes in levels of RETs and MN-RETs in peripheral blood have bben identified. Mutation analysis of two different tissues in the same animals highlights the importance of tissue specificity considerations in risk analysis. Sequential exposure to particle radiations with different LETs resulted in alterations in gene expressions profiles that are distinctly different from single ion exposures.

The results, from this experiment, to date suggest that low dose rate sSPE radiation impacts the hematopoietic system. However, such events are transient. On the other hand, gene expression profiling in the hippocampus demonstrated that sSPE induced down regulation of neurotrophic genes important to functional integrity of CNS. The results indicate that the concept of dose response must be expanded to not only include quantitative changes in the level of response, but also qualitative changes in the profile of genetic expression. Obviously, higher doses of sSPE displays a gene expression spectrum that is qualitatively different from those obtained from animals that were exposed to lower doses.
