Hyperpolarized Noble Gas Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Record ID: NNX07AP82G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A novel biomedical imaging technique, hyperpolarized (HP) noble gas magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is capable of generating high-resolution images of the lung, heart, and brain by using the spin-1/2 noble gases, 3He and 129Xe. The HP gases, created via spin-exchange optical pumping, have greatly enhanced MR detectability. Unlike conventional proton MRI, HP gas MRI does not require a large magnetic field and, therefore, can be performed in very low magnetic fields (~ 0.01 Tesla). Thus, a small, portable, low-power MRI system, practical for space flight, may be designed to implement very low field HP gas MRI. The imaging capacity of HP gas MRI itself is also relevant to space flight research. Under prior NASA funding, the investigators have obtained (i) cerebral blood flow measurements and (ii) lung gas transfer measurements using HP 129Xe MRI in rats. Additionally, in humans they have (i) obtained high-resolution images of the distal airways in lungs up to the 7th generation, (ii) created models of the three-dimensional lung airway tree, and (iii) obtained very low field (0.015 Tesla) images of the lungs, using HP 3He MRI. They now propose to apply this novel technique to investigations of the physiological and functional states of the lungs, heart, and brain. Studies will focus on regional ventilation and perfusion inhomogeneities of the lungs, patent foramen ovale and pulmonary embolism of the heart, and cerebral function and blood flow of the brain. Their proposed ground-based investigations with HP gas MRI will impact space flight research in three important ways: (i) by facilitating the acquisition of novel information about the physiological structure and function of organs and systems most affected by changes in microgravity; (ii) by developing a new technology that may have clinical applications for the diagnosis of pathologies endemic to microgravity environments; and (iii) by developing treatment strategies that address the continuum of a space-induced illness.

APPROACH:
Investigators developed large volume hyperpolarized 3He and 129Xe systems that take advantage of inexpensive, compact, and mechanically robust high-power diodelasers (in excess of 100 W of radiant energy), which have recently become available. By bringing together an optimized continuous flow optical pumping chamber with a storage and delivery system, a dedicated gas generation system was constructed to produce the large volumes of hyperpolarized gas necessary for this project.

RESULTS:
Hyperpolarized (HP) noble gas MRI using 3He or 129Xe provides highly detailed images of the lungs and brain, and offers the immense advantage of providing information on physiological function, including lung ventilation (3He or 129Xe), lung perfusion (129Xe), and cerebral blood flow (129Xe). For example, HP gas MRI allows investigators to pinpoint precise sections of the lungs where gas is reaching, and where gas is not reaching (ventilation defects), something other measurements of lung function cannot do. Because of its greater sensitivity, HP gas MRI can detect pulmonary or cerebral dysfunction at earlier stages, allowing treatment or counteracting measures to be provided earlier and with greater effectiveness. In addition, HP gas MRI provides imaging of physiological function without exposing subjects to ionizing radiation or chemical contrast agents. This increases safety, and also allows investigators and/or care providers to non-invasively image subjects repeatedly over time.

HP gas MRI has countless applications to evaluating vascular and pulmonary problems that could arise in human space flight. Such applications include investigating the effects of microgravity and pressure changes on the physiological functioning of the lungs, and studying the effects of microgravity and pressure changes associated with space flight on vascular functioning. In addition, HP gas MRI is ideal for evaluating aerosol drug administration, a delivery method of particular interest for space flight because it avoids the need for injections of medicines in space.

In this study, investigators further developed hyperpolarized noble gas MRI for use in examining pulmonary and brain function in animal models and in humans, building upon previous work by the PI funded by NASA. HP gas MRI has immense potential for applications to the study of the physiology of the lungs and brain, and for helping diagnose and inform treatment for adverse reactions to microgravity and low pressure environments. Low-magnetic-field MRI, which is made possible by HP gas MRI, is particularly advantageous in this regard because it provides extraordinarily detailed images of lung and brain function with lightweight, low-cost, easy-to-operate equipment that could be used in space environments. With the assistance of generous funding from NASA, the investigating team was able to make significant advances in many areas of HP gas MRI, including pulmonary ventilation analysis and low-magnetic-field MRI.
