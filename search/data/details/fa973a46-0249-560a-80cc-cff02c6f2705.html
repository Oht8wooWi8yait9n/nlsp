Advanced Technology Development of Laser-polarized Noble Gas Magnetic Resonance

Record ID: NAG91489
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This study is a continuation of previous research into the development of laser-polarized noble gas nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). NMR is an emerging biomedical technology that has applications for ground-based and possibly, space-based, non-invasive imaging of the body. Using laser-polarized noble gases (helium and xenon), this technology is a novel multi-functional tool that allows traditional high resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of tissues, as well as non-invasive measurements of tissue perfusion. NMR does not require a large magnetic field like traditional MRIs; thus, it is comparatively lightweight and low-power, making it feasible for use in space flight.

Possible space-based applications for NMR in non-invasive research and diagnostics include exploring the effect of microgravity on the cardiopulmonary system and lung perfusion and measuring cardiopulmonary function (e.g. ventilation, perfusion, gas exchange and concentration). Additionally, ground-based applications include clinical diagnosis and treatment of lung disease and non-invasive measurement of localized blood flow in a variety of organs.

APPROACH:
The investigators developed and tested a second-generation low-field MRI system for optimized open-access human lung imaging. The B0 coils are based on an eighth-order, bi-planar, constant-current four-coil design, providing up to 0.01 tesla with a homogeneity of 50 ppm in a 40 cm diameter spherical volume with an inter-coil distance of ~ 90 cm. The B0 coils are mounted on circular aluminum flanges that are slotted to minimize eddy currents. A water-cooled Alpha current supply provides up to 100 amperes, which run in series through all four coils with stability better than 10 ppm. The magnet is housed in an RF-shielded enclosure that provides noise attenuation better than 80 db at the 50-500 kHz range. Three planar gradient coils have been designed and constructed to provide up to 0.2 G/cm with a linearity suitable for 256 x 256 images with a 40 cm field of view. The gradient coils have greatly improved heat sinking compared to the first-generation system, and can thus generate fast (~ 1 ms) pulsed magnetic field gradients without the need for long delays between echo acquisitions.

The second-generation system also features a commercial RF control and acquisition console designed to operate at kHz frequencies without modification, while a single RF transmit/receive (B1) coil reduces cross-coupling noise in a fully 50 O impedance-matched system. The investigators studied several options for B1 coils, and determined the most useful and practical to be a single solenoidal coil worn by the subject around the chest.

As a demonstration of the second-generation low-field imager’s capability, two subjects were imaged a total of six times, in both horizontal (supine) and vertical (sitting) body orientations. IRB approval was obtained for 3He inhalation/imaging from the Partners Human Research Committee (Massachusetts General/Brigham & Women’s Hospitals) under an inter-institutional IRB agreement with the Harvard University Committee for the Use of Human Subjects. Each subject inhaled ~ 500 cm3 of laser-polarized 3He gas from a Tedlar bag. We employed 2D and 3D gradient-recalled echo sequences. 2D projection images were acquired in both orientations without slice selection over a 50 cm FOV, in ~ 5 seconds. 3D images were acquired over a 50 x 50 x 12 cm FOV, in ~ 30 seconds.

RESULTS:
The 3D imaging technique provides a clear representation of the lungs. After rendering, the pleural boundaries are clear and the cardiac cavity is clearly visible. In the 2D slices, the effect of the diaphragm is seen on the lower portion of the lungs. When the subject is sitting vertically, the characteristic curvature from the diaphragm at the bottom of the lungs seen when the subject is supine is no long visible, and the lungs are distended slightly.
