Comparison of Bending Stiffness in the Dominant and Non-dominant Tibias of Women

Record ID: ARCbedrest142
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
There is limited information on the mechanical properties of the long bones of healthy women compared to the amount of data on the density of bone. We were interested in determining the mechanical strength of a long bone and evaluating it in reference to the strength of the muscles of the same bone. We were also interested in determining gender differences in the laterality of tibial strength since previous data in normal men revealed the left tibia was stronger than the right.

APPROACH:
Twenty women (age 44±11 yrs) were recruited from the working staff of Ames Research Center. All gave informed consent for the study and provided brief historical information on general health and physical activity. The tests for muscle strength were carried out by quantifying the forces required to extend and flex the leg with a LIDO instrument. A non-invasive test of bone strength was performed in the tibia of a relaxed recumbent subject with the probe of instrument positioned on the center of the ‘shin’ bone. The MRTA delivers a “buzz”, detects the response of the bone to a brief vibration at the same site on which the probe is centered and then analyzes the response curve by software that generates a measurement for bending stiffness or EI (E is the modulus of elasticity and I the moment of inertia) for the tibia. The instrument had been previously validated in the bones of monkeys by correlating the non-invasive measurement with three-point bending in a mechanical instrument in the same bone (0.95 was the significance of the results of the in vivo test vs. the extracted bone tested with a mechanical instrument). Gender comparisons were done on a population of 22 normal men, aged 39±7 yrs studied earlier (ref #4).

RESULTS:
In contrast to the findings in men, there was no difference in the bending stiffness of the right and left tibias (150±71 vs. 151±47Nm2). Values in women averaged 28% lower than in men, appropriate for the 24% difference in body weight (men weighed 80±10 vs. women 61±10 kg). Women were shorter than the men but had the same body mass index (BMI = wt/ht2 = 23) and tibial length. Flexion and extension of the right and left legs were also similar (83±22 vs. 84±31 in Nm for flexion and 177±38 vs. 168±51 Nm for extension) and unrelated to EI. We concluded that tibial strength is appropriate for its load carrying function in both sexes, symmetrical in women, but not in men.
