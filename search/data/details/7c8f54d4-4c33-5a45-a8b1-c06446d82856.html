Bed Rest Combined with 0.5% CO2 as a Spaceflight Analog to Study Neurocognitive Changes: Extent, Longevity, and Neural Bases

Record ID: 80NSSC17K0021
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Previous investigations have shown that exposure to microgravity can have significant effects on crewmembers’ posture control, locomotion, and manual control during and after flight. Additional studies provide evidence supporting the idea that the brain may experience structural and functional remodeling similar to the remodeling seen in bone and muscle. In other studies, exposure to microgravity has been shown to result in cortical reorganization of sensory and motor systems. However, the absence of supporting data in human subjects limits the understanding of whether the human brain is similarly effected following space flight, or even participation in space flight analogs.

The goal of this investigation is to quantify neurocognitive changes and associated neural structural and functional alterations occurring as a result of a 30 day head down tilt bed rest plus 0.5% CO2 environment, a well-established space flight analog. Within that context, the objectives of this study are to:

  1. Identify changes in brain structure, function, and network integrity as a function of 30 days head down tilt bed rest plus 0.5% CO2 and characterize the time course of the changes
  2. Specify relationships between structural and functional brain changes and performance and characterize their time course
  3. Identify predictors of individual differences in bed rest plus 0.5% CO2 responses

APPROACH:
This study will use advanced neuroimaging techniques with a battery of behavioral (cognitive, motor, and sensory) assessments to explore the relationships between neural changes and behavioral function. Twelve subjects are required. The subjects will undergo 30 days of head down tilt bed rest with elevated ambient (+0.05%) carbon dioxide. There are no control subjects.

There will be two data collections pre-bed rest at 12 days prior to bed rest (BR-12 days) and BR-7 days, two data collections post-bed rest at 6 days after bed rest (BR+6 days) and BR+13 days, and two data collections during bed rest at bed rest day 7 (BR7) and BR30. At each data collection, brain structural and functional images will be obtained. At the same time points, a broad ranging battery of cognitive, motor, and sensory tests will be performed. However, data collection on BR7 will not include standing tests. On BR30/BR+0, MRI scans are planned for BR30 with upright tests on BR+0. Therefore, balance and mobility tests will only be performed pre- and post-bed rest.

Neuroimaging tests will include both passive structural and functional scans and task-based functional MRI (fMRI) scans. A high-resolution structural MRI scan will provide volumetric measures of brain structures. An ultra-high resolution scan of the hippocampus will also be acquired. Resting state functional connectivity MRI (fcMRI) and Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) scans will be obtained and used to study functional and structural network connectivity.

fMRI scans will be accomplished as subjects perform a variety of behavioral tests including cognitive tests, hand motor function tests, and vestibular function tests. Performance on the behavioral tests will be correlated with measures of brain structure, function, and network integrity.

Results of this study will be compared with results of other related studies, completed or in progress, by members of the investigative team.

RESULTS:
Results are not yet available for this investigation. However, this study may result in identification of the underlying neural mechanisms and operational risks of space flight induced changes in behavior using an established space flight analog. Further, it may determine whether a return to normative behavioral function following bed rest is associated with a restitution of brain structure and function or whether it is supported by substitution with compensatory brain processes.
