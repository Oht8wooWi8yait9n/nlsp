Summary / Description

Mir 20, which launched from Baikonur in Russia, was a precursor of the multinational missions typical of the International Space Station today. It was the second Mir mission with a simultaneous Euromir designation, and the second with an European Space Agency (ESA) astronaut, Cosmonaut Researcher/Flight Engineer Thomas Reiter, as part of the crew.

The Mir 20 crew consisted of Commander Yuriy Gidzenko and Flight Engineer Sergey Avdeyev.

The scientific objectives of Euromir 95 were to study effects of microgravity on the human body, to experiment with the development of new materials in a space environment, to capture samples of cosmic dust and man-made particles in low Earth orbit, and to test new space equipment. Mir 20 was also the second Mir mission to include a U.S. Space Shuttle orbiter docking, STS-74. During that phase of the mission, the station complex housed crews from four countries representing the Russian, Canadian, and U.S. space agencies, as well as ESA.

The Mir 20 crew began activating and calibrating the Euromir 95 experiments on September 13, 1995. The 41 experiments included 18 in life sciences, 5 in astrophysics, 8 in materials science, and 10 in technology.

Life sciences experiments were mostly investigations of weightlessness effects on the human body, including:

  • Cardiovascular monitoring through a network of blood pressure sensors at various points on the body,
  • Bone mass loss studies done by simulating heel striking in weightlessness, and
  • Studies on the effects of weightlessness on kidney, lung and muscle functions.

    A major astrophysics study involved capturing and analyzing natural and man-made particles in low Earth orbit in the European Space Exposure Facility on Spektr.

    Technology experiments included radiation monitoring to study the effects of the space environment on electronic components, methods of measuring microbial contaminants in the station interior, and measurement of disturbances produced by the movements of a small robotic arm.

    Many of the materials science experiments involved the processing of alloy, glass, and semiconductor samples in TITUS, a six-zone tubular furnace capable of attaining 1250°C.

    An average of 4.5 hours each day was allotted for experiment work with the rest of the time devoted to exercise and station maintenance. The Mir 20 crew was active in the Euromir experiments, and the ESA crewmember participated in Russian experiments. As flight engineer, the ESA crewmember also helped to maintain the station's on board equipment.

    Originally scheduled for a mission length of 135 days, ESA and the Russian organizations extended the Mir 20/Euromir 95 missions by 44 days. This extension allowed additional scientific research and a third extravehicular activity (EVA).
