Effect of Microgravity on Renal and Femoral Hemodynamics During Lower Body Negative Pressure and Intravenous Saline Load

Record ID: D2ARCARD1
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of adaptation to microgravity on the control of regional hemodynamics during acute changes in central blood volume and cardiac filling.

APPROACH:
Doppler ultrasound was used to examine renal and femoral hemodynamics in four payload crew members at rest and during progressive lower body negative pressure (LBNP) before and after the 10-day D-2 Spacelab flight and during intravenous saline loading (IVSL) before, during, and after flight. LBNP included 15 min at -11 mm Hg, 5 min at 30 mm Hg, and 15 min at -40 mm Hg and echocardiographic data collection was initiated at 5, 1, and 5 minutes, respectively. LBNP was performed preflight (L-60d and L- 20d), and Postflight (R+ 1 and R+9d). IVSL consisted of an infusion of 22 ml/kg isotonic saline over 20 min. IVSL was performed twice preflight, once with the subject sitting and once supine (L-270d and L-lS0d), inflight (+5d) and postflight (R+7d, supine). Renal vascular resistance (Rr) and femoral arterial flow (Qf) and vascular resistance (Rf) were measured before and during each procedure. Data are presented as per cent change relative to measurements obtained at rest shortly before each intervention. The peripheral circulatory measurements were evaluated in the context of central hemodynamic data obtained at rest before each test. Left ventricular end systolic and diastolic volumes, stroke volume, and cardiac output (LVDV, LVSV, SV, and CO) were measured by echocardiography.

RESULTS:
Renal vascular resistance at rest was reduced inflight (-10 % at +5d), and postflight (-5 % at + Id). Femoral vascular resistance was also lower inflight (-7 %), but had returned to preflight levels by postflight day R+ 1. LVDV and SV were lower inflight (-5 %) and postflight (-10 %) than preflight whereas CO was higher inflight (+ 15 %) due to heart rate increase, but had returned to baseline by postflight day R+1.

During preflight LBNP renal vascular resistance fell by approximately 10 % at -11 mm and IS % at -40 mm Hg. The magnitude of this decrease was similar during pre- and postflight LBNP.

Femoral flow decreased progressively from -28 % at -11 mm Hg to -40 % at -40 mm Hg according to the combined data from the two preflight LBNP sessions. The magnitude of this response was reduced during the postflight (R+ Id) to -15 % at -11 mm Hg and -25 % at -40 mm Hg.

The combined data from the two preflight LBNP sessions indicate that femoral vascular resistance increased progressively from +35 % at -11 mm Hg to +63 % at -40 mm Hg relative to the mean preflight resting level. Femoral vascular resistance increased significantly less during the postflight (R+ 1) LBNP session, + 15 % at -11 mm Hg and +25 % at -40 mm Hg. The heart rate increase was similar during all the pre- and postflight LBNP sessions.

During preflight IVSL, renal vascular resistance was approximately 5 % higher by the end of infusion than at rest. The magnitude of this increase was not significantly different inflight (+ 10 % at day 5) compared to preflight responses Sitting or supine. Femoral flow increased during the preflight test (+ 10 % sitting, +5 % supine), the magnitude of change was +5 % during the inflight test. Femoral vascular resistance decreased during the preflight saline load (-10 % sitting and -17 % supine). A similar decrease was recorded inflight (-16 %).

The changes in regional hemodynamics at rest conformed to the expected pattern. Systemic cardiovascular adaptation to microgravity may be viewed as a restoration of the hemodynamic conditions that normally prevail in the upright body position at Ig with central hypovolemia relative to supine rest at 1 g. Left ventricular end diastolic volume and stroke volume at rest inflight and early postflight were also below preflight supine levels, consistent with moderate hypovolemia. Renal vascular resistance at rest was reduced inflight on day 5 and early postflight (R+1). Femoral resistance at rest was also decreased inflight. The general features of adaptation of the renal circulation to changes of the central blood volume and cardiac filling have been well documented: renal resistance increases and decreases in parallel with changes in left ventricular end diastolic volume. A similar response pattern exists also in the cerebral circulation. Conversely the femoral resistance always decreases at rest during spaceflights and HDT, whatever countermeasures are used or not, whereas the femoral flow follows quite closely the cardiac output changes. This constant decrease of the lower limb resistances is in favor of a reduction of the vascular tone in the lower limb vascular network. On the other hand the passive changes of the femoral flow in relation with the cardiac output confirms that the femoral flow is poorly regulated in comparison to the cerebral one.

During the LBNP renal resistance decreased progressively as the pressure became more negative and caused a progressive decrease in central blood volume and cardiac filling, but the renal vascular response to LBNP was not significantly different pre- and postflight. The decreased resistance response in the femoral circulation during the postflight LBNP is consistent with a significant regulatory defect affecting lower limb postflight vascular response, and perhaps also the regulation of skeletal muscle blood flow in other regions. An attenuated femoral resistance response has previously been observed in subjects exhibiting orthostatic intolerance, e.g. in normal volunteers following prolonged head down tilt (HDT), and in two astronauts studied during and after 15 and 20 day spaceflights. The femoral vascular resistance index, (defined as the reflexion coefficient of the systolic velocity wave on the distal vascular resistance) has been found to be an accurate predictor of orthostatic intolerance during LBNP test. A decreased resistance index compared to the baseline level indicates a failure to produce increased vasoconstriction in the lower limb to compensate for a decrease in cardiac filling, stroke volume, and cardiac output, induced by actual or simulated microgravity. Recent studies including a detailed analysis of the systemic cardiovascular response to postflight orthostatic stress, based on studies in 14 astronauts following flights of 10 to 14 days (Blomqvist et al., to be published), clearly identified inadequate systemic vasoconstriction as the principal feature separating finishers from non-finishers of a stand test performed within 4 hours of landing.

In the present series of 3 subjects, pre- and inflight renal and femoral hemodynamic responses to IVSL were similar despite the reduced central blood volume and cardiac filling after 5 days at microgravity. The femoral flow increased as the volemia during the infusion period which confirms the absence of sensitive regulation in the lower limb vascular bed. The decreases in regional resistances paralleled the changes in total peripheral resistance observed before and during ground based simulations. This suggests that the regulatory defect induced by microgravity may primarily limit the maximal range of vasoconstriction.
