Organ Dose Measurement Using the Phantom Torso

Record ID: 93-E039
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The primary risk to long-duration space flight crewmembers is radiation-induced cancers. One of the main obstacles in assessing radiation risk in manned space flight is related to the estimation of organ level radiation dose-equivalent. Currently both experimental and operational methods for assessing radiation dose are limited to the measurement of surface (skin) dose. Organ level dose is approximated only by calculations using the radiation environment model and the appropriate radiation transport models. These estimates may be overly conservative because of their known limits of accuracy. The accuracy problem is two-fold: separate models are required for the spacecraft shielding distribution and anatomical male or female models, and the incident radiation models are inaccurate. The inaccuracy comes from the fact that they do not account for anisotropy and do not include the production of secondary particles within the spacecraft and the human body. This study proposed to provide the necessary depth-dose-equivalent measurements using a fully instrumented Phantom Torso (with head). The Phantom Torso was to be assembled on orbit, and the sensors activated. Depth-dose-equivalent measurements were to be taken as a function of spacecraft altitude, attitude, location and time. Measurements internal to the Phantom Torso would be supported by other radiation measurement instrumentation.

APPROACH:
The radiation data for this experiment were collected via three main pieces of hardware: a Phantom Torso, Tissue Equivalent Proportional Counter (TEPC), and Charged Particle Directional Spectrometer (CPDS). The Phantom Torso mimicked a human torso in size and shape, and contained over 300 passive dosimeters and three active dosimeters.

Space Shuttle flight 5A.1 carried the Tissue Equivalent Proportional Counter (TEPC), Charged Particle Directional Spectrometer (CPDS), and associated hardware to the International Space Station (ISS). Crewmembers deployed the experiment hardware in empty rack space in the U.S. Lab. The Phantom Torso and remaining experiment hardware was carried to the International Space Station (ISS) by Shuttle flight 6A.

The experiment was designed as follows. After the Phantom Torso was set-up, all of the experiment hardware would be activated. All three pieces of hardware (Phantom Torso, CPDS, and TEPC) were designed to continuously collect data without crew intervention. Data from the CPDS, TEPC, and Phantom Torso (each active dosimeter) were to be downloaded separately and sequentially to the onboard computer every 7 to 10 days. Data would then be downlinked to the ground at the earliest convenience. Deactivation, disassembly, and stowage of experiment hardware would take place as late as possible before departure from the ISS.

RESULTS:
The results of the Torso experiment show that models of space radiation transport developed by the NASA Langley Research Center and the NASA Johnson Space Center are in good agreement with the measured organ doses. The largest differences observed are less that 15% between measurements and calculations. The majority of organ biological doses (80%) on station were from GCRs because trapped protons were well attenuated by the spacecraft and skin and because of the higher biological effectiveness of the GCR. The average quality factor (a measure of the impact of space radiation compared to X-rays) was shown to have a value of about 2.6 on ISS.

NASA uses a risk assessment model that employs orbital inclination, altitude, phase in the solar cycle, vehicle shielding, and body shielding when calculating the risk from radiation. Data from the Phantom Torso experiment indicated the prediction of radiation dose and dose equivalent by the model to have an accuracy of ±25%. Torso results also indicate the need to improve the measurements on secondary neutrons in space flight.
