Space Adaptation Effects on Immune System Impacts Reproductive Function and Mammary Gland Development Across Generations

Record ID: NNA15BA16I
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVE:
Space travel results in adaptation to unique conditions that include exposure to low doses of cosmic radiation and microgravity, and heightened stress. Adapting to these physiological and psychological stressors can alter hormone and immune functions, disrupting normal development of hormone-sensitive organs and negatively impacting reproductive health. Studies investigating mammary gland development in young adult mice will help define how adaptations to space travel can influence these fundamental processes. The ductal network of the mammary gland contains a number of different cell types including epithelial cells, adipocytes, immune cells, vascular endothelial cells, and fibroblasts. Signaling between these cell types is highly sensitive to environmental perturbations. In addition, both hormone and immune responses have been shown to play a critical role in ductal network branching of the developing mammary gland. A combination of novel assays will be used to understand how space exposures impact immune function adversely affecting mammary gland development, stem cell populations, and the reproductive ability of mice. We will test whether space adaptation-induced epigenetic changes in the oocytes of mice correlate with reproductive ability. We hypothesize that the space environment weakens the immune system, and alters hormone balance resulting in alterations in number and function of mammary stem cells and development of the mammary gland. Additionally, we hypothesize that epigenetic changes as a result of space exposure will affect reproductive capability as compared to mice on Earth. We further propose that the sensitivity to these effects is dependent on the genetic background of exposed mice. These studies will aid in defining how alterations in immune function from adaptation to space travel and subsequent re-adaptation on Earth, results in defects in organ development, reproductive ability, and epigenetic changes affecting the fitness of subsequent generations.

Definition stage, ground-based experiments to prepare for in-flight space studies. Hypothesis for ground-based study: We will test the hypothesis that the genetically driven sensitivity of the immune system affects mammary gland development post fractionated Low LET (low energy transfer) radiation exposures. In space flight, mice are subject to chronic low-dose radiation exposures. This low dose of radiation exposure may have a significant impact on endocrine function and immune response and influence organ development. Prior to in-flight experiments it would be useful to perform ground-based studies using fractionated X-ray exposures to streamline procedures and to obtain information regarding how genetic background and associated immune system response impacts mammary gland development following radiation exposures. To answer this, we propose to test our hypothesis with the following specific aims in these modified ground-based studies:

AIM 1: To determine how low-dose fractionated radiation affects immune function in young adult mice and its consequences on mammary organ development.

AIM 1.1: To examine acute effects of immune response to low-dose fractionated radiation on mammary gland development.

AIM 1.2: To investigate how genetic background modulates the biological effects of fractionated radiation.

APPROACH:
The preliminary portion of this study involves ground-based work to optimize protocols and define whether very low doses of fractionated radiation exposures, given to mimic the low constant radiation in space, will influence mammary gland development and genetics role in this effect. For this study we will sacrifice two genetically diverse strains of mice (C57BL/6 radiosensitive and BALB/c radiosensitive) and perform analysis of endpoints identical to that described for the ISS experiments. Initially we will just expose mice to radiation, but in subsequent experiments we will also expose mice to LPS prior to radiation as will be done in space. These studies will help to optimize assays and timepoints for in flight experiments. For ground-based radiation only work we have performed one study using 36 mice and a second study we are in the midst of using 24 mice (in both cases ½ of each of these mice are of a different genetic background). Our next phase will involve a smaller experiment exposing 24 mice to two doses LPS (12 mice each dose) to confirm optimal dose +/- radiation and its dependence on the mouse strain.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulates the innate immune response and will allow us to test the immune system function following the low dose radiation or aboard ISS. Once these preliminary ground-based studies are complete, we will be ready for the next phase of the work aboard ISS. The ISS study entails two different phases, one centering on understanding acute effects of adaptation to space environment, whereby animals will be sacrificed in space and relevant organs and blood sent back for processing. The second phase of the study will focus on understanding re-adaptation effects, and for these studies animals will be sent back and studied for a period before sacrificing a fraction of the animals to test as in AIM 1. Additionally the effects on reproduction and potential epigenetic effects on sperm and egg will be studied by mating some of the mice upon re-adaptation to earth. Overall ~240 mice will be used in this study.

RESULTS:
This experiment is currently in progress. Results will be available at the conclusion of the study.
