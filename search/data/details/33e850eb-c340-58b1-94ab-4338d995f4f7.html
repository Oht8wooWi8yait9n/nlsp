Vertebral Strength and Fracture Risk Following Long Duration Spaceflight

Record ID: Vertebral_Strength
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Astronauts participating in long-duration spaceflights may be at an increased risk for skeletal fragility and bone fractures due to bone loss experienced as a result of exposure to microgravity. Past investigations have used dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) to study the effects of spaceflight on bone strength and fracture risk. However, newer technologies, such as 3D quantitative computed tomography (QCT) may yield better results.

Not only can the newer technologies overcome limitations of DXA-based measurements, QCT images can be used to estimate bone strength using finite element analysis, a standard engineering approach. In fact, QCT images have been used successfully to demonstrate negative effects of spaceflight on hip bone density and strength. However, a similar examination of the effects of spaceflight on vertebral strength has not been performed. Thus the degree of spinal deconditioning and subsequent risk of vertebral fracture following long-duration spaceflight remains unknown.

This study will use validated, state-of-the-art methods to investigate and quantify effects of spaceflight on vertebral strength and the risk of spine fracture. The objectives are to:

  1. Assess changes in volumetric bone density and strength of lumbar vertebrae using previously collected QCT scans from long duration ISS crewmembers
  2. Estimate the risk of spine fracture by computing a load-to-strength ratio in each astronaut for different activities of daily living

APPROACH:
This is a ground study which will use previously collected 3D QCT scans from long duration International Space Station (ISS) crew members. Scans to be used include preflight scans and one year postflight scans for all subjects. Additional scans collected from two to four and a half years postflight for a smaller subset of subjects will also be used. Vertebral strength will be measured using an FDA-approved method that employs finite element analyses of QCT scans, and has been validated against in vitro bone strength testing as well as in large, population-based studies of spine fracture. Loads applied to the spine will be estimated using a musculoskeletal model of the lumbar and thoracic spine that can be adjusted to reflect the subject’s height, weight, spinal shape and specific trunk muscle anatomy.
