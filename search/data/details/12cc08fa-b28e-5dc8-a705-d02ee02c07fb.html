Synergistic Effect of Zero-g and Radiation on White Blood Cells

Record ID: S004
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Biological effects, such as genetic mutation, chromosome aberrations and cell lysis, were seen in both ballistic and orbital space flights that occurred before Gemini 3. In some cases, these effects were of magnitudes many times greater than would have been predicted from the expected radiation exposure received during flight. These magnitudes were thought to occur for two reasons: (1) it was thought that the components (particles) of radiation above the Earth’s atmosphere might have unexpected biological effects that could not be predicted in Earth-based laboratories and (2) it was thought that radiation and zero-g acted synergistically to produce unexpectedly large effects. This experiment was conducted to test these two possibilities.

APPROACH:
A series of human whole blood samples was irradiated during the mission with radioactive phosphorus (32P) beta-rays, with a known quantity and quality of radiation. Inflight control samples were also used, and were not irradiated with the experiment source, but just flew to encounter the normal radiation of the space flight. An identical series of blood samples was irradiated on the ground and used as a control. Increased aberrations in the inflight samples, including the control, would be evidence of an effect of some space flight parameter. Any effect not seen in the inflight control would be attributed to a synergism between radiation and some space flight parameter.

Because induction of chromosome aberrations is a non-linear function of radiation dose, a graded series of four different radiation exposures was used. Two complete sets of blood samples were flown to protect against loss of data; each sample set was from a different blood donor. In total, ten 3 ml blood samples were flown; eight were irradiated with each of the four doses, and two were not irradiated and served as the inflight controls. The simultaneous ground control experiment consisted of ten 3 ml samples drawn from the same donors at the same time as the inflight samples. As an additional control, blood samples were obtained from the flight crew before and after the flight, and short-term leukocyte cultures were prepared. Preflight, on Gemini 3, the samples were obtained as close to the mission as possible (2 days prior to launch) from both the flight and backup crews, so that the samples would remain viable. On Gemini 11, the samples were obtained 9 days prior to launch. On both flights, the leukocyte cultures were prepared and incubated at 37 degrees C, treated with colchicine, fixed and then chromosome preparations were made.

Irradiation was accomplished by identical experimental devices, one on the ground at the launch site, and the other on the righthand hatch of the Gemini 3 spacecraft. Each device contained 22 fluoroglass dosimeters. Approximately 9 hours before launch the two identical experiment devices were assembled and tested, then at 210 minutes before launch (for Gemini 3) and 150 minutes before launch (for Gemini 11), the flight unit was mounted into the spacecraft. On Gemini 3, the ground-control unit was placed inside a temperature-controlled cabinet, in which the temperature was periodically adjusted to correspond to the temperature readout from the spacecraft. The longer duration of Gemini 11 meant that the samples were in the weightless period much longer before they were irradiated. Samples were refrigerated inflight to ensure the survival of the leukocytes. Therefore, the ground control unit was placed inside a refrigerator that matched the refrigerator temperature of the inflight samples (2 to 4 degrees Celsius). Furthermore, the device provided a telemetry signal for monitoring the temperature on the ground, an improvement over the Gemini 3 mission.

Irradiation was initiated by the pilot 50 minutes and 18 seconds after launch, and continued for 20 minutes. Correspondingly, the ground unit was activated and deactivated at 52 and 72 minutes, respectively. Except for minor accelerations caused by maneuvering “burns” of the spacecraft, the flight device remained weightless for nearly 3.5 hours.

Ideally, the team wanted to use X- or gamma-rays to irradiate the blood samples, but could not for two reasons: (1) the small mass allotted to them for the experiment excluded it and (2) too much shielding would have been required to protect the flight crew. Therefore, beta-rays were chosen. Radioactive phosphorus (32P) was chosen as the radiation source for three reasons: (1)it emits only a single beta-particle, (2) the particle energy was suitable and (3) it had been used extensively in radiobiology studies to date. On Gemini 11, the amount of 32P in the device was decreased, since the samples would be subjected to increased beta-ray leakage and Bremsstrahlung radiation within the device.

After the mission, blood samples were obtained as soon as possible from the flight crew (6.5 hours after landing). By 10 hours after launch, all blood samples had been put into culture, and all cultures were fixed between 72 and 77 hours after launch. A cytogenetic analysis was made of each sample; the frequencies of chromosome aberrations were determined. Yields of both single-break and multiple-break aberrations were calculated, both for the flight samples as well as the ground samples.

Production of chromosomal aberrations is one of the best known effects of radiation, so it was selected as the parameter to measure for this study. Human leukocytes were chosen as the cell to be studied, since the cells would all be in a uniformly sensitive stage in the cell cycle and because the investigator team had extensive experience working with leukoctyes.

RESULTS:
Gemini 3 postflight analysis of the fluoroglass dosimeters in the experiment device showed that the doses, start/stop times of irradiation, and temperatures for the ground and flight units all agreed well. For Gemini 3, the flight device temperature stayed between 14 and 38 degrees Celsius, whereas the ground device stayed within a 2 to 29 degree Celsius range. For Gemini 11, the temperature of the flight device increased, starting at approximately 14 minutes after launch, to between 6 to 10 degrees Celsius and remained in that range for the first 24 hours of the mission. The device temperature decreased when the spacecraft was depressurized for the first extravehicular activity (EVA). The device operated within the normal 2 to 4 degree range until switched off simultaneously with the ground unit after approximately 65 hours inflight. The ground unit always remained within the normal temperature range. Later, it was discovered that an unusually high hatch temperature caused the inflight high refrigerator temperature. The higher temperatures did not affect the blood samples nor the results.

The dosimeters also confirmed that the radiation exposures were of the required duration. The inflight readings of the 22 dosimeters in the flight device compared well with the readings of the 22 dosimeters in the ground device. Doses were estimated for preflight and postflight by means of fluoroglass or Fricke-solution dosimetry. As a result, there was a 2 rad dose on the ground samples, with a large portion (about one-third) of that being Bremsstrahlung with a peak energy of about 65 keV. The inflight package registered somewhat higher exposures than 2 rad.

All of the blood sample cultures were successful and yielded satisfactory chromosome preparations. Very little hemolysis was noted. A total of 4600 cells were analyzed.

Results showed that the occurrence of single-break aberrations was much higher in the flight samples. There was no significant difference between the yields of multiple-break aberrations in the flight and ground control samples. The flight crew chromosome aberration analyses showed no increase in aberration frequency due to space flight. However, the small positive effect seen in the differences of single-break aberrations did stimulate further scientific interest in this topic. It appeared that some space flight factor did interact synergistically with radiation to affect the chromosome rejoining system (i.e. the cell’s ability to repair damage), thus producing the single breaks.

On Gemini 11, a parallel test was also conducted on the bread mold Neurospora crassa. Those results are reported in another experiment record in this archive.
