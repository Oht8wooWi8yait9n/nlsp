Arterial Remodeling and Functional Adaptations Induced by Microgravity

Record ID: 9802386
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

Astronauts returning to earth may experience postflight orthostatic intolerance, a condition characterized by the tendency to become light-headed or faint upon standing. Although hypovolemia contributes to this syndrome, evidence is mounting that it may also stem from fundamental alterations in peripheral vascular resistance. The purpose of this study is to determine whether space flight diminishes the ability of peripheral resistance arteries to constrict and if so, what specific factors are involved. In particular, this investigation will evaluate the potential roles of: smooth-muscle atrophy, modified sensitivity to the neurotransmitters norepinephrine and acetylcholine, and alterations in the body's ability to produce the vasodilator, nitric oxide, in space. The general hypothesis of this investigation is that exposure to weightlessness induces adaptations in resistance artery function (resistance arteries being arteries whose flow characteristics can be modulated to adjust vascular resistance). This adaptation, while appropriate to microgravity, is inappropriate upon return to the Earth's normal gravitational field.

APPROACH:

To test this hypothesis, three groups of rats were used: one in space flight and two ground-based controls. It was planned for the soleus-plantaris-gastrocnemius muscle complex to be removed from both hindlimbs of each rat. Then, three resistance arteries were to be isolated from each pair of muscles. The first of these arterioles were to be used to conduct physiology experiments on vascular responsiveness. The second arteriole was to be frozen for gene expression studies, and the third arteriole was to be used to determine vessel morphology.

RESULTS:

The payload was not recovered.
