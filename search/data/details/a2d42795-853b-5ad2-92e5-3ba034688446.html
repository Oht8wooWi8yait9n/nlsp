Forecasting Sensorimotor Adaptability from Baseline Inter-Trial Correlations

Record ID: NCC958PF04104
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
One of the greatest challenges surrounding sensorimotor adaptation to the space flight environment is the large variability in symptoms, and corresponding functional impairments, from one crewmember to the next. This renders preflight training and in-flight countermeasure development difficult, as a one-size-fits-all approach is inappropriate. From an operational perspective, it would be highly advantageous to know ahead of time which crewmembers might have more difficulty adjusting to the novel gravity levels inherent to space flight. This type of knowledge could guide individually customized countermeasures to maximize crew safety and mission success.

The primary objective of this project was to look for baseline performance metrics that relate to locomotor adaptability. A novel hypothesis was proposed that considers baseline inter-trial correlations, the trial-to-trial fluctuations (noise) in motor performance, as a predictor of individual sensorimotor adaptability. Previous studies have demonstrated that baseline inter-trial correlations are strongly related to oculomotor adaptability. The goal of this project was to look for an analogous relationship in the locomotor system.

This work was divided into three specific aims:

  1. Predicting locomotor adaptability from inter-trial correlations in baseline step-cadence and heart rate data through retrospective analyses of previously performed treadmill experiments.
  2. Predicting locomotor adaptability from baseline inter-trial correlations of step-cadence and heart rate during over-ground (non-treadmill) locomotion.
  3. Quantifying the consistency and adaptability of inter-trial correlations.
APPROACH:
The methodology is currently not available.

RESULTS:
Astronauts exhibit significant inter-subject variation in adaptation capabilities upon exposure to altered gravitational levels. Hence the ultimate goal of this project was to uncover individual predictors of sensorimotor adaptability for the purpose of guiding customized countermeasure development to facilitate rapid adaptation to novel sensorimotor challenges. These countermeasures could be in the form of preflight or inflight training prescriptions (e.g., exercises derived from clinical rehabilitation techniques), but would be tailored for each crewmember based on his or her sensorimotor signature revealed by the sensorimotor adaptability predictor. This would enable more efficient use of crew time and provide better outcomes. In the process of seeking these baseline predictors, we hoped to acquire valuable insight as to how motor learning is differentially or similarly accomplished across individuals. Such knowledge has benefits that extend beyond spaceflight research, including, for example, clinical research and rehabilitation techniques for patients with vestibular, cerebellar, and other sensorimotor pathologies. The ability to apply personalized medicine to such individuals would facilitate improved tolerance, enhanced compliance, and faster recoveries. Another significant aspect of this project is the consideration of behavioral performance metrics from two different physiological systems: sensorimotor (locomotor step cadence) and cardiovascular (heart rate). Conventional studies in motor learning typically emphasize the localized effects of a perturbation; their goal is to characterize the aftermath in the context of the response most directly affected. While by no means insignificant, analyzing such responses in isolation limits our understanding of the overall functional capacity of the individual and the collective adaptation process. While impractical to consider every physiological system in a single proposal, adding one non-sensorimotor parameter is the first step in considering these potential predictors more holistically. It is certainly plausible that because of the vast inter-connectedness within the human body, an adaptability predictor discovered in one system may in fact influence, and possibly even stem from, another system. Furthermore, one might posit that an adaptability predictor uncovered in one system might be a more global feature of that individual (e.g., an adaptability phenotype that describes an individual's propensity to retain versus rapidly forget information). For instance, if an individual exhibits strong inter-trial correlations in one system, is it possible that he or she also expresses strong inter-trial correlations in other systems? Such a finding would mean that characterizing baseline performance in one system would not only lead to predictions regarding adaptive capacities in that system, but also predictions about adaptive performance in other systems. By incorporating multiple systems into our study design, we can begin to explore how the CNS (central nervous system) might operate at a universal level. Additionally, with the increased trend toward autonomous, portable assessment devices using mobile technology, our tablet-based wireless motion sensors and heart rate monitor provide a convenient platform for beginning such cross-system investigations.

The initial phases of this project were carried out over a period of three months. The hardware and software necessary to collect over-ground gait data using wireless motion sensors were set up, the algorithms previously developed for analyzing inter-trial correlations in oculomotor data were adapted for step-cadence data, and baseline treadmill and over-ground step-cadence data were successfully collected and analyzed in several pilot test subjects. Retrospective analyses of inter-trial correlations of baseline step-cadence data from one previously performed treadmill adaptability training experiment were initiated.

Several key data observations have been made to-date. First, inter-trial correlations in baseline step-cadence data from the repeated treadmill and over-ground walking trials were consistent within each test subject. Second, for the previously performed treadmill experiment, the statistical nature of the baseline gait data required that multiple performance metrics be considered to describe inter-trial correlations; hence, new analytical techniques that were not employed in previous oculomotor experiments have been incorporated into this project's overall study design. Third, for the previously performed treadmill experiment, baseline inter-trial correlations were unique for each subject.
