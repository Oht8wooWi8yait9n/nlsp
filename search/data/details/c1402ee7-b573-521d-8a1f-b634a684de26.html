Physiological Parameters that Predict Orthostatic Intolerance After Spaceflight

Record ID: Aorta
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objective of this experiment was to predict orthostatic intolerance due to the space environment after return from spaceflight. Orthostatic intolerance is defined as the inability to stand for 10 minutes in the usual flight-medical test directly after return from Space.

APPROACH:
The AORTA experiment was not executed in space, but rather measured parameters of the astronauts before and after the flight. To make this prediction the astronauts/cosmonauts were tested before their spaceflight in an elaborate (3 hr) test protocol on a computerized tilt table, while (non-invasively) measuring blood pressure, ECG and brain blood flow (by ultrasound) as main physiological parameters. This protocol was designed to categorize the cardiovascular reaction pattern of a person and act as predictor for the outcome of the test. As test outcome, the normal stand test that is part of the medical check-up immediately after return was used. Astronauts were asked to stand relaxed, leaning against a wall or some other fixed object for a maximum of 10 minutes. Orthostatic intolerance is defined as the inability to stand for 10 minutes. The upright time before this test should be minimal, since hormonal re-adaptation to 1 G starts immediately after resumption of upright Earth-bound life.

In five cosmonauts, finger arterial pressure noninvasively in supine and upright positions was measuread. Preflight measurements were repeated using venous occlusion thigh cuffs to impede venous return and "trap" an increased blood volume in the lower extremities; postflight sessions were between 1 and 3 days after return from 10- to 11-day spaceflight. BaroReflex Sensitivity (BRS) was determined by spectral analysis and by PRVXBRS, a time-domain BRS computation method.

RESULTS:
Blood pressure (BP)-levels in space were not very much changed from preflight; the circadian BP-rhythm seemed dampened. Only daytime diastolic pressures (both subjects) and nighttime HR (one subject) were significantly lower in Space. However, compared to the effect of a control tilt manoeuvre on the ground, even lower BP values might have been expected. Striking were the BP- and HR-surges during the working days in Space, often related to stressful moments like live appearances on public TV. Systemic vascular resistance (SVR) dropped during the night, unlike HDT. Thus, actual spaceflight refuted our earlier findings in HDT both for BP-levels and for daytime to nighttime changes.
