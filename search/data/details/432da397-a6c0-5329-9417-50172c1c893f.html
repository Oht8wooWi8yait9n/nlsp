Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objective of this experiment was to determine the trends and magnitude of major body organ shifts in a weightless environment, including fluid distribution changes. In addition, variations in height measurement were noted.

Before the Space Shuttle program, Apollo astronauts reported difficulty in donning their space suits inflight and on the moon. The first documented changes in height during weightlessness were on Skylab 4 and ASTP. A series of inflight studies was proposed on the early Shuttle missions; while the magnitude of such changes was fairly well established, the time course was unknown.

APPROACH:
A plan was made to attempt to locate organs by palpation, auscultation and percussion, as well as noting external changes. The crewmember performed the usual manual physical examination procedures to define and outline locations of major organ systems preflight and on orbit. It was desired that each crewmember be examined before and during flight.

For height measurements on STS-8, a barefoot subject was positioned standing fully upright, back against a wall and exerting downward pressure with hands to ensure maintaining solid contact with the floor. A square jig was placed against the wall with its top just touching the subject's vertex, and an index mark was made on the wall. Preflight and postflight measurements were made in a similar fashion. Changes were measured to 1/16 of an inch. Measurements were made preflight during physical exam, inflight daily, and on landing day R+0. On STS-51D, height measurements were made using the precision gauge (+ 1mm) designed by Whitmore.

RESULTS:
There were many obvious changes in limbs, spine, and peripheral tissue. As to the locations of internal organs, auscultation was not only possible under the noise conditions in the Shuttle, but also very useful. Conversely, percussion was virtually impossible to perform. Palpation of tissues, especially of the head and neck, and observations of nasal and aural canals, provided new insight into fluid shifts and its effects. Audiometry results are mainly reported in DSO 410. The conclusion of this experiment was that physical observation can provide invaluable data in some areas, but visceral location will probably require additional aids.

Height: Results were consistent with Skylab 4 and ASTP; the intervertebral discs expand or compress with the changing load in a biphasic fashion. There is an immediate change in height with load, followed by a slower change with a time constant of hours. Regarding time course of height changes, an exponential increase in height under flight conditions was expected and seen within a duration of 10 hours. Changes as much as 1.5 inches were observed. Observations from the height studies resulted in adding an inch to the spacesuit torso length.
