Summary / Description

NASA's fifth Shuttle flight of 1994, STS-64, included two firsts, the crew performed atmospheric research using a laser and conducted robotic processing. The first untethered spacewalk in over ten years was also performed during this mission. The orbiter crew included Commander Richard N. Richards, Pilot L. Blaine Hammond, and Mission Specialists Jerry M. Lineger, Susan J. Helms, Carl J. Meade, and Mark C. Lee.

Primary payloads on STS-64 were the Light Detection and Ranging In-Space Technology Experiment that measured atmospheric parameters from a space platform utilizing laser sensors, the Robot Operated Materials Processing System that investigated robot handling of thin film samples and the Shuttle Pointed Autonomous Research Tool for Astronomy (SPARTAN-201). SPARTAN is a free-flying retrievable platform with two telescopes to study the solar wind, a continuous stream of electrons, heavy protons and heavy ions ejected from the sun and traveling through space at speeds of almost one million miles per hour. Solar wind frequently causes problems on Earth by disrupting navigation, communications and electrical power.

Research on the development and differentiation of a major food crop family that provides half of the world's calorie intake from plants was the subject of the second Biological Research in Canisters (BRIC-02) payload on STS-64. The BRIC-02 payload consisted of passive experiments contained within a metal canister placed in a middeck locker with foam padding for vibration dampening. BRIC experiment specimens were exposed to the temperatures, carbon dioxide, oxygen, atmospheric pressure and humidity conditions of the middeck; there was little to no crew interaction with the payload.

Additional life sciences experiments performed during the STS-64 mission were those classified as Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs). A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies are designed to require minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

Three Department of Defense sponsored experiments were also flown on the STS-64 mission, including the Air Force Maui Optical Site Calibration Test to test ground based optical sensors and examine plume phenomena using the Space Shuttle as a calibration target, Military Application of Ship Trails to examine the effects of ships on the marine environment, and the Radiation Monitoring Equipment III to measure radiation levels in the crew cabin throughout the flight.

Other experiments included the Solid Surface Combustion Experiment to determine how flames spread in microgravity and the Shuttle Amateur Radio Experiment-II (SAREX-II) to educate students in the United States and other countries about what it is like to live and work in space. Several Get Away Special (GAS) experiments submitted from universities, high schools and foreign countries were also flown. The Shuttle Plume Impingement Flight Experiment (SPIFEX) payload gathered plume information to assist in understanding the potential effects of thruster plumes on large space structures, such as the Mir Space Station and the International Space Station, during future Shuttle docking and rendezvous operations.

Mission Specialists Mark Lee and Carl Meade performed a 6.5 hour spacewalk on the eighth day of the mission to evaluate the Simplified Aid For EVA Rescue (SAFER) hardware, several extravehicular activity (EVA) tools, and an Electronic Cuff Checklist developed to allow spacewalkers greater and easier access to information. SAFER is a small, self-contained, propulsive backpack device that can provide free-flying mobility for a spacewalker in an emergency. It is designed for self-rescue use by a spacewalker in the event the Shuttle is unable to retrieve a detached, drifting crewmember.

The STS-64 mission concluded on September 20, 1994, as Discovery landed at Edwards Air Force Base in California.
