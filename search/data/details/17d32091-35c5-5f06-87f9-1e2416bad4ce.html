Impact of Age, Genetics Variants and High LET Track Structure on Mammary Cancer Risk Estimates

Record ID: NNJ13HA96I
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A key NASA objective is to obtain quantitative data defining the risk of carcinogenesis following galactic cosmic ray (GCR) exposure. Current radiation quality factors used to determine cancer risk for space radiation do not provide the accuracy needed, and debate exists as to the relevance of the experimental studies they’re based upon. To formulate better risk estimates, key data is needed including how age, genetic susceptibility, and radiation quality affect carcinogenic potential in irradiation sensitive tissues. Towards this aim, investigators used a novel resource of a large collection of normal mammary epithelial cell strains, from women aged 16 to 80, including many in the age of astronauts, to address these important questions. They obtained quantitative data regarding radiation quality effects; ROS and DNA damage signaling, radiation-induced centrosome defects, and enrichment of stem cell populations using this cohort of strains from various aged individuals. In addition, they also investigated genetic susceptibility, including how loss of p16 expression, a sporadic event in individual cells of normal women in vivo and often observed in breast tumors, affects radiation quality effects and centrosome aberration frequency. Additionally they analyzed strains containing a BRCA1 or an ATM heterozygote genetic background, representing ~0.32 and ~1% of the normal population respectively, to determine how these genetic variants may make them more sensitive to high LET exposures. These studies provide critical data, which can be used to model how genetic susceptibility, age, and radiation quality interplay in defining the cancer risk of mammary cells to high LET radiation.

The intent of this study was to determine how age, genetic susceptibility, and radiation quality modify the risk of high LET radiation-induced cancer. A cohort of mammary epithelial cell strains isolated from women of various age groups was used for these studies. Centrosome studies were performed using both a novel flow cytometry based assay, which provided a less subjective and more rapid way to define centrosome defects, as well as a standard microscopy-based method. Previous studies have revealed that changes in stem cell numbers are associated with an increased risk of cancer. Thus these studies assessed how radiation exposure and radiation quality affect stem cell populations, and whether age plays a role in the differences observed.

APPROACH:
Frozen vials of primary cells from eighteen individuals were obtained from an HMEC Bank at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), generated by Dr. Martha Stampfer. Early passage (p=2) cells were expanded and used for subsequent experiments. Approximately seventy p4 vials were frozen down from each individual’s strain to have sufficient cell stocks to perform all experiments in the study. At Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) cells were exposed to three different doses (0, 0.05, and 0.5 Gy) of cesium, Si 93, and Ti 300 MeV/u in triplicate. Each strain was cultured in a T25 or a T75 flask at BNL, exposed to radiation, and then one day post exposure, cells were either sent back live to LBNL or trypsinized and frozen down for analysis after long term culture post radiation exposure. At LBNL, cells were plated at seeding densities to enable analysis at 85% confluency at different time points post radiation exposure (3, 9, and 14 days).

These studies required extremely labor-intensive tissue culture, scoring for centrosome defects using microscopy, and flow cytometry determination of stem cell population changes. As a majority of studies to date typically defined genomic instability at early times post exposure (3 days) post exposure, investigators assessed centrosome aberrations at two later time points as well, weeks post exposure; these are time points likely more relevant and useful in modeling cancer risk. Their aim was to compare early effects at day three to the persistent effects of radiation exposure at longer times. They examined how centrosome levels are affected by the age of the individual the strain was derived from and the radiation quality of the exposure. They also defined the percentage of stem/progenitor cells in these various exposed populations at long times post exposure to define if radiation influences the growth of this population and how age may impact this endpoint. Furthermore, to gain additional information from this cohort of samples, the investigators also performed assays using a smaller subset to assess the phosphorylation profile of select proteins involved in DNA damage response and miRNA expression from various age groups following high or low LET radiation exposure.

RESULTS:
Genetic instability was quantified based on levels of centrosome aberrations. This work sought to address whether age was an important defining characteristic in describing the effectiveness of the various radiation quality exposures to induce centrosome aberrations. These studies revealed a significant dose dependent increase in centrosome aberrations. In assessing age effects, the investigators noted that the percentage of cells with supernumerary centrosomes increases linearly with age particularly when exposed to Ti as compared to low LET. For Ti, the slopes for low dose (LD) and high dose (HD) appear parallel, with high dose exhibiting an approximately 2 fold increase in centrosome aberrations relative to low dose, for the same age. The baseline for centrosome aberrations in unexposed cells shows a slight increase with age that is not significant. In evaluating the distribution of the number of centrosomes in aberrant cells, results indicate a linear increase in the proportion of cells with >3 centrosomes with both low and high doses of Ti exposure. Time course studies with assessments at day 3, 9, and 12 post exposure revealed that, in comparison to day 3 and day 9, there is a significant age dependent linear increase in the frequency of centrosome aberration at day 12. Interestingly, the fraction of cells exhibiting aberrant pATF2 foci post Ti exposure at Day12 decreased with increasing age, suggesting that the pattern of repair signaling is opposite of what is seen with centrosome aberrations. In summary, this work suggests both age and radiation quality affects the levels of radiation induced-genomic instability.

No datasets exist for the study. A final report was archived.
