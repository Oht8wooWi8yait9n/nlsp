Trace Chemical Contamination: Water Quality

Record ID: 5.3b
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A portion of the potable water supplied to the Russian cosmonauts, American astronauts and other occupants of the Russian Mir Space Station is produced by the direct recycle of water from humidity condensate. Additional supplies come from ground supplied potable water that is delivered on a Progress resupply spacecraft, or fuel cell water transferred from the Shuttle.

The objectives of the Trace Chemical Contamination: Water Quality experiment were: 1) to characterize the chemical composition of the ground-resupply water prior to launch and on orbit, 2) to evaluate the efficiency of onboard water processors and aid in the development of International Space Station (ISS) water processing and monitoring technology by characterizing the chemical composition of Mir recycled water, 3) to characterize the chemical composition of Mir humidity condensate and compare the Mir condensate to the Shuttle humidity condensate, which will support development of the water recycling and monitoring systems for the ISS, 4) to provide in-flight testing of water collection hardware developed for the ISS, and 5) to provide a better understanding of the interaction between atmospheric and water contaminants. Detailed analysis of reclaimed and other Mir supplied potable waters will confirm that the Mir water reclamation system and the ground-supplied water system deliver water that meets established ISS water quality requirements for crew consumption.

APPROACH:
During the Mir 18, Mir 19, and Mir 20 missions, water samples were collected and stored for return to the Earth for post flight analysis. A Water Sampling Kit (WSK) was used to collect water samples in-flight. During in-flight water sampling sessions on board the Mir Space Station, the WSK was first unstowed from its storage location. The water sampling kit contained disinfectant wipes, potable water samplers, waste bags, archival chemical sample bags and storage bags needed for water sampling. A prepackaged disinfectant wipe, soaked in a 1:250 solution of benzalkonium chloride in water, was retrieved from the kit and used to disinfect the Mir galley-hot, galley-cold, or SVO-ZV (ground supplied) water port. Next, a potable water sampler was connected to the port. A waste bag was then connected to the potable water sampler. Using the waste bag, 200 ml of water was collected and discarded. Next, 1000 ml of water was collected into the archival chemical sample bag. The archival chemical sample bag was placed in a self-sealing storage bag and stowed for return on the Shuttle. These procedures were used to collect galley-hot, galley-cold and ground-supplied (SVO-ZV) water for post flight chemical analysis. Following the collection of the chemical samples, microbiological samples were obtained for a separate experiment.

After the samples were recovered on the ground, they were distributed to the water analysis laboratories at the Johnson Space Center (JSC) in Houston and the Institute of Biomedical Problems (IBMP) in Moscow. Parameters tested at JSC included total carbon (total inorganic carbon, purgeable organic carbon, nonpurgeable organic carbon and total organic carbon), specific organics (alcohols, organic acids, semivolatiles, volatiles, nonvolatiles, formaldehyde, amines and carboxylates), turbidity, metals and silver. Parameters tested by IBMP included conductivity, pH, color, chemical oxygen demand, total solids, calcium, magnesium, total hardness and silver.

RESULTS:
Prior to the Shuttle-Mir Science Program, sample bags fabricated from a metal foil laminate polyethylene film were developed to collect humidity condensate samples in the Shuttle for post flight analysis. While adequate for humidity condensate analysis, it was found that the bag material contributed organics at sufficient levels to interfere with the analysis of potable water. Hence, the need for an alternate bag material was identified and sample bags were developed to meet this requirement. The first use of these Teflon® sample bags and other water collection hardware occurred during the Mir 18 mission. The water sampling equipment used to collect samples for post flight chemical analysis (and in-flight and post flight microbial analysis) performed as designed. However, Mir 18 samples leaked from the sample container seals, which required modification of the bag design for future use.

Three recycled water samples were collected during Mir 18 and three were collected during Mir 19. All parameters tested met the joint U.S./Russian water quality specifications except the NASA standards for turbidity, total phenols and total organic carbon. The U. S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) drinking water standard for methylene chloride was also exceeded. Although turbidity levels were not determined on Mir 18 samples, measurements on Mir 19 samples showed levels ranging from 0.95 NTU (Nephelometric Turbidity Unit) to 1.91 NTU. The NASA limit for turbidity is 1 NTU. Total organic carbon (TOC) levels in the reclaimed water also exceeded NASA spacecraft limits. TOC levels ranged from 1.3 to 6.7 mg/L compared to the NASA limit of 0.5 mg/L. These levels did not exceed the Russian water quality limit of 25 mg/L. Total phenol levels were exceeded in all three samples collected during Mir 19, although the Mir 18 samples had no detectable levels of phenol. The Mir 19 levels were 5.7 microgram/L, 5.9 microgram/L and 6.0 microgram/L; the standard for total phenols is 1 microgram/L. Finally, methylene chloride was detected in one Mir 18 sample above the U.S. EPA drinking water standards. The methylene chloride concentration of this sample was 92.85 microgram/L; the EPA standard for this compound is 5 microgram/L. At this time, there is evidence of possible sample contamination during storage of the samples in the Johnson Space Center (JSC) laboratory. Therefore, these levels of methylene chloride are unconfirmed.

Ground supplied water samples were collected prior to Mir 18 and in-flight during Mir 18 and Mir 19. Preflight samples were collected from RSC Energia in Kalingrad, Russia on January 19 and March 6, 1995. In-flight samples were taken on July 3 and November 17, 1995. Tap water from JSC was also collected for comparison. Turbidity levels of the preflight samples exceeded the Joint U.S./Russian water quality specification of 1 NTU. These samples had turbidity levels ranging from 7.19 to 7.43 NTU. One Mir 18 preflight sample also exceeded the Joint U.S./Russian water quality specification for silver, which is 0.5 mg/L. This sample had a silver concentration of 0.63 mg/L. Both preflight and in-flight ground supplied water samples exceeded the U.S. standard of 0.5 mg/L for TOC. TOC levels ranged from 6.56 mg/L to 9.30 mg/L in these samples. Also, methylene chloride and chloroform concentrations in some of the ground supplied water samples exceeded the current EPA maximum contaminant levels of 5 microgram/L for methylene chloride and 100 microgram/L for chloroform. At this time, NASA and the Russian Space Agency (RSA) do not have specifications for these compounds. Because of the possibility of sample contamination during laboratory storage, methylene chloride values measured are suspect.

Humidity condensate samples were collected during Mir 19 and Mir 20. One sample was collected during Mir 19 and four samples were collected during Mir 20. During Mir 18, one sample was reported to have been collected, but was not returned to Earth. Three of the Mir 20 samples were collected as a result of a 1,2-ethanediol (ethylene glycol) thermal coolant leak in the Kvant module of the Mir Space Station. To protect the health of the crew, the condensate generated between November 2 and November 16, 1995 was not recycled for drinking, but was routed into 3 collapsible 22-liter water tanks, known as EDVs. Instead of collecting 2 recycled water samples as planned, one condensate sample was collected from each of the 3 water tanks. Ionic species identified in these samples include chloride (0.321 - 1.120 mg/L), nitrate (2.09 - 9.30 mg/L), sulfate (0.237 - 1.01 mg/L), lithium (90.51 - 0.64 mg/L), sodium (1.25 - 9.38 mg/L), potassium (0.40 - 0.57 mg/L), ammonium (4.93 - 61.8 mg/L), magnesium (0.34 - 1.15 mg/L) and calcium (0.73 - 2.49 mg/L). Volatile organic compounds detected above method detection limits included acetaldehyde, acetone, 2-butanone, carbon disulfide, chloroform, diethyl ether, diazomethane, perfluoro-1,3-dimethylcyclohexanone, methylene chloride and o-xylene. Approximately sixty different semivolatile organic compounds were present in the Mir humidity condensate. Those detected at the highest levels included 3-t-butylphenol (70.6 - 195.8 microgram/L), n,n-dimethylformamide (nondetectable - 701.8 microgram/L), 2-ethoxyethanol (nondetectable - 147.7 microgram/L), 4-ethylmorpholine (66.1 - 166.7 microgram/L) and 2-hydroxybenzothiazole (nondetectable levels - 359.7 microgram/L). Methanol, 1-methoxy-2-propanol, 1,2-propanediol, formaldehyde and ethylene glycol were also detected above minimum detection limits in these samples. Ethylene glycol was nondetectable in the Mir 20 sample taken before the thermal coolant leak and was detected at levels of 149.2 microgram/L, 152.8 microgram/L and 76.8 microgram/L in samples collected after the leak.

As part of Detailed Supplementary Objective 317, humidity condensate samples were collected onboard the Shuttle during STS-45, STS-47 and STS-68 (these were not docking missions with the Mir). These results were averaged and compared to the Mir humidity condensate results to identify any potential interchanges of constituents between the Shuttle and Mir atmospheres during the docking of the Shuttle to the Mir, and to aid in the design of the International Space Station water processors. Approximately 60 compounds identified in the Shuttle condensate were not detected in the Mir condensate, and about 10 compounds found in the Mir condensate were not detected in the Shuttle condensate. Accountability of organic carbon was calculated by summing the concentration of organic carbon for each organic compound analyzed and comparing this result to the total organic carbon value. This comparison gives an indication of the theoretical yield of all organic compounds in the samples. The average organic carbon recovery from the Shuttle condensate samples was 72.0% compared to the Mir condensate average organic carbon recovery of 58.3%. Alcohol, organic acid, formaldehyde and caprolactam levels were significantly higher in the Shuttle condensate as compared to the Mir condensate. Those compounds present in larger quantities in the Mir condensate include ethylene glycol, 1,2-methoxy-2-propanol, acetaldehyde and acetone.

In conclusion, findings from the analysis of reclaimed water show that the water met all requirements of the joint U.S./Russian spacecraft water quality standards. It is noted, however, that the NASA requirements for TOC, turbidity and phenol were exceeded. In addition, the regenerated water did not meet U.S. EPA water quality standards for methylene chloride. The ground supplied water exceeded the NASA requirements for TOC and turbidity, and the U.S. EPA maximum contaminant levels for chloroform and methylene chloride. As noted, selected parameters were exceeded, but not to a degree which would adversely affect the potability of the water.

The Mir humidity condensate had significantly lower levels of alcohols and organic acids as compared to the Shuttle condensate. TOC levels were also much lower than levels previously measured in the Shuttle. Compounds detected in the Mir condensate, which were much higher than those measured in the Shuttle include acetaldehyde, acetone, ethylene glycol and 1,2-methyoxy-2-propanol. One source of 1,2-methoxy-2-propanol is from the Sharpie markers used in a number of experiments onboard Mir. Also, the high levels of ethylene glycol were due to a leak of this compound from a coolant loop in the Mir core module. The ability to collect humidity condensate was critical in planning the operational response to this leak.
