Oxidative Stress as a Mechanism for Altering Acute and Chronic Functional Changes in the CNS Exposed to Low Dose, Low Dose Rates, and Mixed Fields of Protons and HZE Nuclei

Record ID: NNX09AK25G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The space radiation environment poses unique hazards that include solar particle events and galactic cosmic rays. These types of radiations have been shown to cause acute and long-lasting chemical changes referred to as oxidative stress that can alter the normal physiology of the central nervous system (CNS). The chemical species produced can cause a wide range of effects in cells within the CNS that largely depend on the types of pathways they activate and the types of damage they produce. Proper functioning of the CNS depends on many factors, but is known to require subpopulations of neural stem and precursor cells that participate in the repair of CNS injury resulting from irradiation. This proposal focused on analyzing the effects of space radiation on these critical populations of multipotent neural stem and precursor cells. Investigators have developed the technology to isolate and grow these cells in culture and will use a variety of techniques to analyze how cells exposed to space radiation change. They planned to monitor the time course of oxidative stress in these cells and how that depends on the dose, the dose rate and type of radiation applied. They will determine how radiation-induced oxidative stress impacts cellular proliferation, signaling between cells, cell fate and survival. Collectively their studies using both rodent and human neural stem and precursor cells will help NASA understand the acute and longer-lasting sequelae associated with the exposure of the CNS to the space radiation environment. These studies will facilitate efforts to more appropriately establish estimates of risk for radiation exposure to the CNS in space.

RESULTS:
Past data collected by the investigators has demonstrated that the radiation response of multipotent neural precursor cells to x-rays, protons, and HZE nuclei involves an acute and persistent oxidative stress that can last for months. The importance of neural precursor cells to the repair and remodeling of the irradiated CNS is underscored by the data showing that the radiation-induced depletion of these cells is linked to the inhibition of neurogenesis and the onset of cognitive impairment. Investigators believe oxidative injury is critical in this context, and plays a contributory if not causal role in determining neural precursor cell function, survival and fate. A summary of the study findings are listed below.
1. Mice genetically manipulated to overexpress human catalase in the mitochondria were found to exhibit higher basal levels of neurogenesis and after low dose exposure to 150 MeV protons. These data suggest that interventions targeted to reduce oxidative stress in the mitochondria are neuroprotective.
2. A great deal of work characterizing the response of cultured neural stem cells from both rodents and humans were performed. These data reveal a marked predisposition of neural stem cells to exhibit radiation-induced oxidative stress. The functional importance of radiation-induced oxidative stress in regulating adaptive responses was also shown, as treatment of cells with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine eliminated the protective effect of prior exposure on cell survival following exposure to a subsequent higher dose of protons. Collectively, these data provide further evidence in support of the ides that space relevant fluences of charged particles elicit significant levels of oxidative stress that impact survival and proliferation.
3. Work characterizing the effect of HZE particle irradiation on cultured neural stem cells and the irradiated rodent brain was performed. There was a marked and persistent increases in oxidative stress found months after exposure to very low fluences of 600 MeV iron ions. Oxidative stress measurement made in vivo found that low dose exposure caused marked changes in the level and activity of several key antioxidant enzymes two weeks after exposure that returned to background one month after irradiation. Importantly, exposure to 5 or 30 cGy of 600 MeV oxygen or titanium ions was found to elicit significant deficits in novel object recognition 12 weeks after exposure. These data link key changes in redox metabolism to functional changes in cognition, changes that have important implications for astronauts exposed to the space radiation environment.
4. Investigators were able to assess the metabolic activity of neural stem cells after low dose irradiation using non-invasive 2-photon spectroscopy.
5. This is a comprehensive study that has defined the radiobiological response and consequences of human neural stem cells exposed to low LET radiation.
6. A review of the redox stress biology of irradiated neural stem cells and stem cells from other organ systems.

In summary, investigators have obtained significant data over the course of their funding period demonstrating that low dose charged particle irradiation can elicit oxidative stress in multipotent neural stem and precursor cells, derived from both rodents and humans. Their data shows conclusively that prior exposure elicits adaptive responses that are capable of attenuating oxidative stress and survival. Collectively their data suggest that radiation-induced oxidative stress constitutes a biochemical mechanism of functional significance to neural stem cell biology and cognition.
