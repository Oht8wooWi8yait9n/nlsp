Vestibular Experiments

Record ID: 1NS102
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The Vestibular Experiments were planned by a team of researchers from the US and Canada to study the causes of space motion sickness (SMS) and associated sensory-motor adaptations to weightlessness. SMS symptoms have been consistently reported in the Soviet space program and were also seen in the Apollo and Skylab crews. In addition, incidence among Shuttle crews has exceeded 50 percent. Space motion sickness is now recognized as a significant problem affecting astronaut performance, safety, and well-being. Symptoms generally appear soon after insertion into orbit and range from malaise and irritability to nausea and severe vomiting.

Many hypotheses attempt to explain the underlying causes of SMS and the majority of them focus on how the vestibular system is affected by microgravity. The vestibular system is responsible for our ability to sense changes in the speed and direction of bodily movement, even when our eyes are closed. We use information from the vestibular organs to walk, maintain an upright posture, and to see clearly as our head moves. The vestibular system consists of two similar vestibular organs located on either side of the head in the inner ear and it functions to maintain the body's balance by detecting the position and motion of the head. Each organ is made up of three semicircular canals and two otolithic organs which detect angular and linear accelerations, respectively. The otolithic organs depend on gravity to function properly; therefore, they are especially sensitive to microgravity conditions. The vestibular organs and the eyes work in conjunction to send signals to the brain, which then activates the appropriate muscles to keep the body balanced. According to the sensory-motor conflict theory, motion sickness results when incoming sensory signals to the brain no longer match expected patterns learned during previous sensory/motor experience. Exposure to microgravity affects the function of vestibular organs causing a mismatch in visual and vestibular signals which results in a sensory-motor conflict in the central nervous system (CNS). A good example of this occurs when a crewmember is floating weightless and upright in reference to the Shuttle's floor, but in reality he or she may be completely upside down due to microgravity. Accordingly, visual inputs in this situation would indicate the correct "up" and "down" orientation but the vestibular organs would not sense the correct orientation because they rely on gravity to function properly. Researchers believe that this sensory-motor conflict is the underlying mechanism responsible for SMS, illusions of motion, and spatial-orientation disturbances.

These investigations were aimed at assessing human vestibular function and visual responses in space during the 10 day Spacelab 1 mission. Each experiment examined a different motor output to reveal some aspect of the way the CNS adapts to the altered vestibular inputs resulting from exposure to microgravity. The sensory reinterpretation hypothesis which served as a basis for all the experiments was divided into three working hypotheses as follows: (1) that as a result of sensory-motor experience in microgravity, otolith afferent signals are centrally inhibited or reinterpreted as indicating head translation rather than tilt, (2) that longitudinal (z-axis) reflex sensitivity to "falling" is decreased, (3) that visual and tactile cues should play an increasing role in spatial orientation.

APPROACH:
Visual-Vestibular Interaction: The "Rotating Dome" experiment was designed to test the hypothesis that with increasing adaptation to weightlessness, crewmembers become increasingly dependent on visual and tactile cues as opposed to vestibular cues. The experiment hardware consists of a motor-driven rotating "dome" (drum) into which the subject’s head is placed. The visible surface of the dome was white and covered with randomly positioned colored dots. When the drum rotates, a sensation of self rotation in the direction opposite to that of drum rotation is felt. This tilting sensation causes a muscle reflex which attempts to turn the head back to a vertical position. Since the head is actually held stationary by a biteboard assembly, the neck torque produces counter rotation forces in the body. On Earth, the onset and strength of this sensation are thought to be inhibited by the lack of confirming motion signals from the vestibular organs. In microgravity, this inhibition should be reduced and thus researchers expected the crewmembers to feel a stronger sensation of self rotation.

Each experimental run consisted of a series of six trials. For each trial, the dome rotated at a constant angular velocity of 30, 45, or 60 deg/s clockwise or counterclockwise for a period of 50 seconds (s) followed by a 10 s stationary pause separating the trials. Two separate sequences of trials, each with randomized speed and direction, were utilized in the study. Crewmembers were instructed to deflect a rotary knob "joystick" to indicate the speed and direction of their own perceived self-motion. Ocular torsion was measured with a video camera installed in the center of the rotating dome. Body sway data was recorded using a video camera with a back view of the subject and the rotating dome. Inflight, the above experiment procedure was also performed with the crewmember constrained in a bungee-cord harness. The harness was employed to produce tactile and proprioceptive forces on the legs, trunk and feet that the subject would feel in a 1-G environment.

The "Rod and Frame" experiments also investigated visual-vestibular interaction by measuring static visual field dependence. The experiment tested the subject's ability to indicate the direction of the vertical as influenced by a tilted visual framework. The experiment was only performed pre- and postflight, since it's purpose was to determine if the increased dependence on static visual cues for orientation in space would carry over after flight. Subjects were seated in a chair in a dark room and viewed a dimly lit frame with sides 107 cm long by 2.5 cm wide surrounding a rod 99 cm long and 2.5 cm wide, located 180 cm from the subject. This frame was tilted to 28 degrees in either direction from the upright position. The subject told the experimenter to rotate the rod about a central axis until the subject judged it to be vertical. Angular deviation of the rod was measured by a potentiometer, and constituted the determination of field dependence. During each experimental session, four measurements were taken in each direction, as well as two control measurements without the frame.

Otolith-Spinal Reflex: These experiments focused on the neural link between the otolith organs and the motor neurons of the spinal cord which control limb muscle activity. These neural pathways control the otolith-spinal reflex which is responsible for preparing the leg muscles for a landing from a fall. The experiments were set up to assess changes in the otolith-spinal system during and after prolonged exposure to microgravity. Researchers wanted to investigate how the otolith-spinal system adapts to a new sensory-motor environment, and how it readapts to gravity after landing. The protocol for the "Hop and Drop" experiments was designed to examine calf muscle responses during unexpected "falls" to see if the otolith contribution is altered and determine if the time course of this alteration correlates with other otolith response measures.

Pre- and postflight falls were produced as the subject grasped a handle above his head, raised his feet, and then dropped to the floor as the handle was released. In the absence of gravity, the downward acceleration toward the Spacelab floor was provided by adjustable elastic cords which connected a torso harness to the Spacelab floor. A total of nine cords, grouped into 3 sets of 3, ran from the harness attachment points to the floor. When properly adjusted, they provided a downward force approximately equal to the subject's weight as he hung by his arms from the handle. By removing one elastic cord from each set the acceleration was reduced to 0.67 g, and by removing another from each set it was reduced further to 0.33 g. All data were collected in the first 150 milliseconds (ms) after release. The electromyographic (EMG) response was recorded from 2 surface electrodes located over the lower ends of the lateral and medial heads of the gastrocnemius of the left leg. A ground electrode was placed on the midline over the soleus 5-10 cm below the others. During inflight testing, head longitudinal acceleration was recorded using an accelerometer strapped to the subject's head and an electronics package worn at the waist. This signal was used to confirm both the times of onset of fall and of landing, and the magnitude of acceleration stimulus. Contact with the floor was detected by means of a ribbon switch attached under the subject's left foot. During pre- and postflight testing sessions, the onset of fall was detected by a microswitch located in the releasing device. Contact with the ground was determined using a series of ribbon switches located under a section of carpet upon which the subject landed. All test sessions included 45 falls, with a stimulus of 0.2 g for the first 15, 0.5 g for the next 15 and 1.0 g for the final 15. To achieve stimuli of less than 1.0 g during pre- and postflight testing, the subject wore a parachute harness which was attached to counterweights via a cable and pulley system.

Awareness of Orientation and Limb Position: Many astronauts have reported that they lose their sense of "up" and "down" orientation with respect to the vehicle when their eyes are closed or when the lights are turned off. Researchers wanted to investigate this problem further by examining the role of limb position awareness in this orientation disturbance. On Earth, the central nervous system uses tactile and proprioceptive cues from the limbs to factor in the effect of gravity on the "up" and "down" orientation process. However, in microgravity the tactile and proprioceptive cues from the limbs may be altered, and researchers wanted to investigate the origins of the loss of spatial orientation. More specifically, they examined whether the disorientation occurs immediately after closing the eyes or whether it requires some time for the memory of orientation to "wash out". They also examined whether the loss of limb position awareness is caused by microgravity or sleep periods. Experiments were conducted in the Spacelab module in both a loosely and tightly tethered condition to control for the effects of tactile forces. Experiments were also conducted in the Orbiter middeck bunk area in order to make observations after sleep. Subjects were instructed to view the location of several different target objects placed on nearby walls, were blindfolded, and were then asked to describe the position of their limbs and to point accurately to the targets. Different intervals of time were measured between the time of blindfolding and the time of describing the position of their limbs.

Posture Control: Researchers have found that exposure to microgravity causes postural difficulties both during microgravity and for several days postflight. The difficulties may be due to the changes in central processing of vestibular information that occur during prolonged exposure to microgravity. This study was designed to identify changes in electromyographic (EMG) latency and amplitudes that might explain the postural instabilities observed postflight. These experiments were only conducted before and after the 10 day Spacelab 1 mission. The "Posture Platform" experiments used a pneumatically driven platform to initiate changes in standing posture. Subjects wore hard soled shoes and were instructed to stand on the platform with their eyes open (or closed) facing a wall with head erect and legs straight. As the platform is suddenly tilted proprioceptive reflexes that normally stabilize posture are inappropriate to maintain stability. Therefore, the body relies on other sensory systems such as the vestibular organs to compensate for the disturbance in posture. Prior to testing, surface electrodes were placed over the tibialis anterior (TA) and the gastrocnemius-soleus (G-S) muscles of the left leg. The raw EMG signals from the ankle flexors (TA) and ankle extensors (G-S) were sampled for 10 s at 200 samples per second by a microcomputer that also controlled the movement of the platform. Six trials were performed consisting of a randomized set of three up and three down tilts of the platform with the eyes open, followed by a different randomized set of six trials with eyes closed. The EMG signals were later analyzed for latency, amplitude, and frequency of oscillation of the late, post 500 millisecond, response.

The "Sharpened Romberg" test is a standardized procedure for measuring standing stability and posture. This test was also used as a method of evaluating the postflight postural instabilities of the Spacelab 1 crew. The experiments used two long rails (one narrow and one wide) mounted on wooden beams to conduct four different tests. (1) Narrow rail walking: The subject walked with arms folded in front, heel to toe, along the narrow rail for a maximum of six steps. (2) Narrow rail standing: The subject stood, eyes open, with arms folded, in the heel to toe position on the narrow rail for a maximum of 60 s per trial. (3) Wide rail standing/eyes closed: The subject stood heel to toe on the wide rail and when stable, closed his eyes. The position was maintained for a maximum of 60 s. (4) Wide rail standing/eyes open: The subject stood heel to toe on the wide rail for a maximum of 60 s with eyes open. All trials were terminated if the subject unfolded his arms or placed a foot on the floor. The operator measured the duration of the test or, in the case of the narrow rail walking test, the number of steps. Individual scores for each subject were the sum of the best three out of five trials for that test.

Motion Sickness Susceptibility: The motion sickness susceptibility investigation involved a head movement/symptom monitoring experiment and pre- and postflight susceptibility tests which were designed to systematically collect data on the origins, prediction, symptoms, and prevention of the disorder. The design of the experiments placed emphasis on resolving the issue: is space sickness another form of motion sickness? In order to document the relationship between head movement and symptom intensity two subjects wore the Acceleration Recording Unit (ARU). The system consisted of 3 angular and 3 linear miniature accelerometers mounted on a rectangular metal case. The package was held on the back of the head by a velcro cloth brow band so that it moved with the head during all movements. Subjects were asked to don the ARU as soon as possible after reaching orbit and to wear it as much as possible during their waking hours. To obtain frequent reports for correlation with head movement data, subjects were asked to make brief reports consisting of Mission Elapsed Time and a numerical estimate of "overall discomfort". The overall discomfort scale was based on a midrange value of 10 which was chosen as the marker for the symptoms felt when one was halfway to vomiting. Subjects also used pocket voice recorders and a multi-item symptom checklist to record observations on symptoms as they were noticed and as time permitted. In addition to symptom monitoring, two subjects were scheduled to make provocative head movements to explore the influence of eye closure and axis of head movement. The protocol consisted of a "Susceptibility Test" and a "Symptom Comparison Test" which were scheduled for the end of the working day on Mission Days 0, 3, and 8. If the subject was asymptomatic to start the test, the subject would strap into a seat and make 7 forehead to knee head movements, then rest for 10 s while making a checklist symptom report, and then repeat until the first symptom occurred or 5 min elapsed. If symptoms were present at the start, the Susceptibility Test would be skipped, and the subject performed only the Symptom Comparison Test. This approach was used because the Comparison Test protocol required a slightly symptomatic subject so that the stimulus/response relationship would be immediately obvious. Subjects first made up to 7 forehead to knee movements with eyes closed, and then repeated this with eyes open, and ranked the two conditions in terms of provocativeness. After a pause for recovery, subjects were then to make 90 degree head movements (eyes open) for 20 s periods successively in pitch, yaw, and roll, and then rank these movements also.

Vestibular Reactions to Lateral Acceleration: Linear acceleration is a stimulus of prime importance in the investigation of the effects of microgravity on vestibular function. Researchers have found that exposure to microgravity alters otolith function during and after space flight. These pre- and postflight experiments were designed to assess otolith adaptation after space flight by exposing the subjects to transient linear acceleration. Preflight tests established a baseline for each test subject and each test. Sled test sessions were conducted 120, 64, 43, and 10 days before the mission. Postflight measurements were made during the period of readaptation following landing. Postflight testing sessions were conducted on landing day three to five hours after landing for two of the crewmen and on all four crewmen 1, 2, 4, and 6 days after landing.

The subject was restrained in a cushioned aluminum chair by a five-strap harness. The head was restrained with an instrumented foam lined helmet. The helmet provided a means for measuring acceleration at the subject's head, eye movement by electro-oculography (EOG), and attachment of a Nikon F-3 camera to take pictures of both eyes. A biteboard carrying a fixed reference in the view of the camera enabled measurement of and correction for any residual head movement within the helmet. Four tests with the U.S. Laboratory Sled were performed using lateral upright (y-axis) acceleration: (1) perception of motion, (2) closed loop otolith assessment test (CLOAT), (3) dynamic ocular torsion, and (4) horizontal eye deviations.

(1) Perception of linear motion: This test measured a subject's ability to detect the presence and direction of small linear accelerations. The cart was positioned at the center or end of the track and a step acceleration was then applied to the cart. A step deceleration was applied after the cart had traveled one quarter of the track. Thirteen step accelerations ranging from 0.001 g to 0.08 g were presented in a random order, once in each direction for each acceleration level. When the subject detected his direction of acceleration, he indicated this by displacing the hand-held joystick in the direction of subjective acceleration. For a given trial of thirteen levels presented randomly, the threshold was defined to be the lowest acceleration level for which the subject correctly detects three out of four runs at that level and above.

(2) CLOAT: This test measured the subject's ability to use linear acceleration cues to perform a manual control task. During CLOAT the subject made use of otolith sensory information in a closed loop sensorimotor nulling task where the motor function (hand manipulation of a joystick) was presumed relatively unaffected by exposure to microgravity. The subject was in control of sled velocity using a joystick. A disturbance motion was generated by the computer and combined with the subject's control. The disturbance consisted of a random appearing velocity profile made up of 12 logarithmically spaced sinusoids added together lasting 82 s. The subject's task was to try to nullify the disturbance motion by holding the cart still, thereby closing the loop. CLOAT was performed once in the light and three times in the dark for each test session. The resultant sled velocity profiles in the dark were averaged in the frequency domain before further analysis was performed.

(3) Dynamic ocular torsion: This test isolated the gravitoinertial contribution to dynamic ocular counterrolling. The inability to distinguish between gravitational and inertial forces results in an ocular torsion response to lateral acceleration with the head upright. In this case, the gravitoinertial force vector, rather than the pure gravitational force vector, is rotated with respect to the head. The orientation of the head remains fixed and no rotational accelerations impinge on the head. Sinusoidal oscillation of amplitude 0.61 g was used at two frequencies, 0.42 Hz and 0.83 Hz, for 12 and 15 cycles, respectively. Torsional eye position was obtained by correlation and trigonometric analysis of eye and head position in photographs obtained with a camera. Photographs were taken at a rate of 2.5 frames per second. Eye torsional position was derived from two landmarks obtained from either naturally occurring landmarks in the iris or marked contact lenses adhered to the eye by a drop of distilled water. Head roll position was obtained from fiducial marks on extensions of a dental biteboard which appeared in the field of view of the film frame.

(4) Lateral eye deviations: This experiment complemented the ocular torsion study and involved measurement of the vertical and horizontal axes of eye movement during linear acceleration along all three body axes. Eye movements were recorded via electro-oculography (EOG) using surface electrodes placed around the eyes and preamplifiers built in to the sled helmet. Horizontal eye deviation and ocular torsion tests were performed with the subject facing sideways to the direction of the motion stimulus and fixating on an imaginary point on the laboratory wall. Vertical eye movement and nystagmus tests were performed with the subject seated and pointed in the direction of motion or lying down with his face to the ceiling. The subject was asked to indicate the direction of illusory subjective rotation, if any, verbally and with the Sled joystick.

Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR) and Nystagmus Dumping: These experiments were designed to test how the semicircular canals and otoliths function to control compensatory eye movements and nystagmus during body rotation about the vertical axis. Specifically, the researchers wanted to investigate how otolith signals possibly override semicircular canal signals to create postrotational nystagmus "dumping" in subjects who are rotated about the vertical axis and suddenly stopped. Investigators predicted that the dumping effect would be absent in microgravity due to altered otolith function. The subject was seated in the tubular frame seat which was mounted to the Spacelab floor on a pivot, and manually rotated by a second crewmember . Horizontal and vertical eye movements and head angular acceleration were measured and recorded by a belt mounted data recording system worn by the subject. Eye movements were measured with bitemporal EOG electrodes. The subjects eyes were covered but remained open. They were instructed to look straight ahead. In each session, the subject was rotated clockwise about a vertical axis for 1 min at 120 deg/sec, stopped, and postrotational VOR was recorded for 1 min. This procedure was repeated 3 times, alternating rotation directions. In the first two tests the subject maintained the head erect position throughout. In tests 3 and 4 (dumping tests), the subject pitched his head forward 90 degrees 5 seconds after chair rotation stopped, maintained this position for 5 seconds, and then resumed the head upright position. Nystagmus slow phase velocity (SPV) was then calculated. Preflight tests were conducted 151, 120, 65, 43, and 10 days prior to launch. Postflight data was obtained on days 1 and 2 after landing.

RESULTS:
Visual-Vestibular Interaction (Rotating Dome): Subjective Reports and Vection (Visually Induced Motion Sensation) indications: All subjects' comments indicated an increased visual dependence for orientation during flight, with an enhanced effect late in flight. Large individual differences in preflight sensations were also reflected during the inflight tests. Localized tactile cues reduced the visual effect in various ways early in the flight, but had less of an effect late in flight. All subjects reported stronger and more rapidly occurring vection when they experimented free of the biteboard in front of the rotating dome.

Visual-Vestibular Interaction (Rotating Dome)- Vection Indications: To measure the effects of weightlessness on the sensation of vection, statistical tests of the time from the start of dome rotation to onset of vection were performed. For each subject, each preflight dome run was compared with each inflight free-floating dome run. Despite the uniform comments that the bungee-cord-induced localized tactile cues inhibited vection in flight, neither average vection intensity nor average onset latency supported the subjective reports with statistically significant results. For seven of the eleven inflight comparisons, the average onset latency for a six trial dome run was longer when tactile cues were applied. Only 27 of the 47 individual trial comparisons produced weaker average vection intensity with tactile cues than for the free-floating conditions. When all trials were combined for each subject in a given test run, the variability in vection onset time became too large to permit statistically significant conclusions. However, the inflight mean onset times were generally lower than preflight, as were the postflight onset times.

Visual-Vestibular Interaction (Rotating Dome)- Body Sway: Rear-view video images of the subjects during all the flight experiments revealed considerable body sway only for one subject during the free-float dome run on the seventh day of flight.

Visual-Vestibular Interaction (Rod and Frame): Three of the subjects were field independent, with average deviations of the rod toward the frame of less than six degrees. The raw rod and frame data show high variability, but there is a trend toward increased field dependence which lasts for several days postflight. Each of the four subjects showed an increase in visual field dependence following the flight.

Otolith-Spinal Reflex - Response to Sudden Falls: All four crewmembers were tested before and after flight, but only two subjects were able to participate during flight and on landing day. One subject was tested on mission days (MD) 0, 1, and 6, and the other subject on MD 0, 1, 6, and 7. EMG responses were recorded in 200 ms increments, with 25 ms shown before the moment of release and 175 ms after. The early EMG burst actually consisted of two sub-components. The latency which marked the dividing line between the first and second sub-components varied between subjects, but was somewhat constant for individual subjects.

Otolith-Spinal Reflex - Two Subjects Tested Pre-, In-, and Postflight: Sub-component 1 showed similar results for both subjects. In nearly every case, the size of the response increased with the strength of the acceleration stimulus. There was no apparent change of the response postflight relative to preflight. Sub-component 1 decreased significantly immediately upon reaching orbit, and declined further during the flight, with most of the latter reduction occurring between MD 0 and 1. After MD 0, stimuli of 0.33 g and 0.67 g were close to or below threshold for eliciting a response to sudden falls. Sub-component 2 showed a different pattern of behavior. Except for inflight, the response did not usually increase with the acceleration stimulus. Postflight, a stimulus of 0.2 g tended to elicit a smaller response than the same stimulus preflight. Inflight, each subject seemed to adopt a different strategy. Sub-component 2 in subject A decreased upon entering weightlessness, and then changed little for the remainder of the flight. In subject B, however, sub-component 2 resulting from a 1-g stimulus increased dramatically, whereas those resulting from 0.33 g and 0.67 g stimuli were decreased on MD 0. All three continued to decline throughout the flight.

Otolith-Spinal Reflex - Four Subjects Tested Pre- and Postflight: The results from subjects A and B were combined with results from subjects C and D to extend the data analysis to four subjects. However, because of extreme variability of sub-component 2 between subjects, only sub-component 1 results are presented. No significant trends were noted in the data pre- or postflight, nor was there a significant difference postflight compared to preflight. This indicates that readaptation of the otolith-spinal system under study is a very rapid process, occurring much quicker than the original adaptation to microgravity.

Awareness of Orientation and Limb Position: In the absence of usable graviceptor information regarding body orientation in weightlessness, the nervous system must pay increased attention to the remaining sensory orientation signals. Subjective reports from crewmembers indicate large variations in individual styles, but never a prolonged sense of absence of a reference frame or disorientation.

Posture Control: Due to the variability of the responses, a better understanding of the relationship between pre- and postflight EMG activity was achieved by examining individual responses rather than averaged data. EMG activity of subject B during the first tilt up trials with eyes open on test days L-10, R+0, R+1, and R+6 was analyzed. The eyes open (EO) tilt up responses of the TA muscle showed small changes between the responses on the last preflight test, L-10, and postflight R+6. On R+0 and R+1, the first response to a tilt up had a larger initial response. However, no consistent pattern of change in the initial response amplitudes within or across the subjects was found. The G-S responses were markedly different than the TA response pattern. The late responses had generally the same shape, being characterized by one or two peaks following the initial peak with most of the response remaining flat for the remainder of the period. The eyes closed (EC) tilt up trials qualitatively showed postflight changes primarily in the late response. The initial peak changed very little from the preflight level. The late response on R+0 and R+1 showed several large and prolonged periods of EMG activity in the TA and G-S muscles as the subject fought to maintain balance. Successive EC tilt up responses from subject A on L-10, R+0, and R+1 show clear changes between preflight and R+0 data with less distinction on R+1. The increased amplitudes throughout the recording session clearly separated preflight from R+0 EMG responses in subjects A and B. The tilt down trial responses changed from preflight to postflight. The EO and EC responses on L-10 and R+6 showed a similar pattern of TA and G-S activity. The postflight data on R+0 and R+1 showed higher TA activity than that found preflight or on R+6.

Motion Sickness Susceptibility: Three of the four crewmembers reported persistent symptoms which were proportional to activity level. These three crewmen also vomited during the first or second day of the mission. Head movements on all axes were provocative, particularly in pitch and roll. ARU amplitude distributions of head angular and linear acceleration were computed over successive 15-minute intervals. These data showed that both crewmen who wore the ARU were compelled to limit their head movements when symptoms appeared after several hours of physical activity in orbit. However, after several days in orbit symptoms subsided, and head accelerations increased in magnitude and variability. Static visual cues were identified as a second major stimulus in space sickness. Difficulties in identifying the subjective floor, walls, and ceiling using static visual information were noted. Sudden changes in subjective self-orientation were occasionally experienced due to reinterpretation of available visual cues. The inflight symptom pattern was generally the same as the preflight pattern, except that facial pallor and cold sweating were usually not seen. Medication (0.5 mg scopolamine/2.5 mg dexedrine or 25 mg promethazine/25 mg ephedrine) was taken by all crewmembers and was judged helpful, with minimal side effects.

Vestibular Reactions to Lateral Acceleration: Perception of Linear Motion: The three parameters measured were threshold acceleration, velocity constant, and a regression coefficient associated with determining the velocity constant. Eight preflight trials were conducted with subject A and six trials with each of subjects B, C, and D. In general, preflight measures showed fairly stable measures of velocity constant. Preflight measures of threshold were more variable than velocity constants on a trial by trial basis. The lowest threshold obtained from among the trials in a particular session was used as the indicator of threshold for that day. Preflight thresholds were in the range of 0.002 g to 0.004 g. Postflight, measures of both velocity constant and threshold tended to be more variable than the preflight baseline.

Vestibular Reactions to Lateral Acceleration - CLOAT: Each of the four crewmen tended to have his own control strategy which developed in the early preflight sessions. Performance was determined by the scalar performance measure (SPM), a single parameter value derived in the frequency domain. Performance stabilized in the late preflight sessions for subjects A and B. For each crewmember, performance in the dark and in the light improved over the first three preflight sessions. For CLOAT in the light, little change was exhibited between pre- and postflight.

Vestibular Reactions to Lateral Acceleration: Dynamic Ocular Torsion: Only pre- and postflight data are available from subjects A and B due to inflight equipment failure. At each test session the folded torsion measurements were correlated to the sinusoidal stimulus frequency. However, the fitted sinusoidal amplitude showed high variance from one preflight session to the next in the same subject. In each case, the first measurement postflight had an amplitude smaller than or equal to three of the four preflight measurements in the same subject at the same frequency. Subsequent amplitude measurements postflight increased for both subjects at both frequencies.

Vestibular reactions to Lateral Acceleration - Lateral Eye Deviations: No results available.

Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex Nystagmus Dumping: Head erect post-rotatory VOR gain was unchanged in the pre- and postflight measurements. A shortening of the apparent time constant of post-rotatory slow phase velocity decay was observed in 3 of the 4 subjects. Increased decay was statistically significant 6-20 seconds after chair stop. Four days postflight, the group time constant had recovered to preflight norms.
