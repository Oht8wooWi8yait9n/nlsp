Exosomes and Secretory Factors as Mediators of Non-targeted Effects of HZE Particles

Record ID: NNX15AD63G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This is a proposal devoted to understanding the mechanism of radiation-induced non-targeted effects, and in particular, how they lead to genomic instability in bystander cells (those not directly traversed by a radiation track). Once a bystander cell has become unstable, it is susceptible to mutations induced by endogenous and exogenous stressors, which in turn creates a permissive environment for acquisition of oncogenic mutations. Non-targeted effects (NTE) are a crucial mechanism contributing to solid tumor carcinogenesis in the space environment, yet in many respects they remain poorly understood. Investigators hypothesize that NTE signaling leads to reliance on low-fidelity repair pathways in bystander cells; that the NTE signal is transmitted by exosomes, soluble secretory proteins, or both; and that the NTE signal affects repair through changes in the proteome and transcriptome. To test these hypotheses, investigators propose three specific aims.

Aim 1 is to determine effects of radiation quality, dose, and dose rate on genomic instability in bystander cells, as measured by use of low-fidelity repair pathways. Experiments will focus on range of energies for intermediate Z particles. Experiments will use human bronchial epithelial cells, believed to be the target for lung carcinogenesis and bladder cancer cells, used as a positive control because they are known to generate strong non-targeted effect signals.
Aim 2 is to determine the non-targeted effect signaling mechanism, with a focus on exosomes and secreted soluble proteins as candidates.
Aim 3 is to investigate the exact mechanism by which NTE signaling produces genome instability in bystander cells.

APPROACH:
Human cells will be irradiated and co-cultured with I-SceI-expressing reporter cells at intervals up to 21 days post-irradiation. Assays will be developed to measure the relative efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9-induced inversion in a number of candidate cell lines.

RESULTS:
This study is in progress. However, preliminary results are available.

Establishment of a new assay system for repair fidelity, based on mis-joining of double-strand breaks in endogenous genes (rather than the artificial reporter genes used in earlier work):
The assay uses the CRISPR/Cas9 nuclease to induce a paracentric inversion involving the EML4 and ALK genes. This is the most commonly occurring rearrangement in lung cancer patients and is particularly frequent in never-smokers, the population of most interest to NASA. A sensitive and quantitative qPCR assay was established to measure the relative efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9-induced inversion in a number of candidate cell lines. Three of these cell lines were chosen for radiation studies: near-normal human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEC3-KT); HZE radiation-transformed HBEC3-KT cells (HBECK3KT-F25F); and, as a positive control, the human bladder cancer cells (DsR-7F4) used in previous work by the investigators.

Irradiation of a panel of human cell lines in the NASA Space Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) 15C run:
To define radiation quality effects, 16-O, 28-Si, and 48-Ti ions were used at energies calculated to provide linear energy transfer (LET) values of 100 and 200 keV/µm. These represent a range of species that might be encountered within a shielded crew habitat. Irradiation was performed at doses ranging from 0.2 to 1.0 Gy. Initial results showed that radiation led to a modest, dose-dependent reduction in cell growth rate, as expected. At day seven post-irradiation, the directly irradiated and bystander cell populations were challenged with CRISPR/Cas9 nuclease to induce targeted strand breaks in the EML4 and ALK loci. Molecular analysis of the relative frequency of inversions in different treatment groups is in progress.

Establishment of methods for analysis of radiation-induced exosomes, including light scattering and electron microscopy to determine the size distribution, immunoblotting, and mass spectrometry to identify exosomal proteins, and fluorescence labeling to allow visualization of uptake in bystander cells:
Irradiated cell conditioned medium was also collected from 48-Ti-irradiated cells in the NSRL (15A and 15C runs). This conditioned medium will be used to prepare exosomes for proteomic analysis.
