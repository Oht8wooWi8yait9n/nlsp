Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
As with the Gemini, Apollo and Skylab missions, the primary purpose of this study on the Apollo-Soyuz mission was to provide hematologic and immunologic data for the assessment of crew health preflight and postflight. A second objective was to evaluate the influence of space flight on the immunohematologic system. Supporting data and sample logistics were provided for the Cellular Immune Response and the Polymorphonuclear Leukocyte Response studies.

APPROACH:
Blood samples were collected by venipuncture from the crewmen on L-30 (30 days preflight), L-15 and L-5, R+0 (recovery day) and R+1 (1 day postflight), R+2, R+4, R+8, R+13, and R+29. In addition, identical samples were collected from the backup crewmen and three ground-based control subjects preflight and during the mission.

Radioisotope studies using chromium 51 (Cr51) and iodine 125 (I 125) to determine red cell mass and plasma volume, respectively, were conducted on L-15, R+0, and R+28.

RESULTS:
Varying degrees of hemodilution were evidenced in all three crewmen postflight. A slight reduction in red blood cell count was found postflight. Hematocrit and hemoglobin were slightly decreased at R+0 and decreased further 10-15% by R+3. These changes were the result of shifts in plasma volume inflight and postflight and are similar to those observed in previous manned space flights.

Methemoglobin increased to 4.2% at R+0 compared to 1.3 % at preflight. By R+1, it had dropped to a mean value of 2.0%. This transient rise was most likely caused by crew exposure to nitrogen tetroxide (N2O4) vapors during reentry.

There was a decrease in lymphocyte percentage postflight, but absolute lymphocyte count did not change. White blood cell count was significantly increased postflight due to increased absolute neutrophil count. The elevated neutrophil levels persisted and even showed a further increase in two of the crewmen. This continued elevation was possibly related to the resultant pulmonary damage and subsequent steroid therapy due to N2O4 exposure.

The reticulocyte counts were unchanged immediately postflight. There are no data on reticulocyte counts during the subsequent 2-week period, when the red cell mass was being replaced. However, based on previous data, only a modest response would be expected. Classification of reticulocytes, based on density of the residual intracellular ribosomal network, indicated no significant shift in the age distribution of the reticulocytes on R+0 or R+1.

Red cell mass decreases were observed immediately postflight in the two crewmembers tested. The mean decrease of 8% was less than the 10% decrease from Apollo 14-17. The change noted in one crewman was 2.5 times higher than the other crewman. By R+28, red cell mass was recovering and was decreased only 3% from preflight. Red cell survival was slightly decreased in one crewman. Plasma volume showed an 11% decrease which was greater than the mean 4.4% decrease seen in Apollo 14-17. It is possible that exposure to nitrogen tetroxide partially accounted for the greater decrease in plasma volume after this mission than in previous missions. The plasma volume of both crewmembers had returned to near preflight values by R+28. The plasma volume loss produced higher peripheral hematocrits obtained shortly after recovery (44%) than were obtained preflight (41%).

Individuals showed varied immunologic responses. No significant plasma protein abnormalities were found preflight or postflight. Total serum protein levels showed some postflight variation that was probably due to the changing plasma volume. Serum protein electrophoretic patterns were normal, except for a low albumin value on R+28.

There was a 48% elevation in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) on R+0. The distribution of LDH isoenymes did not change drastically, but there were slight elevations in the fifth band. The diagnostic significance of this small elevation in the fifth band was undetermined. Lipoprotein fractions were normal.

The most significant alteration in serum protein quantitation was a postflight elevation in the haptoglobin concentration. This change was observed frequently in crewmen returning from the earlier Apollo missions. It may have reflected an acute phase protein response, perhaps caused by tissue destruction. Because of exposure to N2O4 during recovery and subsequent lung tissue damage, it is impossible to identify the specific effects of space flight on the haptoglobin levels in this crew. The fact that the maximum values were observed 3 to 4 days after recovery is also suggestive of a response to the toxic gas rather than to weightlessness.

No changes in immunoglobins (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD) were observed, with the exception of a reduction in the IgG levels in one crewman. His postflight concentration of 909 mg/100ml of serum was within the normal range but was 34% below his preflight mean.

A significant shift occurred in red cell morphology postflight. Discocytes dropped 28% and remained below the preflight mean throughout the postflight examination period. There were significantly more stomatocytes, spherocytes, and knizocytes postflight. There was also evidence of cell fragmentation and increased echinocytes. The implications of these data are not clear, but it would appear that morphology was aggravated by exposure to the space environment or N2O4.

The C-reactive protein was negative at R+0 but strongly positive on R+1. This change probably reflects the tissue destruction observed in the crew on R+1.

Iron turnover was not determined postflight. Spleen-liver ratios showed no rise in the crewmembers, but did in the controls. This is evidence against splenic trapping as the cause of the decreased red cell mass.
