Foot Reaction Forces During Space Flight

Record ID: 96-E318
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The loss of bone mineral in the lower extremities is widely viewed as the critical factor that may limit long term human habitation of space. Decrements in muscle funciton as a result of prologned exposure to microgravity also have important implications for performance and safetly during space missions. Lower extremity loading during daily life in microgravity is thought to be markedly reduced compared to 1-G; although, no measurements have yet been made to quantify the load reduction. Recent studies have shown that as much as 1.58% per month of bone mineral is lost from the proximal femur during flights lasting from four to fourteen months and that more than 20% of knee extensor strength is lost in flights lasting 60 to 80 days. This study was performed in attempt to quantify mechanical load during periods of time that represent typical activities of daily life both on Earth and during a mission on the International Space Station (ISS).

The objective of the FOOT experiment was to quantify and explore the relationship between loading of the human body and changes in the homeostasis of the musculoskeletal system during space flight compared to Earth.

Another objective was to study the foot-ground reaction forces, electromyographic signals and joint movements at the hip, knee and ankle during twelve hour periods over several days of daily activities on Earth on ISS; and to examine postflight changes in strength, bone mineral density (BMD), and muscle volume.

APPROACH:
Hardware designed for ambulatory data collection on the Human Research Facility (HRF) was used to collect ground reaction force, electromyography (EMG), and three lower extremity joint angles (right hip, knee, and ankle) during full work days. Daily mechanical load stimulus was calculated using two competing theories and daily neural drive was estimated from the measurements obtained. Dual Energy X-Ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scans of the proximal femur, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the leg muscles and maximum ankle, knee and hip joint torques were taken preflight and postflight to quantify any bone density, muscle volume and muscle strength changes that occurred during the mission.

The human body is designed to bear weight. Without the stimulation caused by placing weight on lower extremities, whether due to the microgravity environment or lack of use on Earth, bones lose mass and muscles lose strength. The Foot experiment characterized the load placed on lower extremities during daily activities on ISS and examined to what degree mechanical load stimulus, via an in-flight exercise routine, could prevent the muscle atrophy and bone loss associated with space flight.

To achieve this objective, Foot had several sensors mounted in a special pair of Lycra exercise pants known as the lower extremity monitoring suit (LEMS). The total force-foot ground interface (TF-FGI) served as an insole that, when placed inside a shoe, measures the amount of force placed on the bottom of the foot. Joint excursion sensors (JESs) record joint angles at the ankle, knee, and hip. Electromyography electrodes recorded muscle activity, including net neural drive, along the leg (the vastus medialis, rectus femoris, biceps femorics, gastrocnemius, and tibial anterior) and in the right arm (the biceps brachii and triceps brachii). Information was collected by an ambulatory data acquisition system (ADAS) and downloaded into the Human Research Facility (HRF) laptop on board ISS after each session.

A baseline for each participant was created preflight: a DXA image established bone mineral density; a muscle cross-sectional area was measured via Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI); and joint strength was determined using Cybex, an isokinetic dynamometer.

During their stay on ISS, the participants wore the LEMS and accompanying armband for approximately eight hours as they went about their daily activities, including exercise. Data was taken for four days evenly spaced throughout the Expedition. The FGI-FCU was used to calibrate the insoles; a nitrogen source kept a neoprene bladder inflated to maintain contact with the surface of the insole. The data was transferred from the ADAS1 to the HRF laptop after each session.

After flight, additional DEXA and MRI images and Cybex measurements were taken on each participant. The preflight, inflight, and postflight data are compared to create a complete dataset of activity, muscle strength, and bone density changes.

RESULTS:
Prior to the start of this experiment there was poor understanding about the negative effects of long-term exposure to microgravity on bone and muscle, and particularly the effectiveness of the exercise countermeasures in counteracting them. Previous studies have only reported bone and muscle loss in microgravity; whereas, the current experiment characterizes the movement environments that cause such atrophy from joint excursions, muscle activity and foot force data collected over entire typical working days. Comparison of data collected on the ISS with that on Earth gives a clear understanding of the amount of activity that still needs to be supplemented to compensate for weightlessness. The results provided insights on the improvements the exercise modalities and the exercise regimens themselves need, both qualitatively and quantitatively in order to provide the required stimulus that would preserve bone and muscle. The results have a direct impact and lessons to offer when planning for missions to the moon and Mars.

The findings from this experiment support the conclusion that the current exercise durations, combined with markedly reduced loading during ISS locomotor exercise compared to that on Earth, minimal loading from the cycle ergometer and the moderate loads from iRED exercise, may be insufficient to provide the loading stimulus required to prevent bone resorption. There is still high rate of bone and muscle loss in spite of the presence of exercise countermeasures. Muscles experienced a more dominant pattern of isometric action in microgravity at the expense of eccentric and concentric actions, with the hip, knee and ankle joints experiencing fewer, smaller amplitude, slower velocity movements.

It is recommended that approaches to using increased loads that are comfortable for crewmembers while they exercise on current ISS exercise devices be explored for future ISS missions. Future generations of exercise equipment should be designed to provide higher loads. Until increased loading is provided, it is too early to determine whether or not exercise can serve as an effective countermeasure to musculoskeletal deconditioning that occurs in long-duration exposure to microgravity. The possibility also exists; however, that even with improved exercise regimens, pharmacological or other mechanical intervention, perhaps in combination with exercise, will be required to maintain musculoskeletal health. Future experimental work is required to address these uncertainties.
