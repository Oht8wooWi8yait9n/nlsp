Interface Consistency (Phase II): Risk Assessment, Standards, and Guidelines

Record ID: Consistency_2
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objective
To achieve mission objectives, future exploration missions depended on the design and development of vehicles and sophisticated technologies from both NASA and numerous outside commercial partners. NASA's current strategy to commercial spaceflight development is to give providers considerable flexibility and possibilities to innovate, notwithstanding the existence of consistency-related restrictions. Because inconsistent interface features and interactivity may affect crew performance and mission effectiveness, the level of standardization across Artemis vehicle interfaces is an issue. According to the Interface Consistency Phase I pilot study's findings, inconsistent interfaces are linked to greater workload, inefficiency (slower performance time, more actions needed), and decreased system satisfaction. Since there is no evidence regarding the differential impact of particular types of inconsistency related to complex systems, determining the amount of risk is challenging. The high levels of stress and unpredictability that come with more autonomous future missions may make these consequences worse. This study expanded on the research initiated in Phase 1: Characterization and Measurement, that addressed many of the shortcomings found in the literature by creating a definition for consistency, formulating a taxonomy of tangible interface characteristics, applying this framework as guidance for developing consistency assessment tools, and conducting an exploratory pilot study to assess performance of these tools.

During Phase 2, the investigative team accomplished these objectives using the following specific aims:
1. Refine the assessment tools using feedback and results obtained during Phase I, including: item reduction, item phrasing, adding item descriptions and/or free-form responses, and weighting items or scales, if applicable
2. Refine tasks and prototype stimuli using feedback from Program human interface and Artemis training Subject Matter Experts.
3. Characterize risk by understanding differences in human performance and subjective feedback related to each specific type of inconsistency (i.e., interface characteristics and interaction)
4. Propose a set of standards and guidelines addressing aspects of inconsistent design that appear to have the most impact on performance

Approach
During Phase 2, investigators focused on the feedback and results obtained during Phase 1 to refine the assessment tools, bringing forward one tool intended for expert/practitioner use and one tool for crew use. Interviews were conducted with program human interface and training subject matter experts (SMEs) to identify tasks that were likely to be performed across exploration vehicles, to increase the fidelity of tasks conducted during the risk assessment research. These interviews identified interface attributes encountered across vehicles, which influenced updates to the prototype designs. Together, the inputs from SMEs helped create prototype designs that are more representative of exploration systems, more resilient supporting more practical tasks, compared to Phase 1. The prototypes demonstrate consistency changes between assessed pairings at each taxonomy level such as highly consistent (interfaces and interaction highly consistent), high interface consistency, low interaction consistency, low interface consistency, high interaction consistency, and highly inconsistent (interfaces and interaction highly inconsistent). This research gave insight into the kinds of consistency that have a greater influence on performance and subjective feedback. Participants completed a variety of tasks (e.g., monitoring telemetry, sending messages to ground control, configuring systems) on multiple prototypes. Performance (e.g., eye tracking, error rates, time on task), subjective data (e.g., workload, usability, Intersystem Consistency Scale consistency scores), and transfer of training were measured for participants working with different versions of the prototype.

Results
The results of this study convey the risk associated illustrates each consistency category and whether there might be interactions between categories. This will reveal the impact of different types of consistency and allow further refinement of the assessment tool(s). These results also suggest which consistency types should be subject to greater scrutiny when evaluating designs proposed by commercial providers. The team has developed a set of standards and guidelines from the research that will help ensure the most important types of consistency required for future programs.
