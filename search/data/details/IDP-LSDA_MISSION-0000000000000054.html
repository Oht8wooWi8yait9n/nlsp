Summary / Description

STS-51 was the 14th flight of the Space Shuttle Discovery and the 57th flight of the Space Shuttle system. The launch of this mission was postponed multiple times due to problems with the solid rocket boosters, main engine fuel sensors and delay pending the outcome of the Advanced Communications Technology Satellite (ACTS) Independent Review Team. This team met to review the design of the ACTS spacecraft in light of the loss of of contact with the Mars Observer spacecraft and the failure of the NOAA-13 weather satellite. STS-51 finally lifted off on September 12, 1993, from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying a crew of five. The crewmembers were Commander Frank L. Culbertson, Pilot William F. Readdy, and Mission Specialists James H. Newman, Daniel W. Bursch, and Carl E. Walz.

The primary objective of this mission was the deployment of the Advanced Communications Technology Satellite (ACTS). This satellite, which was launched from the Shuttle on the first day of the mission, served as a test bed for advanced experimental satellite communications.

The primary payload on this mission was the Orbiting Retrievable Far and Extreme Ultraviolet Spectrometer (ORFEUS) telescope mounted on the Shuttle Pallet Satellite (SPAS) payload carrier. ORFEUS was designed to provide information on the birth and death of stars, while studying gaseous interstellar clouds. Also in the cargo bay was the Limited Duration Space Environment Candidate Materials Exposure (LDCE) experiment, a materials exposure experiment that was intended to assist with the design and construction of future spacecraft.

STS-51 was the fourth flight of the Chromosome and Plant Cell Division in Space (CHROMEX-04) experiment. CHROMEX payloads flew in the middeck of the Orbiter and consisted of experiments using the Plant Growth Unit (PGU), which allowed scientists to grow whole plants in orbit. The CHROMEX-04 payload consisted of three scientific experiments, including plant reproduction studies, plant cell developmental studies, and cell wall formation and gene expression studies. The plants grew for the length of the mission and were analyzed postflight to determine the effects of microgravity and space flight on their chromosomes, mitosis and reproductive structures.

Other life sciences experiments performed during the STS-51 mission were those classified as Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs). A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies are designed to require minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

Additional payloads included the Commercial Protein Crystal Growth experiment to investigate the growth properties of protein crystals in microgravity; the Radiation Monitoring Equipment-III experiment to measure gamma ray, electron, neutron and proton radiation levels in the crew cabin throughout the flight;the Air Force Maui Optical Site (AMOS) experiment to test ground based optical sensors and examine exhaust plume phenomena using the Space Shuttle as a calibration target; Auroral Photography Experiment-B to photograph the airglow aurora, auroral optical effects, the Shuttle glow phenomenon and thruster emissions in different modes of photography; High Resolution Shuttle Glow Spectroscopy-A to help identify the cause of the Shuttle glow; IMAX for filming general orbiter scenes; and Investigations into Polymer Membrane Processing to research the mixing of various solvent systems in the absence of convection found on earth in hopes of controlling the porosity of various polymer membranes.

During the mission, a 6-hour spacewalk was performed. In addition to performing tasks to investigate a spacewalker's mobility, several tools were evaluated that were to be used during servicing of the Hubble Space Telescope on the STS-61 mission, including a power socket wrench, a torque wrench, foot restraint, safety tethers and tool holder.

STS-51 landed at Kennedy Space Center (KSC) on September 22, 1993 at 3:56 a.m.; this was the first nighttime Shuttle landing at KSC.
