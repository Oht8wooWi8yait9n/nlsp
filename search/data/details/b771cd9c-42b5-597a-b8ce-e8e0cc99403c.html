Biomedical Investigations with Laser-Polarized Noble Gas Magnetic Resonance

Record ID: NAG91166
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The investigators developed a laser-polarized noble gas nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) as a novel biomedical imaging tool for ground-based and eventually space-based applications. This emerging multidisciplinary technology enables high-resolution gas-space magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-e.g., of lung ventilation-as well as studies of tissue perfusion. In addition, laser-polarized noble gases (3He and 129Xe) do not require a large magnetic field for sensitive detection, opening the door to practical MRI at very low magnetic fields with a lightweight, low-power device. The investigators pursued two specific aims in this research. The first aim was to develop a low-field (< 0.01 T) instrument for noble gas MRI of humans, and the second aim was to develop functional imaging of the lung using laser-polarized 129Xe.

The investigators developed a large Helmholtz coil assembly for use in low field noble gas MRI of human lungs. The coil assembly has been used to create a magnetic field of 20 gauss with better than 100 ppm field uniformity over a 30 cm diameter volume centered between the coils. The Helmholtz coil assembly is housed inside a wire-mesh cage to provide shielding from any background electromagnetic noise. The investigators also constructed low-rf pulse/drive coils and associated electronics employing low-cost audio amplifiers with custom trigger/gate circuitry. Using this 20 gauss low-field system, they obtained high SNR signals from a laser-polarized 3He phantom, and acquired a proton NMR signal in a single acquisition. They recently began the design of signal detection coils for low field MRI of human lungs, since they are not available commercially at the audio frequencies used with low field MRI. The investigators have also begun the construction of low-noise receiver electronics for these coils.

APPROACH:
The investigators began development and testing of xenon interphase exchange NMR, a new technique designed to measure the exchange of xenon atoms between the gas and dissolved phases in porous media such as the lung. They used xenon interphase exchange NMR to determine the surface-area-to-volume ratio (S/V) of four samples of porous high-density polyethylene polymer (materials commonly used in filtering, wicking, etc.) They machined each of the four samples to fit tightly in a 0.5" glass cell that excluded free gas around the polymer core. The team performed NMR experiments on a GE Omega/CSI 4.7 T spectrometer operating at 55.3 MHz for 129Xe. They filled the samples and a similar empty cell with thermally- polarized xenon, and made NMR measurements of the sample porosity f, 129Xe partition coefficient b, and dissolved-phase 129Xe T1. For interphase exchange measurements, they evacuated the sample and admitted approximately 1 bar of laser- polarized 129Xe gas into the cell. The team performed a saturation/recovery experiment in which they selectively saturated the dissolved-phase 129Xe polarization and observed its recovery after a variable waiting time due to diffusive exchange from the gas-phase. They determined S/V by fitting this recovery of the dissolved-phase 129Xe NMR signal to an expression they derived for non-equilibrium interphase diffusion. In addition, the team performed independent measurements of S/V for each POREX sample using optical confocal microscopy, a well-established technique which requires sectioning of the sample. They found the NMR and optical measurements of S/V to be in qualitative agreement.

RESULTS:
The results with phantoms indicate the potential for MRI experiments at low field strengths in a large, open-access system, despite low-field limitations of narrow bandwidth RF coils, and gradient pulses that are not necessarily small compared to the applied field. Further system optimization and human inhalation studies are planned for the follow-on NASA grant for this project.
