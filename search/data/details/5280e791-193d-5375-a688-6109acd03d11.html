Retrospective Study of Serum Sclerostin Measurements in Bedrest Subjects

Record ID: ROI_Sclerostin
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Reduced mechanical loading, as in prolonged bed rest, immobilization or space flight, is invariably associated with bone loss. New investigations are needed to gain insight into the mechanisms underlying the musculoskeletal response to mechanical loading, thereby identifying novel targets for preventing musculoskeletal deterioration in situations of reduced mechanical loading, such as bed rest, immobilization, stroke, spinal cord injury, and age-related reductions in physical activity. Osteocytes are believed to play a key role in skeletal mechanosensing, modulating bone modeling, and remodeling by orchestrating the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. However, little is known in humans as to how osteocytes respond to and regulate skeletal adaptation to mechanical unloading.

In mice, exposure to hindlimb tail suspension leads to increased osteocyte apoptosis in both trabecular and cortical bone. It has been postulated that osteocyte cell death is an initiator signal for osteoclast remodeling activity or that loaded osteocytes release an osteoclast-inhibiting factor. Furthermore, mature osteocytes are one of the only cells that express, postnatally, the protein sclerostin, a product of the gene SOST. Sclerostin protein has been shown, in vitro and in vivo, to be a strong inhibitor of bone formation, acting in part through the Wnt/B-catenin pathway to reduce osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Consistent with the negative effect of sclerostin on bone mass, transgenic mice over expressing SOST have low bone mass, whereas SOST-null animals have high bone mass in line with the human conditions of Van Buchem’s disease and sclerosteosis, which are due to inactivating mutations in the SOST gene. Furthermore, the SOST/Sclerostin pathway has been implicated in the bone’s response to mechanical loading, such that increased skeletal loading reduces SOST/Sclerostin levels, and decreased mechanical loading leads to higher SOST/Sclerostin levels.

Specific Aims:

1. Determine whether skeletal unloading increases circulating levels of sclerostin by conducting assays in archived serum specimens of subjects exposed to 90 days of bed rest.
2. Determine association between serum sclerostin and standard measures of bone turnover markers (urine N-teleopeptide (NTX), serum osteocalcin and bone specific-alkaline phosphatase (BSAP).
3.Determine association between increase in serum sclerostin and BMD changes following bed rest (an analog for space flight).

APPROACH:
Seven healthy adult men underwent 90-day six-degree head down tilt bed rest at The University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) – NASA bed rest facility. Subjects were: a) between the ages of 25 and 55 years; b) required to pass a modified Air Force Class III flight physical within one year prior to the study; c) mentally fit for bed rest study as assessed by psychiatric interview and test; and d) not currently taking prescribed medication that would have interfered with physiological measurements, including bisphosphonate therapy. Serum and urine were collected prior to bed rest and during bed rest. The study protocol was approved by the NASA JSC Institutional Review Board, and all subjects gave informed consent prior to participation.

Areal bone mineral density (BMD) was measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, Hologic Discovery). BMD of the whole body, lumbar spine, left and right hips, heel, and forearm was assessed at baseline and repeated at BR-60 and 5 days after the end of the bed rest period (BR+5). Serum sclerostin levels were assayed using the APLCO/Biomedica human sclerostin kit per manufacturer’s recommendations. Measurements were made on two pre-bed rest measurements and during bed rest days 28, 60, and 90 (BR-28, B-60, and BR-90). Assays were run in duplicate.

Markers of bone metabolism, formation and resorption were also assayed in urine and serum specimens, including serum parathyroid hormone (PTH), testosterone, leptin, 1,25 Vitamin D, markers of bone turnover (bone specific alkaline phosphatase, RANKL/OPG, and osteocalcin), and urinary calcium and phosphorus excretion normalized to creatinine, and 24 hour pooled urinary markers of bone turnover normalized to creatinine. All biochemical analyses were performed by personnel at the NASA Johnson Space Center Nutritional Biochemistry Laboratory with standard commercial techniques. For all serum and urine markers, the two pre-bed rest measurements samples were averaged to provide a single baseline value for each subject.

RESULTS:
Serum sclerostin increased after bed rest in all subjects. Specifically, serum sclerostin levels increased above BL at BR-28 and appeared to plateau in most subjects at BR-60. Sclerostin levels remained mildly elevated at BR-90, although this result did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, serum PTH levels declined at BR-28 and BR-60, remained reduced at BR-90, but did not reach statistical significance.

Urinary levels of bone resorption markers (NTX, DPD, PYD) increased significantly compared with BL at all time points. Urinary calcium excretion was also significantly increased throughout the study, whereas urinary phosphorus levels were elevated at BR-28 and BR-60, with a return to BL at BR-90. Serum bone formation markers (serum BSAP, osteocalcin), serum RANKL, OPG, and the RANKL/OPG ratio did not change. 25 (OH) vitamin D was above BL at all time points (P = 0.005), whereas 1,25 (OH)2 vitamin D was significantly lower than BL at BR-28 and tended also to be lower at BR-60 (P = 0.06) and BR-90 (P = 0.07).

Subjects had normal BMD because BL Z-scores at the distal radius, lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck were 0.2 ± 0.7, 0.6 ± 1.0, 0.2 ± 1.0, and 0.0 ± 1.3, respectively. BMD declined significantly at BR-60 and BR+5 at all weight-bearing skeletal sites, including the lumbar spine, hip, femoral neck, and calcaneus. There was no change in forearm BMD.
