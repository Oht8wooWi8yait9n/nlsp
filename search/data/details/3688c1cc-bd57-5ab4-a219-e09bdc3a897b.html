A Study of Radiation Doses Experienced by Astronauts During EVA

Record ID: 96-E011
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The space radiation environment is dominated by high-energy electrons and protons, and their fluxes are a function of orbital position and time. In general, particle fluxes are higher in the South Atlantic Anomaly and Polar Horns, and with solar flares or other high solar activity, the particle fluxes can temporarily increase considerably. Shielding from the spacecraft structure and/or space suits changes the space radiation reaching the surface of a crewmember's body by removing low energy particles and generating secondary radiation. The radiation environment inside the spacecraft consists primarily of high energy protons and secondary particles, which include neutrons generated by the interactions of the high energy protons and the shielding material of the spacecraft structure. Activity external to the spacecraft exposes humans to much higher radiation levels as a result of decreased shielding. The type and flux of radiation inside the space suit depends on the shielding afforded by the particular part of the suit. Consequently, different parts of the body (e.g., arms, body, eyes) receive different levels of radiation.

The aim of this investigation is to determine the levels of radiation dose received to the skin, eyes, and blood forming organs (BFOs) of crewmembers during EVA.

APPROACH:
Twelve sensor badges were used in this experiment. They were grouped into four sets (EV1 to EV4) of three badges, each containing a specific head, torso and leg badge. All badges were read at regular intervals while on the space station, and specifically before and after any EVAs. During an EVA, the astronauts were required to wear all three badges.

Crewmembers wore three active dosimeter badges (placed on the leg, torso, and near the eye) during Extravehicular Activity (EVA). The badges were placed in temporary pockets sewn into the Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) undergarments. The leg and torso badges were located either on the Thermal Comfort Undergarment (TCU) or the Liquid Cooled Ventilation Garment (LCVG) worn inside the EMU, and the eye badge was placed on the Communications Carrier Assembly (CCA). The badges were read individually before and after EVA using an electronic reader. Following EVA activities, the radiation data was downloaded to the onboard computer and batch-downlinked to the ground once per week. The dose readings were recorded onboard the spacecraft and analyzed against time and position in EVA, the orbit, altitude and attitude of the ISS, and any shielding effects provided by the ISS.

RESULTS:
Data derived from the study has helped determine the overall dose levels experienced in EVA, the variation of dose received by the skin, eyes, and BFOs, and the orbital factors and degree of shielding afforded by the EVA suit and the spacecraft structure. From this information, recommendations and guidelines for possible implications in radiation protection will be made.

EVARM results show that geomagnetic storms do increase the radiation doses to which spacewalking astronauts are exposed, although these storms are not very common. Shielding from the ISS significantly affected the dose each badge received. To determine radiation doses received by specific organs, results showed that one torso badge was not enough, and that badges must be placed more accurately near the organ to be monitored.

Expedition 4: data were received for eight extravehicular activities (EVAs). Two astronauts participated in each EVA, during which both were expected to wear all three head, torso and leg badges. However, some of the badges were not worn at certain times. Head badges were not worn on five of the EVAs (#1, 2, 6, 7 and 8) and leg badges were not worn on EVA #3. These unworn badges were left inside the space station and read normally. This was taken into consideration in the analysis of the data.

Expedition 5: Six EVAs occurred during Expedition 5. Two astronauts participated in each EVA, during which both were expected to wear all three head, torso and leg badges. However, some of the badges were not worn at certain times. These unworn badges were left inside the space station and read normally. This was taken into consideration in the analysis of the data.

Expedition 6: Three EVAs occurred during Expedition 6. Two astronauts participated in each EVA, during which both were expected to wear all three head, torso and leg badges. All badges were worn as expected, therefore six individual EVAs were obtained. In addition to EVA measurements, an inflight internal dose measurement intercomparison was made between EVARM badges and the NASA Intra-vehicular Charged Particle Directional Spectrometer (IVCPDS) instrument.

In conclusion, although radiation doses have been measured in Shuttle and Mir missions for many years, the emphasis has been on the dose received to blood forming organs (BFOs) in crew quarters. Extremity dosimetry has not been well addressed, primarily because the crew radiation environment is dominated by high-energy protons that mainly affect doses to the BFOs. Missions on the International Space Station (ISS) will require significantly more Extravehicular Activity (EVA), and it will be important to measure skin and eye doses as well as dose to BFOs. Radiation doses have previously been measured by chemical thermoluminescent dosimeters that require considerable processing before and after use. This investigation used electronic badge radiation dosimeters, based on silicon transistor technology, which can measure small radiation doses. Data derived from these dosimeters helped determine which parts of the human body are exposed to the highest doses of radiation and under which EVA situations this arises.Data derived from the study will help determine the overall dose levels experienced in EVA, the variation of dose received by the skin, eyes, and BFOs, and the orbital factors and degree of shielding afforded by the EVA suit and the spacecraft structure. This information is required to determine radiation protection criteria for the ISS crew. In addition, the new badge option will eventually allow EVA crew to measure and monitor their radiation doses on an as-required basis, allowing for safety decisions based on total body dose received to all critical organs during the flight career of crewmembers.
