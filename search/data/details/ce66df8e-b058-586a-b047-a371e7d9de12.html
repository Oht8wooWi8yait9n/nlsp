Automated Use of DNA Probes for Rapid Detection of Bacteria in Water

Record ID: 9601212
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The need for rapid, reliable tests for microbial agents has never been more important. Technology for rapid diagnostics and environmental analysis has not been available. Most technologies commonly used as the "gold standards" in both clinical and environmental laboratories are based on culture techniques. This technology has been used for more than 100 years and is not rapid enough for current needs. Much work is being conducted to help alleviate this urgent problem. In recent years, considerable progress has been made in molecular approaches to addressing this problem. A DNA probe is one such technology that can provide relatively rapid analysis of microbes and is not based on culture technology. The successful completion of this NASA research task should provide probe technology for the rapid detection and identification of waterborne bacteria. This could be used in the food and beverage industry, pharmaceuticals, semiconductor industry, water producing companies, and others. The technology can also be used on clinical applications, such urinary tract infections, allowing for a more rapid treatment and better clinical outcome.

Three objectives were addressed by this proposal. The first was to define a common set of protocols for use of DNA probes against specific target bacteria and bacterial groups. The second objective was to analyze the application of chemiluminescent compounds as reporters of hybridization and automate the hybridization protocol. The third objective was to evaluate the automated system in both ground-based and microgravity environments.

APPROACH:
Development of an automated DNA probe technology for the monitoring of bacteria in water for use during space flight was planned. This simple water quality assay will provide a rapid, specific, and sensitive means of identifying and measuring the number of total bacteria, total coliform bacteria, Escherichia coli, Burkholderia (Pseudomonas) cepacia, Methylobacterium spp, and other heterotrophic bacteria in water samples. Using this approach, a completed test should be attainable in less than 8 hours, with a sensitivity of 100 CFU/100 ml of water or better.

The feasibility of using probes that target 16S rRNA for monitoring of water quality has been demonstrated previously. Assays based on hybridization arrays (DNA chips) or on direct detection with chemiluminescence or fluorescence are feasible. It was envisioned that a sandwich assay format might be used in either case. There were three major difficulties to overcome to make 16S rRNA targeted probes useful in the space environment. These are (1) development of an appropriate set of hybridization probes with minimal cross-reactivity, (2) increasing the sensitivity of the assay, and (3) the development of spacecraft compatible procedures for extracting and purifying the target nucleic acids.

RESULTS:
The team focused primarily on refinement of probe sets. The team tested capture probes for RNA affinity of appropriate scope and detector probes for the necessary specificity. Chemically synthesized probes were purchased with a 3 biotin modification to be used in an affinity detection system. Dot-blot assays of total RNA preparations from Escherichia coli, Vibrio proteolyticus, Proteus vulgaris, Acinetobacter sp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas putida, Burkholderia cepacia, Ralstonia pickettii, Serratia marcescens, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus xylosus and Halobacterium vallismortis were used to test the specificity of various probes. Most of the capture probes, as expected, showed little species specificity, since they are designed to be "degenerate" at several positions so that they can capture a variety of 16S rRNA types. In contrast, the detector probes should be very specific. In this regard, the team found very promising probes for Burkholderia cepacia and Acinetobacter. In addition, an all bacteria pan probe that gives a positive signal with all the RNAs being tested was identified. Other probes in the original set, e.g. especially E. coli and Staphylococcus, did not perform adequately, even at high hybridization temperatures and stringent washing conditions.

To simplify and improve the redesign of these probes, a new bioinformatics tool was developed that more directly searches for unique and characteristic sequences in the ribosomal RNA database. The program known as OligoSCAN selects a fixed length oligonucleotide in the target RNA, starting at the 3' end of each sequence and scans it against the rest of the sequences for possible similarities. Only oligomers that do not match any region of other rRNA sequences, in either orientation are selected. The program then selects the next oligonucleotide on the sequence. The program continues to scan each target sequence until the end of the rRNAis reached. A mismatch value can also be specified, in which case the oligonucleotide is considered unique even if it matches regions of other sequences, the total number of matches being less than the mismatch number. The resulting oligonucleotides are concurrently subjected to an analysis of %G+C and Tm, which eliminates those candidates that are not within appropriate ranges. OligoSCAN was run with the length of the desired oligonucleotide set at 17 and the mismatch number ranging from 10 - 14. At least one unique oligonucleotide was identified for each sequence in the data set when the mismatch number was 12. The potential oligonucleotides that were identified were crosschecked for specificity against the rRNA database with the BLAST program. A new set of chemically synthesized oligonucleotide detector probes were designed in this way and end-labeled with 32P-ATP using T4 kinase for detection using autoradiography. Two additional pan probes and a species specific probe for Vibrio proteolyticus were identified.
