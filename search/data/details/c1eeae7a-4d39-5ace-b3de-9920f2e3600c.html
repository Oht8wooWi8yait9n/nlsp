Simulated Microgravity and Radiation-Induced Bone Degeneration: Oxidative Stress- and p53-Dependent Mechanisms

Record ID: ARC00XX71
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

To determine the risks and mechanisms of tissue degeneration in the spaceflight environment, we will examine the combined effects of radiation and simulated weightlessness on bone. We will focus our studies on two important pathways, p53 and oxidative and nitrative stress-related species, in bone marrow progenitor populations because there is abundant evidence that the biological effects of weightlessness and those of radiation share these mechanism. Experiments will be conducted using the rodent hindlimb unloading model, the best validated animal model for simulating weightlessness, followed by irradiation. In Aim 1, we will test the hypothesis that simulated weightlessness of mice modulates the response of bone and bone marrow to radiation, both acutely and during tissue regeneration. In Aim 2 we will identify the target marrow-derived stem, progenitor and differentiated cell populations sensitive to the combined stimuli of simulated weightlessness and radiation. In Aim 3, we will explore the relevance of specific ROS/RNS and p53-dependent mechanisms for mediating cellular responses to combined simulated weightlessness and radiation. The results of these studies are expected to provide important information for the development of countermeasures both to help prevent degeneration of connective tissues such as bone during spaceflight and to improve subsequent tissue regeneration.

RESULTS:

We were the first to report in the scientific literature on the influence of simulated weightlessness and radiation on mineralized tissue. Our published results show that total body irradiation of adult male mice with doses of iron as low as 0.10 Gy and doses of photons (Cesium-137) as low as 1.0 Gy can cause significant loss of mineralized tissue within the spongy areas of bone. This bone loss is very rapid (within 3 to 7 days), and appears to be caused by an increase in the activity of bone resorbing osteoclasts. The importance of the osteoclasts for radiation-induced bone loss is further supported by our findings that radiation can increase the number of osteoclasts in cultured cells.

One of our surprising findings is that radiation-induced bone loss is restricted to high turnover rate cancellous tissue, in contrast to unloading-induced bone loss, which manifests in both cortical bone tissue (outer shell) as well as cancellous tissue. As a consequence, the mechanical properties of the spine are largely preserved after low dose, total body irradiation alone and are likely to be preserved for most bones, at least in the short term.

Simulated weightlessness at the time of radiation exposure appears to impair skeletal remodeling even during a prolonged period of recovery. Furthermore, relatively low doses of space radiation can exert system-wide and persistent change. All of our experiments have been conducted using adult, male mice of a single strain (C57Bl/6J). Differences in biological responses to treatments can occur simply due to age, gender, strain, as well as species, and therefore further studies are needed to help assess human risk. Nonetheless, our findings suggest space radiation, alone and combined with musculoskeletal disuse, may pose a long-term risk to the skeletal health of astronauts.
