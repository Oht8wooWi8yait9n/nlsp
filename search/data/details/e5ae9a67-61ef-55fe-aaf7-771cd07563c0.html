PharmaSat: Effect of Microgravity upon Yeast Susceptibility to Antifungal Drugs

Record ID: NNX07AD28A
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

The PharmaSat experiment was designed to measure the effect of microgravity upon yeast resistance to the antimicrobial agent - voriconazole. Voriconazole is a stable and broad spectrum antimicrobial that targets the fungal cytochrome p450 sterol 14-demethylase enzyme (CYP51) Previous experimentation in microgravity and under modeled microgravity conditions suggest that in these environments, resistance to azoles is increased. Voriconazole has excellent stability under ambient conditions and exhibits a broad spectrum of activity and is being evaluated in vitro as a potential antifungal counter-measure in space. The PharmaSat experimental design uses a recognized laboratory testing method for antimicrobial resistance. The experiment investigated three concentrations of voriconazole (a low concentration, a concentration at the minimal inhibitory concentration, MIC and a high dose, plus a no-drug control) for their effect on yeast growth cell and viability. Saccharomyces cerevisiae was used as a model microorganism and has been the subject of previous space-flight studies, and is used as a standard to evaluate resistance to antifungal agents. Growth was measured by two independent methods - optical density (OD) and Alamar Blue reduction as a metabolic indicator. Statistical analysis of the data used longitudinal data, a more robust approach than traditional end-point-based analysis. The underlying hypothesis for this experiment was “yeast growing in microgravity has altered resistance to azole antifungal agents”. The experiment was conducted in a 59-well Fluidic Card with 48 biology wells in four independently plumbed banks with 11 reference wells in the center bank. Gas- permeable membranes on both sides of the wells allowed for air exchange and 1µm Nylon filter membranes retained the yeast cells inside the wells The experiment used a complex fluidic system capable of two fluid exchanges, dilution of the antifungal agent and replenishing evaporative losses by using 2 pumps, 9 reservoir bags and 14 valves.

APPROACH:

The nanosatelite was deployed ~20 min after launch and radio contact was established during the first orbit. The satellite orbit became stabilized in ~24 hours. Its orbital trajectory put PharmaSat in sunlight ~ 67% and darkness ~ 33% of each 97-min orbit. After stabilization (gravity < 10-3 g), at t = 47 hr after orbital deployment, the temperature of the fluidic card was set to 27 °C. After allowing 3.6 hr for temperature stabilization, S. cerevisiae growth was initiated (in all 48 wells of the microfluidic plate) by introducing RPMI1640 cell culture medium containing the viability dye - Alamar blue. Each 12-well bank of wells was activated in sequence. Two concentrations (0.1 and 0.2 OD600) of the yeast were utilized. In each bank one set of six wells were loaded with yeast at the lower optical density, while the remaining 6 wells of each bank were loaded with yeast at the higher OD (0.2). This strategy insured that if insufficient organisms survived the stasis period in the lower OD groups of wells, the higher OD wells would provide an ~2X higher initial population to begin growth. Since antifungal tests are more reliable at lower cell densities, the successful growth of all 24 wells at the lower OD cultures led us to choose those data for our initial ground/flight comparisons.

Voriconazole, a stable antifungal agent was introduced after the yeast had initiated growth. Based on initial growth rates, voriconazole was introduced by pumping ~ 12 hr after initial growth medium introduction. Ground controls for this experiment consisted of an identical satellite system at NASA Ames, housed in a thermal chamber with temperatures set to those reported by the satellite. This Ground Control, with S. cerevisiae loaded from the same flight cultures was conducted as a “delayed synchronous” (~1 day) ground control experiment to compare ground and space results.

After allowing for initial growth (re-activation of the yeast cultures), growth medium + voriconazole was pumped into the four 12-well banks at 0.25X, 1X, and 4X the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). The MIC of voriconazole for S. cerevisiae was 0.5 µg/mL in ground studies. This allowed each of the four conditions tested with multiple independent microwells at both concentrations.

RESULTS:

Yeast cultures showed a longer “lag time” (time to the mid-point of mid-logarithmic growth), growth time (time in exponential growth) and doubling time in space samples than ground controls. All values were significant except for cell division for the growth time measurement. It may be the case that the yeast grow more slowly in space because the transport of nutrients to the cells and/or the transport of waste products away from the cells is slower, being driven solely by diffusion, than on Earth where convection can play a role in mass transport. Overall, the PharmaSat experiment was a success. Cell growth and metabolic activity was observed in all wells, and an antifungal effect was observed in both ground and spaceflight samples. All equipment functioned nominally. Biological results showed: 1) differences in the growth rates between flight and ground samples, 2) antifungal activity was observed in both cultures, 3) no significant differences between the spaceflight and ground cultures were observed with the low and MIC concentrations but a large difference was observed at the high antifungal concentration.
