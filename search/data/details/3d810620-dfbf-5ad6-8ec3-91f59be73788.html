Advanced Trace Gas Monitoring Technology for NASA Human Space Flight

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This project was a joint Rice University- George Washington University-JPL research and development program of mid-infrared diode laser sources in the 3 to 4.4 micron region and their application to trace gas sensing in NASA applications relevant to spacecraft environmental monitoring and advanced life support. Presently, single frequency diode and quantum cascade lasers are not available in the proposed mid infrared spectral region that covers an important range where sensitive, selective and fast trace gas detection can now only be performed with complex spectroscopic sources, such as lead salt diode lasers and indirect laser sources based on difference frequency generation. Pulsed and continuous wave Type I quantum-cascade lasers, now commercially available cover the wavelength region from 4.4 - 24 microns. However, there is a significant need to access wavelengths from 3 to 4.4 microns, where a number of critical molecules (e.g. CH4, HCl, NO2, H2CO,SO2, and CO2) possess strong ro-vibrational lines. The Laser Science group at Rice University has accumulated a decade of experience in laser based trace gas detection in applications directly relevant to NASA as well as in other areas such as atmospheric chemistry, environmental monitoring, industrial process control and medical diagnostics. The group of Prof. Houston Miller provided expertise in spectroscopic strategy for detecting Code M molecular target gases as well as developing a cavity ringdown spectroscopy (CRDS) based sensor technology. JPL, with world-class semiconductor facilities and the expertise necessary, developed Sb-based diode and type II interband cascade lasers with distributed feedback structures for meeting the application needs. At Rice the primary emphasis was to demonstrate and emphasize a novel optical detection technique based on an ultracompact photoacoustic analyzer design that will significantly enhance the performance and robustness of near infrared and mid-infrared gas sensors.

APPROACH:
Investigators used a photoacoustic for the detection of trace gases utilizing a quartz tuning fork (TF) as a resonant acoustic transducer. Advantages of the technique called quartz-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (QEPAS) compared to conventional resonant photoacoustic spectroscopy include QEPAS sensor immunity to environmental acoustic noise, a simple absorption detection module design, and its capability to analyze gas samples approximately 1 mm3 in volume.

RESULTS:
CRDS: Investigators have demonstrated the measurement of several air constituents at levels of detection required for indoor air pollution, medical diagnoses, or agricultural/industrial monitoring. Further, they have demonstrated that their sensors can be operated unattended for several days and provide reliable habitability data.
QEPAS: Demonstrated very compact robust sensors that can be used in industry-process streams, petrochemical and/or biomedical fields.
Investigators developed both technologies to the point where a select outside panel made a downselect and chose QEPAS for further development for deployment on the International Space Station (ISS) as a technology demonstration. QEPAS sensor and electronic is approximately 0.8 l total volume. Demonstrated approximately 60 ppb for formaldehyde but could go even lower. The downselection was made after an extensive system engineering study at JPL, using Team X resources to develop strawman instrument designs.
