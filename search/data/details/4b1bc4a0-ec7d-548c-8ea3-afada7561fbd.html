Medical Microbiological Analysis of U.S. Crew Members

Record ID: ASTP014
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The Apollo-Soyuz Test Project (ASTP) was a unique space flight in which spacecrafts from America and the Soviet Union docked together in space. This presented an opportunity to study cross-contamination of one crew with the medically important microorganisms carried by the other crew. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were to identify and trace all microorganisms of potential medical importance. Medical microbiological analysis of U.S. crewmembers was performed as a part of a larger study, the Microbial Exchange Experiment (AR-002), conducted during the historic ASTP space flight.

APPROACH:
Microbial samples were collected from the three Apollo crewmembers and two Soyuz crewmembers on L-45 days, L-30, L-15, L-7, L-0 (launch day), once during flight, and on recovery days R+0, R+15 and R+30. Backup crewmembers served as experimental controls. Sample collection sites included:

1) A 20 square centimeters area of hair (and scalp) on the top of the head
2) Right and left external auditory canals
3) 20 square centimeters area below hairline at base of neck
4) Internal area of both nostrils
5) The tonsils and posterior pharyngeal vault
6) 20 square centimeters area on right and left palms
7) 20 square centimeters area below the hairline of each axilla
8) 5 centimeter strip from rear to front on right and left inguinal area between legs
9) Area between the two smallest toes of each foot
10) The throat and mouth

Preflight and post flight, microbial specimens were collected from each crewmember using calcium alginate swabs wet with 0.3 millimolar phosphate buffer. Dry calcium alginate swabs were used to sample the tonsils and pharyngeal vault prior to the collection of the gargle specimen.

In-flight samples, consisting of the first six areas outlined above, were obtained from all five flight crewmembers on flight day 3. A specially developed microbial sample collection device was used to collect in-flight samples. This device consisted of a cotton-tipped Teflon swab on a capillary tube containing a conservative fluid, which kept the microorganisms alive. Each swab was housed within an airtight case to prevent desiccation.

Each sample was serially diluted under aseptic conditions and inoculated onto nutrient media. The variety of media, number of plates inoculated and dilution range were selected on the basis of what was required to isolate and quantitate the autoflora present in each sample area. Sabauraud's Dextrose Agar (SAB) plates were incubated at 30 degrees Celsius for five days. Cornmeal, malt-extract, yeast extract (CMMY) agar plates were incubated at 25 degrees Celsius for 7 days and all others were incubated at 37 degrees Celsius for 48 hours. Following incubation, the resulting aerobic bacterial colonies were categorized and counted with one sample of each morphologically different colony type transferred to nutrient media and stained with Gram's stain. Apollo sample analysis was conducted at the NASA Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center (JSC) in Houston, Texas.

RESULTS:
Gram-negative Rods:
Escherichia coli, Enterobacter aerogenes and Proteus mirabilis, members of the enteric bacilli group, were found on the ASTP crewmembers at sites other than the lower intestinal tract. Bacilli species are generally considered of potential medical importance when they are recovered repeatedly, or in large numbers, from sites other than the lower digestive tract.

Escherichia coli is generally accepted as the most reliable evidence of fecal contamination. Outside the intestinal tract it can cause urinary tract infections, peritonitis, gall bladder infections, wound infections, septicemia and enteritis. E. coli was repeatedly recovered from the groin of ASTP subject B and occasionally from the upper respiratory tract of the other two prime crewmembers. In all cases, no medical significance was indicated by the occurrence or quantities of E. coli.

Enterobacter aerogenes is found in the large intestine of man. Its pathogenic significance is similar to that of E. coli. This species was always carried in the nasal and buccal cavities of ASTP subjects B and C and was frequently isolated from two backup crewmembers. E. aerogenes was not spread to ASTP subject A or to other sites on the carrier subject during the flight. In addition, E. aerogenes quantities were not increased postflight.

Proteus mirabilis can cause urinary tract infections, abscesses and gastroenteritis. More often, they cause secondary infections of the middle ear, mastoid process, meninges and urinary tract, as well as in existing wounds. P. mirabilis was carried in low numbers in the nasal passage of ASTP subject A. This species and E. aerogenes provided a good model system for analyzing the response of Gram negative rods to space flight conditions. It has previously been reported that the Gram-negative rod component of the autoflora could change during or following confinement in a closed environment and that this change could result in "dysobacteriosis," such as flooding of an area with a particular species. The ASTP mission had no such effect on the qualitative or quantitative presence of these microorganisms.

Moraxella species are parasites found in the mucous membranes of man that can cause diseases such as conjunctivitis. Although carried in the mouth of ASTP subject C for 45 days preflight, no discernible microbial alteration was demonstrated following the mission.

Acinetobacter calcoaceticus, of minor medical importance, was isolated from multiple sites on ASTP subject B. It did not contribute significantly to the microbial load of Gram-negative rods recovered from ASTP crewmembers.

Four species of Haemophilus were isolated from the oral cavity of each ASTP astronaut. These were H. influenzae, H. haemolyticus, H. parahaemolyticus and H. parainfluenzae. Although each of these species is a common inhabitant of the human mouth, each is a strict parasite, requiring certain growth factors present in blood. H. influenzae is found in lesions and in the upper respiratory tract of carriers. It may be either a primary or a secondary invader. This microorganism is responsible for meningitis, septicemia, conjunctivitis and upper respiratory tract infections, and as a secondary invader in influenza and pertussis cases. H. influenzae was isolated from each crewmember preflight in quantities from 10,000 to 600,000 viable cells/ml of gargle. The post flight loss from two crewmembers and quantitative reduction in contamination of ASTP subject A was contraindicative of a space flight mediated increase in infective potential.

H. haemolyticus and H. parahaemolyticus are often associated with acute pharyngitis when present in high numbers. As with the H. influenzae, the overall incidence and quantitation of H. haemolyticus and H. parahaemolyticus decreased following the ASTP flight. H. parainfluenzae is the least harmful of the four species recovered and is used largely as a marker organism due to its universal appearance.

Candida albicans
Candida albicans has been recovered from astronauts throughout the Apollo and Skylab missions. Because this species can cause serious oral cavity diseases, its presence was carefully monitored. C. albicans was isolated from ASTP subjects B and C at all sampling periods. Data analysis indicates the inflight transfer of C. albicans to ASTP subject A. No such transfer occurred in the backup crew, although C. albicans was carried by one crewman. All of these events demonstrate the importance of this species in space flight. The importance could increase during the in-flight use of antibiotics, because this could cause a loss of competing bacterial species and eventual overgrowth of C. albicans.

Staphylococcus aureus
Although Staphylococcus aureus is a common skin and nasal contaminant, all strains are potential pathogens. S. aureus can cause boils, abscesses, meningitis, furunculosis, pyemia, osteomyelitis, suppuration of wounds and food poisoning. Several studies have indicated increases in the toxigenic activity, virulence and pathogenicity of this species with stressful confinement of the human host. Lesions caused by S. aureus in space flight were important because of their interference with close contact surfaces, such as the tight fitting and abrasive pressure suits. Accordingly, the presence and activity of this species was monitored before and after each space flight. Data from Apollo 13, 14 and 15 missions, and from the Skylab missions confirm in-flight cross contamination with S. aureus.

Data show that each crewmember (flight and backup) carried a different strain of S. aureus. Different strains are expressed as bacteriophage types. One type (52,52A,80,81), carried by ASTP subject C, was transferred to ASTP subject A during flight. Apparently colonization did not ensue, as this strain was not recovered from subject A post flight.

During the ASTP mission, a post flight increase of S. aureus did not occur among the astronaut population, as reported during previous missions, and disease events did not result from its presence.

Total Load of Potential Pathogens
One idea that resulted from previous space flight microbial studies was that crewmembers could suffer "microbial shock" upon return to Earth, and therefore, respond negatively to renewed contact with the potentially pathogenic microorganisms absent in the space flight environment. This was based on the assumption that contact with potential pathogens during space flight would be very limited, resulting in a reduction of immunocompetence. However, there was no demonstrable decrease in the incidence of medically important microorganisms recovered from the astronauts on R+0, a finding which supports earlier results. Therefore, if a reduction in total immunocompetence occurred during the ASTP mission, it was not in response to decreased contact with medically important components of the resident microflora.

Although several potential pathogens were recovered from each of the flight and backup crewmembers before and after flight, no disease events were reported. C. albicans and S. aureus were transferred from one crewmember to another in-flight, but no other medically significant changes in the microbial population were observed.
