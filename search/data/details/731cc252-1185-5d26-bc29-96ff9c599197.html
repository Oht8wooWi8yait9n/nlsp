Crew Activities / Maintenance Study

Record ID: M516
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
NASA spacecraft utilized prior to the Skylab Orbital Workshop (OWS) contained a habitable volume of 15 cubic meters or less. Within this volume, the crew interfaced with approximately 1,300 controls and displays and 800 stowage items. The Skylab OWS increased the habitable volume to over 340 cubic meters, and the man/machine interfaces to approximately 3,000 controls and displays and over 10,000 stowage items. The ability to operate this complex array of hardware on a daily basis and to produce meaningful scientific results were some of the prime areas for investigation during the Skylab missions.

The Crew Activities/Maintenance Study involved manual dexterity, locomotion, logistics management, maintenance, and various planning functions necessary for creating a favorable daily working routine. The objectives were:

1. To evaluate man/machine relationships by gathering data concerning the crew's ability to perform work in 0-G.
2. To systematically document man's performance.
3. To evaluate the data obtained relative to the design criteria for Skylab missions.
4. To report the evaluations and conclusions in terms useful to the designers of future manned spacecraft and to the planners of future manned missions.

APPROACH:
Because the crew's daily activities limited the opportunities for data collection, the spectrum of daily tasks was carefully evaluated to identify suitable candidates for providing the desired data. Data were obtained via crew comments, 16-mm motion-picture film, and television transmissions.

Film was apportioned and distributed throughout each mission to observe technique changes, as a function of time, to as many different activities as possible by selecting the lowest possible frame rates. Scheduled and unscheduled television transmissions provided near real-time visual cues to assess the ease or difficulty of performing onboard activities. Crew comments were solicited through structured questions and evaluation forms contained in onboard checklists. Ad hoc crew commentary during the missions added to the data bank, and structured postflight debriefings completed the collection and clarification of information.

Logistics Management: To categorize the inventory of re-locatable items onboard Skylab, groupings based on the mass of the item were established. Items weighing less than 5 kilograms (kg) were considered small, items weighing between 5 and 50 kg were considered medium, and items weighing more than 50 kg were considered large. The hypothesis was that subjective crew impressions of the ease or difficulty of handling these items, combined with photographic and television coverage, would lead to a definition of boundaries for mass and dimensions of hardware.

Maintenance: Planning for the Skylab Program, as in previous NASA manned programs, did not include detailed recommendations for the possibility of inflight maintenance. Design reliability and redundancy were supposed to eliminate the need for inflight maintenance. However, a committee was established during the design and production phases to study inflight maintenance, recommend a set of tools, and provide a list of candidate tasks for the missions. A standard set of mechanical tools was recommended, with specific deletions of certain items in a continuous set when no particular task could be identified that required that individual tool. Obvious debris generators (saws, drills, etc.) and all types of test equipment were excluded from the inventory of maintenance equipment to be launched initially. Tasks selected for the experiment involved predominantly simple replacement chores (changing lightbulbs and filters) and numerous remove-and-replace tasks for failed items.

RESULTS:
Mass: The largest mass requiring manual relocation on board Skylab was the food container. Six of these containers were bolted to the grid floor of the forward compartment of the Orbital Workshop (OWS) at launch. Each container had a mass of approximately 100 kg and dimensions of approximately 0.6 by 0.6 by 1.0 meter. During the first day of activation, the Skylab-2 crew moved these containers from their launch location to a permanent stowage rack approximately 6 meters away. The relocation presented no difficulty; even without handles on the containers, the mounting flange served as a natural handle. Numerous other items classified in the large and medium categories were transferred by each crewmember with exceeding ease and efficiency across distances varying from 1 to 20 meters.

Techniques for moving objects varied among crewmembers. A common method for moving items in the medium and large categories involved the crewmember moving the item along the desired path, accelerating himself past the item, and then reorienting himself at the terminal point to catch the item as it arrived.

Items that fell into the lower end of the small category (less than 5 kg) were the ones that presented logistics management problems to the crewmembers. Individual items with masses from a few grams to 1 kg were easily perturbed in 0-G and were extremely difficult to release with no residual motion. Airflow created by the ventilation system was sufficient to move these small objects about quite readily. Also, when several such items were stowed in a common location without means of retention, it was difficult to retrieve one item without upsetting the others. Quite often, the entire inventory would be extracted. The time lost in retrieving and re-stowing such inadvertently freed objects became an irritating perturbation to the timeline.

Size: The limiting factor for handling large objects in 0-G was the cross-sectional area of the item rather than its mass. If a crewmember could see where he and the item being transported were going, he could effect the maneuver very readily. The cross-sectional dimensions of the food containers approached the upper limit of visual obstruction that could be tolerated. The food containers presented a visual obstruction because they had to be aligned and slipped into a rack over the full 1-meter length.

Large items that did not present a visual obstruction were handled very effectively. Several of the Skylab experiment containers were quite massive but had a rather long and narrow configuration. One experiment container slightly less than 2 meters in length with a cross section of approximately 25 by 25 centimeters was transferred from the command module to the forward compartment of the OWS (a distance of approximately 20 meters) without incident. Five hatchways and a short tunnel of less than 1-meter in diameter presented no problems during the transfer.

Very small objects were just as troublesome because of size as they were because of mass. They were easily misplaced, hard to contain in multiple quantity, and not amenable to individual restraint. Clothing pockets were the most efficient transport containers for such objects.

Handling Provisions: Small objects and the majority of the medium objects could be handled as whole objects. When attitude control of the item during transfer became a concern, specific handles (preferably as close to the center of gravity as possible) were needed. In those instances in which one handle resulted in awkward handling characteristics, two handles in opposing positions were deemed appropriate.

Logistics management in a weightless environment required thought in both the design of items to be handled and the actual inflight management of these items. Large masses could be easily unstowed, transported, and restowed, provided they did not obstruct the crewmember's view of the translational path or the stowage interface, and provided the crew had sufficient grasp for easy motion initiation, termination, and attitude control.

Maintenance: Because the need for repair-type tasks seemed unlikely, demonstration tasks were proposed to ensure that representative disassembly, changeout, and reassembly-type tasks would be performed in weightlessness. Less than halfway through Skylab-2, the need for creating these demonstration tasks were conclusively removed. Without the inflight maintenance accomplished by each crew, the Skylab Program would have ended.

The most important lesson learned in the area of inflight maintenance was that man could perform maintenance tasks as readily in 0-G as he could on Earth with proper tools, worksites, restraints, accessibility, procedures, and illumination.

Although various unscheduled repair tasks were distributed among all three manned Skylab missions, each mission had a distinct maintenance character of its own. The anomalies associated with the unmanned launch of the OWS left the Skylab-2 crew with the job of repairing the workshop and rendering it habitable. Consequently, much of the Skylab-2 mission was devoted to effecting major spacecraft repairs and putting the ship into proper order for follow-on use. The Skylab-3 crew was then confronted with the majority of the system and experimental "bugs" that required many man-hours of troubleshooting to locate, isolate, and either correct or recommend corrective action. Thus, the Skylab-4 crew inherited numerous servicing and corrective action tasks.

Tools: For man to be as effective as possible in conducting space flight operation and inflight maintenance, the proper tools and test equipment should be included for performing the required tasks. Skylab crewmembers found a use for virtually every tool they had on board and, in some cases, used ingenuity to fabricate a needed part or tool when it was not available. When a screw broke off the bicycle ergometer pedal during the second mission, the sawtooth and file blades of the Swiss Army knife were used to pry and gouge the fractured remains from the hole. An ensuing scavenger hunt eventually located a suitable replacement item from a mounting bracket on one of the spare urine separators. Thus, not only were the tools important but an appropriate inventory of repair consumables was also necessary.

Two primary complaints were voiced by Skylab crewmembers regarding tools. First, all the necessary tools were not included. Second, if a tool was available, it sometimes did not perform well because of safety requirements that had been imposed to render the tools ready for flight. Most tools were stripped of their original finish and replated with safety-acceptable exterior coatings. This resulted in some instances of poor fits between interconnecting elements such as handles, drivers, extensions, and end fittings.

In categorizing items, tools were considered small and, as such, were subject to the handling problems previously discussed for those objects. The prime nuisance was in transporting tools and repair parts from their stowage location to their use location. The tool caddy used was ineffectual for this job, and tools and parts were usually transported in pants pockets.

Worksite: A manned spacecraft should have a dedicated worksite where inflight maintenance tasks could be performed. A workstation could be permanent or portable and should provide restraints for the item being repaired, the tools used, and the loose pieces resulting from disassembly. Skylab had no such workstation; therefore, the crewmembers had to improvise when performing off-line maintenance tasks.

Numerous sites were chosen as temporary workbenches at different times by different Skylab crewmembers. The eating table in the wardroom was a favorite place as a workstation but the compartment was a high-traffic area and the table was not well suited for the restraint of loose items.

During the Skylab-2 mission, docking probe troubleshooting, verification, and checkout tasks required the services of all three crewmembers; one to hold the probe, one to read the procedures, and one to perform the work (which required both hands). A worksite capable of restraining the object of interest, retaining the tools, and holding the checklist would have freed two crewmembers for other activities.

The Skylab-3 crew sometimes chose to work on the top surface of the ventilation cover that housed the ventilator fan and filter for the Waste Management Compartment (WMC). This unit protruded approximately 1 meter above the surface of the grid floor in the forward compartment and was located approximately 1 meter from the front of the rack of food containers, thus presenting a nearby surface for use as an interim stowage area. Pieces of tape were looped to form an adhesive outer surface when affixed to the food containers, thus providing retention of small parts such as screws, nuts, and bolts.

The most effective worksite was improvised by the Skylab-4 crew. They took many of the small off-line repairs and servicing tasks to the air mixing chamber return-air vent screens in the dome of the OWS. The 60 mesh screens had an air velocity of approximately 1.5 meters per second across the surface and were approximately 0.3 square meters in area. This combination of aerodynamic retention and available useable surface area provided for a suitable workbench. A small bin was created by taping cardboard sides approximately 10 centimeters in height to the screen, thus reducing the tendency of small loose items to be dislodged from the screen by either airflow turbulence or an inadvertent bump by the working crewmember. This principle of aerodynamic retention should find application in the design of a workbench for future vehicles.

Accessibility: Some maintenance tasks had to be performed while systems remained on-line because of the nature of the task or the inability to remove the suspect element for off-line inspection and repair. Particularly for these types of tasks, as well as for those in which the necessary component could be removed, it is important that the crewmember have free access to the task. This aspect of maintainability engineering should be addressed during the design of subsystems to ensure the crewmember can get to the job during flight. Accessibility was of interest both inside and outside the spacecraft because some of the most significant maintenance tasks were performed during periods of extravehicular activity (EVA).

The jammed solar array wing was easily freed by the Skylab-2 crew after they gained access to it. Two of the crewmembers spent the major part of an EVA period trying to position one of them to cut the strap that was holding the wing. An access path to the area would have made the task routine.

During another EVA period, the Skylab-4 crew came across the same situation when trying to position themselves to troubleshoot and repair a malfunctioning antenna within the Earth resources array of experiment equipment. They finally accomplished the task but only after expending considerable time and energy. A path to the worksite and proper restraints in the area of the task also would have rendered this task routine.

The discovery of a malfunctioning coolant loop during the Skylab-3 mission led to a time-consuming and complicated troubleshooting activity in search of the leak that was causing the problem. A multitude of high-torque screws had to be removed to open the access panels and to expose the portion of the system in question. The time and effort required to perform this preliminary activity almost resulted in the cancellation of the actual troubleshooting task. With the access route cleared by the Skylab-3 crew, the Skylab-4 crew was able to repair the coolant loop with replacement coolant in a very routine manner, thus not only rendering the subsystem usable again but also demonstrating the feasibility of such servicing operations in weightlessness.

The Skylab-4 crew performed several activities related to accessibility that resulted in marked improvements in the daily living and working routine. By unbolting and removing three sections of flat metal plate from the wardroom floor, they exposed considerably more grid floor for use as a restraint interface, which improved the efficiency of the activities within that compartment. The crew also cut cardboard sections from checklist covers and used them to construct modules within drawers and close-outs between drawers in the film vault. This action solved the problem caused by loosely stowed components in one drawer floating into a position that jammed the operation of the drawer.

As demonstrated repeatedly throughout the Skylab Program, with proper tools, restraints, accessibility, procedures, and an adequate worksite, man can conduct inflight maintenance tasks as effectively on orbit as he can on Earth. This capability should be incorporated into the planning of future programs from the initial design stages to ensure the most effective use of a crewmember's time. If inflight maintenance could be accepted as a nominal activity, perhaps more cost-effective measures could be taken in designing systems, experiments, and equipment.
