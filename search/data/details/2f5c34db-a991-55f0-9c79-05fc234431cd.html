Microgravity Effects on In Vivo Transvascular Transport and Vascular Control

Record ID: NNC04GB41G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
When astronauts are exposed to a microgravity environment there is a characteristic tissue fluid shift from the lower extremities to the upper extremities (cephalad fluid shift), which is reversed during re-entry to normal gravity with concomitant reduction in orthostatic tolerance.. A major fraction of the fluid shift occurs by exchange of water and solutes across the endothelial cell (EC) layer which lines all blood vessels and provides the principal barrier to transport of material between the blood and the underlying tissue. Major alterations in vascular pressure associated with removal (or addition) of the gravitational field changes the transvascular pressure differential, which drives fluid transport between the blood and tissue compartments. The investigators have previously shown, using a tissue culture model of the EC transport barrier in vitro, that a sudden increase in transvascular water flux induces an early adaptive response in which the hydraulic conductivity (water flux / pressure differential) decreases over a one-hour period. This mechanism tends to maintain constant transmural flow in the presence of changing vascular pressure conditions. However, these in vitro experiments have further revealed that beyond a one hour exposure to increased pressure, the hydraulic conductivity begins to increase, and reaches a new steady state level which is significantly elevated after about five hours. Furthermore, this increased hydraulic conductivity can be reversed by pharmacological intervention (inhibition of nitric oxide). The investigators hypothesized that this alteration in hydraulic conductivity in response to altered pressure in microgravity contributes to the fluid shift problem. In this study, they investigated the influence of altered vascular pressure on hydraulic conductivity and albumin permeability of endothelial layers using both in vivo and in vitro models and explored the biomolecular mechanisms underlying these changes.

A second important mechanism which controls transvascular fluid exchange is the regulation of blood flow to the tissue experiencing a change in blood pressure. For example, normal control of fluid transport to leg tissue after standing (which increases blood pressure due to the added gravitational force) is associated with the contraction of arterioles in response to increased pressure (the so-called “myogenic response”). The myogenic response limits blood flow and reduces downstream pressure and available surface area, all of which tend to restrict transvascular exchange. This mechanism prevents significant leg edema when upon standing. The mechanical factors which drive the myogenic response, however, are not well understood. The traditional view has been that increases in smooth muscle cell (SMC) tension in the blood vessel wall driven by increases in pressure stimulate SMC contraction. However, using an in vitro SMC cell culture model, the investigators recently demonstrated that fluid flow shear stress on SMC can also drive significant cellular contraction. This strongly suggests that transmural flow, which imposes fluid shear stress on SMC and is driven by changes in vascular pressure, will affect SMC contraction and the myogenic response. Therefore, the investigators hypothesized that alterations in the myogenic response driven by changes in transvascular flow in altered gravity affect the normal mechanisms which regulate fluid accumulation in tissues. In this study, they looked at the influence of altered vascular pressure on SMC tone using both in vivo and in vitro models. They isolated the contributions of vascular tension and fluid flow shear stress to the overall myogenic response and elucidated the underlying biomolecular mechanisms..

Specific Objective 1. To determine the effects of changes in transvascular pressure on the transport of water and albumin across the endothelial layer. Investigators used in vivo and in vitro models to approach this specific objective. The in vivo studies relied upon the microcirculation of the rat mesentery as the experimental model. Chronic pressure changes were introduced using a micro-perfusion technique and hydraulic conductivity and albumin permeability was measured over a six hour period using the modified Landis technique and fluorescent tracer intensity monitoring. The in vitro studies employed bovine aortic endothelial cells (BAECs) and bovine retinal microvascular endothelial cells (BRECs) grown on porous supports as models of the endothelial transport barrier. Using a unique apparatus developed in the laboratory, Investigators monitored the water flux and albumin flux across the endothelial monolayer simultaneously for six hours after introducing a change in transendothelial pressure. Changes in the tight junctions and adherens junctions between endothelial cells, which are hypothesized to regulate transendothelial transport, were monitored using antibodies to the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin and the adherens junction protein, VE-cadherin. Signal transduction pathways involved in remodeling the tight junctions in response to changes in transmural flow (pressure) were then elucidated.

Specific Objective 2. To determine the effects of changes in transvascular pressure on smooth muscle cell contraction. This specific aim was also approached using both an in vivo and an in vitro model. The in vivo studies determined the mechanisms by which myogenic tone is developed in the arterioles of the rat mesentery after sudden (step) and gradual (ramp) changes in pressure. Particular emphasis was placed on determining the role of transmural interstitial flow in generating myogenic tone. For the first time, investigators measured transmural flow during the myogenic response to determine whether it is regulated. In addition they manipulated transmural flow at a fixed level of pressure change using osmotic agents in order to dissect out, for the first time, the contribution of transmural flow to the development of myogenic tone. The in vitro experiments employed rat aortic SMC in a 3-dimensional collagen gel model through which controlled interstitial flow was perfused. Contraction was observed and quantified under a microscope using video microscopy and image processing, and the biochemical mechanisms mediating the response were probed with a variety of chemical agonists and inhibitors.

RESULTS:
Specific Objective 1. To determine the effects of changes in transvascular pressure on the transport of water and albumin across the endothelial layer.
In response to an increase in intravascular pressure, arteriolar blood vessels will stretch initially, but then constrict in a second phase, which is called the myogenic response. Mechanisms of the myogenic response have not been completely established. The investigators hypothesized that transvascular fluid filtration from plasma across smooth muscle cells and into the surrounding interstitium helps regulate arteriolar myogenic tone, or the state of arteriolar constriction. In their work, arteriolar diameters in the rat mesentery were monitored prior to and following vascular occlusion with a glass micropipette. Arteriolar occlusion not only gave an increase in hydrostatic pressure that initiated myogenic constriction upstream of the pipette, but also allowed measurement of fluid filtration rate by monitoring the movement of vascular red blood cells. A statistically significant correlation (p<0.001) existed between basal myogenic tone and fluid filtration. Additionally, the myogenic response was attenuated by 47 +/- 7% (N=10) when an osmotic solution of albumin, or albumin plus Ficoll, was infused into the bloodstream to decrease fluid filtration by 53 +/- 3%. Moreover, the same inhibition of myogenic tone was found in isolated, cannulated rat soleus muscle arterioles when filtration was osmotically attenuated by intravascular dextran. Taken together, these results are consistent with the hypothesis that shear stress on arteriolar smooth muscle, induced by transvascular fluid filtration, is a contributing factor that helps control myogenic tone.

Specific Objective 2. To determine the effects of changes in transvascular pressure on smooth muscle cell contraction.
The effects of mechanical perturbations (shear stress, pressure) on microvascular permeability primarily have been examined in micropipette-cannulated vessels or in endothelial monolayers in vitro. The objective of this study was to determine whether acute changes in blood flow shear stress might influence measurements of hydraulic conductivity (Lp; permeability of the microvascular wall to water) in autoperfused microvessels in vivo. Rat mesenteric microvessels were observed via intravital microscopy. Occlusion of a third-order arteriole with a micropipette was used to divert and increase flow through a non-occluded capillary or fourth-order arteriolar branch. Transvascular fluid filtration rate in the branching vessel was measured with a Landis technique in which the declining distance between intravascular red blood cells was used to calculate transvascular filtration. Flow (shear)-induced increases in Lp disappeared within 20-30 s of the removal of the shear and could be eliminated with nitric oxide synthase inhibition. The shear-induced increase in Lp was greater in capillaries compared to terminal arterioles. An acute change in shear may regulate Lp by a nitric oxide-dependent mechanism that displays heterogeneity within a microvascular network.

Part of the final study addressed the effect of a sustained change in pressure on microvascular permeability assessed by hydraulic conductivity (Lp) measurements from microvessels of the rat mesentery. Using a micro-perfusion technique, transvascular filtration (normalized to surface area; Jv/S) and Lp were measured in small arterioles (baseline Lp = 0.26 x 10-7 cm/s/cmH2O) and venules (baseline Lp = 2.88 x 10-7 cm/s/cmH2O). The main finding of this study was that step increases in microvascular pressure led to time-dependent alterations of Lp. Immediately after a two-fold step increase in pressure, Jv/S increased in proportion to the pressure change. This observation is consistent with Starling’s Law that predicts filtration proportional to the overall pressure gradient when Lp is constant. However, when Jv/S measurements continued for 60-90 minutes past the step in pressure, there was an initial decrease in Jv/S for 30 min (“sealing effect”) followed by a substantial increase in Jv/S out to 90 minutes. The sustained increase in Jv/S suggests an increase in Lp of 36 +/- 7% for small arterioles and 42 +/- 5% for small venules. In addition, the increase in Lp in response to an increase in pressure was attenuated significantly by nitric oxide synthase (NOS) inhibition. These results indicate that a pressure-induced mechanical stimulus (possibly Jv) activates a nitric oxide-dependent biochemical response that leads to an increase in hydraulic conductivity.
