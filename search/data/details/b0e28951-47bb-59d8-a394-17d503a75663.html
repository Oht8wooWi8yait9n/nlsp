Adaptation to Linear Acceleration After Space Flight

Record ID: DSO 207
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The orientation axis of the vestibular system to gravito-inertial acceleration is important in angular locomotion on Earth, where the center of mass of the body must be aligned with the gravito-inertial acceleration to maintain posture and gait. It is essential to know how and where spatial orientation shifts in space flight, especially when astronauts return to a partial or complete gravitational environment. If the orientation axis of the vestibular system moves to a body axis, it would explain why there is postural imbalance on return to Earth from space flight. An understanding of this factor was necessary in order to develop countermeasures against postural imbalance.

During this study, constant linear acceleration was delivered by centrifugation. Centrifugation has great potential for use as a method to generate artificial linear acceleration, which will become an important issue regarding man's long-term stay in the microgravity environment during the forthcoming Space Station era. It will be considered as a basis for countermeasures against various unfavorable body situations in space, and to prepare the smooth reentry to the earth’s environment.

This Detailed Supplementary Objective (DSO) was performed pre- and postflight to determine how microgravity affects the orientation vectors of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) and of the optokinetic nystagmus (OKN).

APPROACH:
Flight Requirements and Procedures:

a. Preflight: A familiarization session occurred at approximately L-6 months; pretest impressions for a face mold were also be made at this session. There was a maximum of three follow-up sessions to ensure the face mask was comfortable and fit properly; each of these sessions lasted 1.5 hours.

The data collection sessions consisted of measuring eye movements and subjective motion and/or orientation perception during eccentric rotation and during static body tilt. These sessions were held at approximately L-3 months, L-2 months, and L-1 month, and lasted 130 minutes.

During eccentric rotation and during static body tilt the subject was placed in darkness or presented with moving visual scenes (optokinetic stimulation) or moving visual targets (smooth pursuit). Eccentric rotation was generated by a short-arm centrifuge which delivered centrifugal forces of 0.5 g and 1.0 g. Static body tilt was obtained by means of a tilt chair, which allowed a sitting subject to be tested in the upright position, or lying on his left or right side (90° roll tilt), or supine with a 30° head down tilt relative to the horizontal.

b. In-flight: No activity.

c. Postflight: The preflight protocol was repeated at R+1, and at approximately R+2 and R+5. The three sessions lasted 130 minutes.
