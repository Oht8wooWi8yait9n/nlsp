Mechanisms of Microgravity Effect on Vascular Functions

Record ID: NAG91149
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The overall goal of this project was to determine the role of nitric oxide dependent mechanisms in microgravity-induced vascular hyporesponsiveness. In addition, the effects of microgravity on selected vascular signaling pathways were explored. Microgravity was simulated using the classical rodent model, hindlimb unweighting (HU) in the rat. Most experiments involved in vitro studies of blood vessels isolated from control and 20-day HU rats. However, in one important experiment, blood pressure responses to intravenous norepinephrine and intravenous. aminoguanidine, a selective inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) inhibitor, were measured in anesthetized rats.

Furthermore, it was also the goal of this study to identify possible cardiovascular countermeasures. With respect to nitric oxide-dependent mechanisms, this study supports the concept of using selective iNOS inhibition to prevent or reverse orthostatic intolerance. With respect to vascular signaling mechanisms, the results of this study support the concept of using serotonergic agonists as vasoconstrictor agents to reverse orthostatic intolerance.

APPROACH:
HU in rats was used to simulate microgravity. Namely, a harness was placed on the tail of the rat and tethered to the top of the cage. The tether was a shortened sufficiently to elevate the hindlimbs above the cage floor 0.5 cm when the limbs were fully extended. This tilted the body of the rat to a head down angle of 35 degrees with respect to the floor of the cage. The HU rats had full mobility with 360 degree rotation. Animals were subjected to 20 days of HU and were compared with caged controls held for the same time period.

Control and HU rats were euthanized and the abdominal aorta, carotid and femoral arteries were isolated, cleaned and sectioned into three mm rings. In some experiments, the endothelium was removed by mechanically rubbing the luminal surface. Vessel rings were mounted in tissue baths for the measurement of isometric contraction. The agonists, norepinephrine (NE) or serotonin, were added cumulatively in half-log increments to generate concentration-contractile response curves. In some experiments, vessels were precontracted with 10µM phenylephrine, and acetylcholine was added cumulatively to generate concentration-relaxation response curves. Antagonists and inhibitors were added at least 30 minutes prior to the addition of agonists.

The expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), iNOS, neuronal NOS (nNOS) and other intracellular signaling proteins was measured by Western analysis. Briefly, artery rings were homogenized and centrifuged at 12,000 g and proteins in the supernatant were analyzed. This supernatant contained both cytosolic and plasma membrane-bound proteins. The proteins of the supernatant were separated by gel electrophoresis and transferred from the gel onto a chemiluminescence membrane. In turn, this membrane was incubated first with a primary antibody for the protein of interest, and then with secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase. Protein density was detected by the chemiluminescence method and quantified by laser densitometry.

In another experiment control and HU rats were anesthetized and the left carotid artery and jugular vein were cannulated for blood pressure measurement and drug injection, respectively. While the rat remained under anesthesia (Inactin, 100 mg/Kg IP), 0.15 11glkg NE was injected and after 30 minutes, the iNOS inhibitor, 30 mg/Kg aminoguanidine, was injected and blood pressure was measured.

RESULTS:
It was found that endothelium removal restored the NE-induced contraction of HU carotid artery toward that of the control vessel (Figure 1 ). This indicated that HU might upregulate endothelial vasodilator function. Further support for this was obtained by precontracting carotid artery rings with phenylephrine and measuring the endothelium-dependent relaxation to ACh. The HU carotid artery was 10-fold more sensitive to the relaxing effects of ACh than the control artery.

Functional experiments ruled out a role for changes in endothelium in the femoral artery. However, indirect, functional indices of iNOS activity indicated that this NOS isoform was increased in both carotid and femoral arteries and contributed to the contractile hyporesponsiveness of both of these vessels. In contrast, the marked contractile deficit to NE observed in the abdominal aorta was completely independent of oxide mechanisms.

Because HU was shown to upregulate eNOS-dependent vasodilation in the carotid artery, experiments were earned out to determine if endothelial prostaglandin-dependent mechanisms might also be affected by HU. NE concentration response curves were obtained in control and HU carotid arteries in the presence and absence of indomethacin to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis. Indomethacin had no effect in control, but markedly reduced the already depressed contraction to NE in HU carotid arteries. A different cyclooxygenase inhibitor (ibuprofen) and a thromboxane A2-prostaglandin H receptor antagonist (SQ 29548) yielded similar results. On the other hand, removal of the endothelium abolished the effect of indomethacin. This suggests that HU upregulated an endothelium-dependent vasoconstrictor prostaglandin.

The finding that the various isoforms of NOS are upregulated in cardiovascular and noncardiovascular tissues, implied that NOS might be generally upregulated in all arteries. In order to test this, five blood vessels that experience differential hemodynamic changes during HU were studied. Both eNOS and iNOS expression were measured, as were the tissue levels of nitrite and nitrate (NOX), the stable metabolites of nitric oxide. eNOS and iNOS both increased in the thoracic aorta and carotid artery, while NOX did not change significantly. Both NOS isoforms and NOX decreased the femoral artery, but were not changed in the femoral artery. Finally, eNOS and iN OS did not change in the cerebrovasculature, but the stable metabolites of nitric oxide were significantly increased.

Studies in the abdominal aorta ruled out the role for nitric oxide in the HU induced hyporesponsiveness of this vessel. In addition, it was found that while HU reduced the contraction of abdominal aorta to NE, it had no effect on that to serotonin. This suggested the possibility that HU could impair intracellular signaling pathways. This was explored in a presently unpublished series of experiments. Two approaches were taken. First, the effects of selective signal transduction inhibitors on the contraction to NE were explored. For example, the general tyrosine kinase inhibitor, genistein, was found to inhibit contraction to NE in control, but not HU abdominal aorta.
