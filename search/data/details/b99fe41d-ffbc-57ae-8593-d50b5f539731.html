Visuo-Motor Coordination during Space Flight

Record ID: 9301008
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Astronauts have reported that visuo-motor performance degrades early in a space mission. During later stages of the flight, gross motor skills fully recover, while fine motor skills remain disturbed. Little quantitative data is available on visuo-motor performance in weightlessness. During the brief microgravity episodes of parabolic flight, individuals performing simple pointing movements, while prevented from viewing their hands, produced movements that were consistently deviated upwards. Pointing responses produced after several hours of Space Shuttle flight exhibited no such bias. In contrast, performance in a demanding compensatory-tracking exercise remained degraded throughout a 13-day mission.

The Visuo-Motor Coordination During Space Flight experiment was one of three Sensory Motor and Performance investigations performed during the 1998 Neurolab mission. By identifying and analyzing motor-skill compensation and motor-skill deficiencies, investigators will learn more about the dependence of human motor functions on normal gravity and the adaptation to weightlessness. Furthermore, this will extend the knowledge of how prolonged exposure to microgravity impacts sensory motor performance. Information to be gained from this experiment is important to investigators who seek to better understand the role of gravity as a fundamental reference frame for spatial orientation and the adaptive capabilities of the human visuo-motor system. The information is also of potential value to scientists seeking insight into the limitations of visuo-motor performance in humans on Earth. In addition, testing devices, such as those developed for this experiment, will be valuable for assessing motor disorders in Earth-based patients.

APPROACH:
Using the Visuo-Motor Coordination Facility (VCF), investigators assessed changes in the eye-hand coordination of astronauts during their adaptation to microgravity. As the VCF projected visual targets onto a screen, astronauts pointed at the targets as they moved from place to place, grasped at them as they changed in size, and tracked them as they moved in a circle. During the test, the subject wore a specially designed glove that allowed precise tracking of hand movements and response times. The glove, equipped with light emitting diodes (LEDs) at the thumb and index fingertips, provided a trail that was recorded by the VCF. Other hardware included two cameras, a video-analysis system based on the Sharp-CORE board, and a PC compatible computer. The computer reconstructed 3-D locations from 2-D data and stored the results on a disk; it also generated the stationary and moving targets of various sizes and shapes that the subject tracked on the screen.

The Visuo-Motor Coordination (VMC) of Neurolab crewmembers was evaluated at 30, 29, 15 and 14 days before launch, on Flight Days 1, 7, and 14 and on the day of landing, as well as 2 and 9 days after landing to detect changes in performance and chronicle the adaptation of the nervous system as the subject adjusted to microgravity. During the test sessions, subjects performed exercises including double-step pointing, double-step grasping, circular tracking, and a group of reaction time tasks. During double-step pointing, the subject pointed an index finger at sequentially presented visual targets that suddenly changed location. For double-step grasping, the subject used the thumb and index fingertips to grasp at a target that, in a percentage of trials, changed size. For circular tracking, the subject tracked targets that moved in circular paths with the index finger. For the reaction time tasks, the subject quickly pressed the hand reaction switch when the targets changed. Reaction time tasks included predictive hand tracking in a reaction-time task, predictive eye tracking in a reaction time task, and a general reaction-time task. For the Predictive Hand Tracking in a Reaction-Time task, the subject tracked, by hand, a target moving along a predictable trajectory. For the Predictive Eye Tracking in a Reaction-Time task, the subject visually tracked a target moving along a predictable trajectory. For the general Reaction-Time task, the subject observed a stationary target in the center of the computer screen.

RESULTS:
Twelve of 12 nominally scheduled sessions were performed by the payload crewmembers. Five additional sessions were performed by the Orbiter crewmembers. Data appear to be of good quality based on initial evaluation while the hardware performed flawlessly. Pointing responses were slowed distinctly in microgravity, but the speed of tracking movements was not affected. The secondary task had comparable effects on tracking performance in space and on Earth.
