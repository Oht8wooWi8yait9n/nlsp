Robust Human-System Interface Design for Spaceflight-Induced Environments

Record ID: NCC958SA02002
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The next generation of human-rated space flight vehicles will expose astronauts to altered gravito-inertial forces and elevated vibration levels during launch and landing that can affect their ability to perform their assigned tasks. Furthermore, current plans call for crew action in critical tasks, including the override of flight automation in off-nominal conditions, while the crew may be exposed to four times normal gravity or more combined with vibration levels potentially higher than those experienced during Apollo and Shuttle. The risk associated with these dynamic phases of flight becomes even more acute when critical tasks are performed by de-conditioned crew returning after a long-duration space flight. Altered gravitational environments experienced in spacecraft impact human manual and gaze control and high-frequency vibration is also a serious challenge to visuomotor performance and gaze control. Unfortunately, the current quantitative knowledge of these effects is too limited to develop and validate operationally relevant predictive models of crew performance of sufficient reliability to support interface design or requirement verification. In particular, the interactions between increased gravity and vibration are poorly understood. The goal of the proposed research is to measure, analyze, and model human performance in display navigation and flight control tasks during centrifugation and vibration (in isolation and combination) to simulate ascent/descent conditions of space flight.

The proposed research will determine the impacts of transient and sustained acceleration on human sensorimotor performance. The benefit of this research for NASA is that it will fill a critical knowledge gap that is currently hampering the optimal design of astronaut and pilot displays and interfaces needed to maximize performance and safety and minimize human error. The benefit of this research for humanity above and beyond enabling safer and more productive space exploration is two-fold. First, the human performance databases (and models) generated will allow for better design of aircraft and other vehicles thereby ultimately facilitating the enhancement of safety for the traveling public by supporting improved cockpit/interface design and training. Second, the performance assessment technologies developed and validated in this project may have medical applications for enhancing diagnostic methods to identify sensorimotor deficits due to disease or injury.

APPROACH:
The proposed research aimed to measure performance (and associated workload) in operationally relevant display navigation and manual control tasks under simulated dynamic phases of flight and will compare the effectiveness of a variety of potential display and interface configurations. These data will enable investigators to identify and model the human capabilities and limitations in order to provide validated quantitative design guidelines for displays and controls to support crew-cockpit design engineers and operations/training development. This research will enable the development of validated spacecraft interface design guidelines and human-system verification tools with the ultimate goal of reducing mission risks and enhancing safety.

RESULTS:
Investigators measured the effects of elevated Gx-loads and vibration on human oculomotor, manual, and autonomic behavior to assess the impacts on vestibular, visual, and motor function and workload of induced environments representative of dynamic phases of flight. They found that gaze stabilization (via the Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex) is not the limiting factor for sensorimotor control with Gx loads up to 3.8Gx and vibration levels of 0.5g at up to 16 Hz. They found that retinal blur is typically about 0.25 degrees or less, indicating that the combined linear and translational Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex is nearly perfectly compensatory, even under challenging Gx-load and vibration conditions.

By comparing open-loop vs. closed-loop saccadic and reach performance, investigators found that:
1. 3.8-Gx loading significantly increases systematic errors for both saccades and manual reaches, compared to performance at 1 Gx.
2. Both saccadic and reaching accuracy and precision improved under closed-loop conditions as compared to open-loop conditions, indicating that oculomotor and manual control performance is visually limited under open-loop conditions (because of visual spatial uncertainty).
3. Vibration significantly decreases the precision of both saccades and reaches.
4. There is a significant correlation between the errors in eye and reach end points, indicating that at least some of the error is due to shared neural noise in visual localization for both oculomotor and manual control.

By comparing pursuit tracking performance in an upright (+1 Gz) vs. a supine (+1 Gx) posture, and under low (+1 Gx) vs. high (+3.8 Gx) G-loads, investigators found that visual motion processing is largely robust to altered G conditions with some exceptions:
1. The cardinal-oblique anisotropy (better performance for target motion along the cardinal directions than for those along the oblique direction) is insensitive to changes in the direction and magnitude of the G vector;
2. However, the horizontal-vertical asymmetry (typically higher direction gains for vertical than for horizontal target motion) is significantly flattened at 3.8 vs. 1 Gx, showing that this visual processing non-linearity is due to a different (possibly cortical) mechanism that is modulated by vestibular cues.

Elevated Gx-loads and vibration generate changes in Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) function, consistent with increased workload:
1. Both heart and breathing rates are significantly elevated during exposure to +3.8 Gx, as compared to +1.0 Gx.
2. There is a correlation between heart and breathing rate and response time showing that ANS effects are linked to task performance.
3. Heart and breathing are significant altered by vibration frequency, although dramatically differently for the two Gx levels.
4. This observed non-linear interaction between Gx-loading and vibration confirms why one must measure them in combination, as what was have done in this project.
