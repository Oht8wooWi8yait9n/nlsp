Summary / Description

Shuttle mission STS-77, launched on May 19, 1996, was devoted to continuing the effort to help open the commercial space frontier. Primary objectives during the flight included microgravity research aboard the commercially owned and operated Spacehab Module, deployment and retrieval of a research satellite, and rendezvous operations with a test satellite. The Shuttle crew included Commander John H. Casper, Pilot Curtis L. Brown, and Mission Specialists Daniel W. Bursch, Mario Runco, Jr., Marc Garneau, and Andrew S. W. Thomas.

The Spacehab single module carried nearly 3,000 pounds of experiments and support equipment for 12 commercial space product development payloads in the areas of biotechnology, electronic materials, polymers and agriculture, as well as other space life sciences payloads, including the Aquatic Research Facility (ARF). ARF was a joint Canadian Space Agency/NASA project that allowed scientists to investigate the process of fertilization, embryo formation, the development of calcified tissue, and feeding behaviors of small aquatic organisms in microgravity. This research may possibly improve the way scientists model human development, and identify factors that may disrupt such development.

Other life sciences payloads included the Immune Response Experiment (Immune-3), Biological Research in a Canister (BRIC-07) and the National Institutes of Health-Cells 7 (NIH.C7). Immune-3 was designed to test the ability of insulin-like growth factor to prevent or reduce the detrimental effects of space flight on the immune and skeletal systems of rats. Together with extensive ground-based research, the knowledge acquired here may be used to develop protocols for protecting the immune systems of patients undergoing chemotherapy and those with a broad range of other infectious diseases.

BRIC-07 investigated the metamorphosis period of the tobacco hornworm, specifically the synthesis of protein necessary to form muscle. This study was designed to clarify mechanisms behind the endocrine system in insects, potentially aiding in endocrine system research in general, including those of humans subjected to microgravity.

NIH.C7 continued the collaboration between NASA and the National Institutes of Health. NIH.C7 was a middeck-locker experiment that repeated and built upon previous experiments investigating the effect of space flight on musculoskeletal development at a cellular level. Results from this research may lead to the development of measures to maintain the strength of muscles and bones during long-duration space voyages and may provide insights and health benefits for people on Earth as well.

Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs) were also performed on this mission. A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation that is performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies are designed to require minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

Other payloads on board STS-77 included the Advanced Separation Process for Organic Materials, Commercial Generic Bioprocessing Apparatus, Plant Generic Bioprocessing Apparatus, Fluids Generic Bioprocessing Apparatus, Commercial Protein Crystal Growth, Gas Permeable Polymer Membrane, Handheld Diffusion Test Cell, Commercial Float Zone Furnace, and the Space Experiment Facility.

On May 29, 1996, after a mission duration of ten days, Endeavour landed at Kennedy Space Center (KSC) in Florida.
