Microgravity Effects on Standardized Cognitive Performance Measures

Record ID: 8913063
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Space travelers are subject to a variety of stresses during space flight. These include adapting to the microgravity environment, physical isolation, confinement, lack of privacy, fatigue and changing work/rest cycles. On Earth, both fatigue and changing work/rest cycle degrade cognitive performance and productivity. The purpose of this experiment was to determine the effects of microgravity upon cognitive skills critical to the success of operational tasks in space. Scientists have hypothesized that performance will deteriorate as a result of time in microgravity and that fatigue will increase concurrently.

APPROACH:
The subjects for the Microgravity Effects on Standardized Cognitive Performance Measures experiment were four crewmembers of the seven-person crew on Space Shuttle flight STS-78. A preflight orientation session provided subjects with background information and a demonstration of the cognitive test battery known as the NASA Performance Assessment Battery, or NASA-PAB. Data were collected from each subject during 37 experiment sessions: 24 preflight sessions, 9 inflight sessions performed approximately every other day, and 4 postflight sessions also performed every other day. A total of 1183 data sets were recorded for the experiment.

To evaluate any changes in cognitive performance and productivity, the investigators developed a test battery consisting of a questionnaire and several performance tests. Each test subject performed the battery at one sitting, meaning that no breaks were allowed once the test was started. A brief description of each performance test and its duration follows.

Mood Scale - This task consisted of 8 scales and involved pressing an arrow key to indicate the level of agreement with the mood description associated with each scale. The test took less than 1 minute to perform.
Fatigue Scale - This task required the subject to press a numbered key to indicate which statement best matches the subject's fatigue state. The task took less than 30 seconds to perform.
Tracking - One of the effects of microgravity is a disruption of visual-motor coordination. To investigate this effect, a tracking test was used. The test required the subject to maintain a target in the center of a horizontal line. The target was displaced by the computer program at a fixed difficulty rate, and the subject had to manipulate a control device to null the input disturbance.
Matrix Rotation Task - The functional deafferentation of the otolith organs that occurs in space, along with associated effects on the visual and cerebellar systems, raise serious questions about the individual's spatial oreintation. Thus, it is desirable to look for subtle effects on a subject's ability to perceive, remember and process spatial information. The Matrix Rotation task is used to evaluate the effects of microgravity on spatial information processing. The test used 100 basic patterns, each of which was a 5 x 5 matrix with five illuminated cells that had been selected at random. At the beginning of the trial, the subject saw a pattern. The subject studied the pattern and then pressed a response key. The pattern was immediately erased and a new one presented. The subject was asked to decide as quickly as possible if the new pattern was identical to the first pattern. The subject then pressed a designated key for "same" or another designated key for "different". As soon as the response was made, a third pattern appeared. Now, the subject had to compare the new pattern to the immediately preceding pattern, etc. For "same" responses, the two patterns were never presented in exactly the same orientation; the second pattern was always rotated either 90° to the left of 90° to the the right relative to the preceding pattern. Both reaction time and percentage-of-corrections-made were recorded. The self-paced test lasts about 1.5 minutes.
Sternberg Memory Search - The general Sternberg paradigm required subjects to respond as rapidly and accurately as possible to visually presented letters. At the beginning of the test, a set of letters drawn randomly from the alphabet was presented to the subject for memorization. Next, the screen was cleared and a series of single test letters were presented. If the presented letter matched one of the letters in the previously memorized positive set, the subject responded "same" (key press). If a different letter appeared (negative set), then the subject responded "different" (key press) indicating a non-matching letter was presented. The self-paced test lasts 2 minutes.
Continuous Recognition - One critical aspect of higher cognitive function is the ability to maintain attention and to carry out repetitive cognitive processes over some period of time. In many ways, such activities encompass those that were traditionally referred to as "vigilance". However, they add the dimension of active processing of information, rather than simple monitoring. One task that appears to capture the performance elements above is the Continuous Recognition Test. In this test, the subject saw two numbers, one above the other. The task was to remember the bottom number. When the next two numbers appeared, the task was to determine if the new top number was the same as the previous bottom number. However, before responding, the subject must note the new bottom number because as soon as a response was made, the numbers were replaced by a new pair. Thus, the subject must not only exercise very short-term memory, but, more importantly, must inhibit the response until the new button number ws committed to memory. The appropriate strategy was to develop a set pattern of observing, memorizing, observing, comparing and responding. This sequence was different from that required by most routine tasks in that it required constant attention allocation. Even brief lapses resulted in errors. The task could be made even more difficult by requiring the subject to remember and respond to numbers further removed (two or even three back) from the one immediately proceeding, thus imposing a much higher load on immediate memory. The self-paced test lasted about 2 minutes.
Performance Switching Task - Manikin and Mathematical Processing - Time-sharing was different from another required attention process that could be affected by microgravity. Astronauts must make rapid shifts in the attentional focus, as well as in the skills required to respond to a change in task demands. This externally-directed behavior cannot be made automatic in any true sense, since it must be flexible enough to respond to unusual demands. Thus, a test was needed to probe the subject's ability to shift attention and resource allocation in response to rapidly changing and unpredictable external demands.

In this procedure, the subject had to discrete tasks to perform. One was a spatially-based task, and the other was a mathematically-based test. Each of these appeared, trial by trial, simultaneously on the screen. However, an indicator appeared at the same time, directing the subject to the task that was "active" (i.e., must be responded to). The subject had to made an exclusive response to the active task, where reaction time and percent-correct data were obtained only for that task. The switching from task to task for each trial was random (within constraints). Therefore, the subject had to remember to watch the indicator on each trial, allocate the appropriate resources to respond to the trial, and then make the appropriate response. This paradigm provided a test of switching skills.

The two tests selected to exercise this paradigm were the manikin test and the mathematical processing test. As implemented in this microgravity experiment, a manikin "stick figure" was presented facing either forward or backward. In addition, the figure could be either upright or upside-down. The figure was also standing on a box and inside the box was either a rectangle or a circle. In the figure's two hands were a rectangle and a circle. The subject's task was to note which symbol was inside the box, and then to determine which of the manikin's hands was holding the designated symbol. The subject then presses the left or right of two keys corresponding to the manikin's left or right hand.

Exposing the astronauts to a series of manikins presented in a variety of orientations in microgravity, where the astronaut had the freedom to position his own body during work and sleep in non-traditional gravity orientations, was one of the more theoretically interesting tests of spatial processing using a human form. The effects of microgravity on whole-body orientation may give insight into whether this test was a perceptual measure of spatial transformation of mental images or involved pure spatial abilities requiring readaptation.

The Mathematical Processing test was based on similar tasks. It presented three single-digit numbers that must be added or subtracted. If the answer was greater than 5, one response was given. If the answer was less than 5, another response was required. This task had been reported to be a relatively pure index of mathematical functioning. The computer-paced test lasted 4 minutes.

Dual Tasking - Tracking and Sternberg Memory Search - One of the most critical and potentially sensitive higher cognitive functions that might be affected by microgravity is the ability of the subject to allocate attentional resources among several tasks. To investigate this, the task used the time-sharing paradigm which consisted of the Sternberg task and tracking task being presented simultaneously. In the implementation of the dual task, the tracking task was presented in the middle of the screen and the letters of the Sternberg task appeared in a fixed location directly about the center null point. The target of the compensatory tracking risk moved laterally. One memory set was used, consisting of 4 letters. Due to the nature of the dual task, the "fixed set" procedure was used, in which the same memory set letters were presented with several probe letters for each daily session. The test lasted 3 minutes.

RESULTS:
With only four subjects it is not possible to generalize results to a population of future space travelers. However, if one confines their analysis and interpretation to the four subjects tested in this 16-day mission, some tentative conclusions can be drawn. Generally, in-Qrbit subjects were able to respond to the tests without significant impairment. The mathematical processing task, a part of the Directed Attention Test, showed significantly degraded performance in two out of the four subjects. The Matrix test performance was degraded in one subject. The improvement seen in the Tracking test for one subject appeared to be the result of an improved tracking strategy developed in the last two practice sessions prior to launch.
