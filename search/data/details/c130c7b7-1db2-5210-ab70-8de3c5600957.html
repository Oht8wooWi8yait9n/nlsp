Viral Reactivation

Record ID: 2.4.3
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Seven or more herpesviruses commonly establish infections in humans. Herpes simplex type-1 (HSV-1), herpes simplex type-2 (HSV-2), cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), human herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6) and human herpesvirus-7 (HHV-7) are some examples of the double stranded DNA viruses that may establish a life-long latent infection in a host organism. Latent viruses are subject to intermittent reactivation. For example, replication of the reactivated herpesvirus genome may give rise to both infectious and noninfectious (defective) virus particles. Since one or more of the herpesviruses infects nearly 100% of the adult population, they are prime candidates for studying the effects of space flight on latent viral reactivation in astronauts.

Latent virus reactivation may be an important threat to crew health during extended space missions where crewmembers live and work in a closed environment for 3 months or more. Exposure to infectious agents such as bacteria, fungi, viruses and parasites can be controlled by preflight quarantine as opposed to latent virus reactivation, an infection that originates inside the host organism. The establishment of viral latency and the subsequent reactivation are not well understood, but changes in the immune system are known to increase the incidence and duration of viral reactivation and shedding. Increasing scientific data indicates that the physical and psychological stressors associated with space flight cause alterations in the human immune response. Therefore, the objectives of this experiment were to evaluate the effects of long-duration habitation in space on latent virus reactivation, indicative of perturbations to the intricate host-parasite relationships. In this experiment, scientists tested the hypothesis that stressors associated with space flight will alter the host-parasite relationship in crewmembers inhabiting the Mir space station during extended stays, increasing both the incidence and the duration of herpesvirus reactivation and shedding.

APPROACH:
The approach to this experiment was to assess viral reactivation by the collection of serum and saliva samples. The original plan for sample collection included both the Mir 18 and 19 crew. The inflight and postflight sampling sessions on Mir 18 were not completed due to a cancellation of this experiment. Preflight saliva samples were obtained, but no blood samples were collected. However, contingency saliva sampling was performed on one crewmember during the flight. Preflight, inflight, and postflight saliva samples were collected from two crewmembers of the Mir 19 mission. Detection of the herpesvirus reactivation and shedding was conducted by saliva sample collection, DNA extraction from saliva, Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) amplification of herpesvirus DNA, and analysis of PCR products by gel electrophoresis.

DNA extraction from saliva
Saliva was collected and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes for extraction. DNA was extracted using a modification of the RNAzol method. Genomic/viral DNA was isolated simultaneously by adding 0.75 ml of Tris-saturated phenol (pH 6.6) RNAzol solution to 250 microliters saliva and briefly vortexing. After incubating on ice for 15 minutes, the tubes were centrifuged for 15 minutes at 12,000 x g and stored at -20 degrees Celsius overnight. The tubes were then centrifuged for 30 minutes at 16,000 x g and the supernatant removed. The DNA was then washed once with 7% ethanol and resuspended in 20 microliters DEPC-treated water. The suitability of extraction was evaluated by gel electrophoresis without nucleic acid quantitation.

PCR Amplification of Herpesvirus DNA
The oligonucleotide primers selected for this investigation amplified a highly conserved region of the DNA polymerase gene from the herpesvirus group. These primers allowed amplification of DNA to be directed to selected nucleotide fragments of the DNA polymerase genes for HSV-1, HSV-2, CMV and EBV . DNA was amplified in a total volume of 25 ml or a reaction mixture consisting of 50 mM KCl, 10mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.3), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.01% gelatin, 5% dimethylsulfoxide, 200 micromolar deoxynucleoside triphosphates, 20 pmol of each oligonucleotide primer, 0.625 U Taq polymerase, and 2 ml purified DNA solution. The reactions were performed in an automated thermocycler. The cycle, repeated 40 times, consisted of 15 seconds of denaturation at 96 degrees Celsius, 15 seconds of annealing at 60 degrees Celsius, and 15 seconds of elongation at 72 degrees Celsius. All samples were run with simultaneous positive control and negative (reagent) control. Measures to control contamination included the use of latex gloves, aerosol-resistant pipette tips, and two separate laboratories for preamplification and postamplification procedures.

Analysis of PCR Products
Ten microliters of the reaction mixture was run on a 2% Nusieve GTG agarose gel containing 1 microgram of ethidium bromide per ml in 1x TBE buffer. The gels were then placed under UV light for visualization of the amplified products and photographed.

Results:
The cancellation of this experiment on Mir 18 resulted in a limited sample collection. Inflight and postflight samples were not collected from the Mir 18 crewmembers. Preflight samples were collected from three crewmembers and five inflight samples were collected from one crewmember as contingency samples. Therefore, the only samples analyzed were those collected from the crewmember that participated in the inflight collection. The samples collected from the other crewmembers are archived for future analysis. All of the preflight samples were negative for the presence of herpesvirus DNA. However, two of the five inflight samples were positive for the presence of herpesvirus DNA. Since these data were very limited, no conclusions were made concerning the effects of microgravity on the host-parasite relationship. However, the methodology and sample handling logistics were successful.

Two cosmonauts, both backup crewmembers for Mir 18, participated in the viral reactivation study on the Mir 19 mission. Thus, the preflight saliva specimens collected to establish baseline shedding patterns were collected prior to the Mir 18 mission. Saliva specimens were collected before, during, and after the flight. The samples were analyzed for the presence of Herpes simplex type-1 (HSV 1) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). All specimens were negative for HSV 1. Unfortunately, preflight saliva specimens were insufficient in number and frequency to establish a sound baseline for either crewmember. However, preflight shedding of EBV in the saliva was detected in over half of the total specimens. EBV DNA were detected in the saliva of crewmember A before, during, and after the flight. At least six episodes of shedding of EBV were observed during the mission. Inflight saliva specimens were collected beginning at about FD 25 and ending on FD 75. Shedding was also observed on R+14 and R+24. Crewmember B shedded EBV much less frequently during the mission than crewmember A. EBV DNA was observed in saliva from crewmember B on only two occasions during the mission. The conclusions made from this experiment were: (1) PCR methodology was capable of detecting latent viral DNA in saliva specimens collected and stored on the ground and on Mir, (2) neither crewmember appeared to be shedding HSV 1, (3) both crewmembers shed EBV before, during and after the mission, (4) shedding patterns of EBV of the two crewmembers were highly different during the inflight phase, (5) the technical feasibility of the approach was clearly demonstrated. The low number of subjects prevented any major conclusions from being drawn related to host-parasite relationships during space flight.
