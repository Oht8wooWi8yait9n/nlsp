Reactivation of Latent Viruses During the Lunar-Mars Life Support Test Project

Record ID: LMLSTP5.3
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Reactivation of latent viruses may pose an important health risk for people living and working in extreme environments, such as space and Antarctica. Risks associated with many infectious agents are reduced by the preventative measures of the health stabilization program before each mission. However, reactivation of latent viruses is unaffected by such actions. A variety of stess factors may cause reactivation of these viruses, such as psychosocial stress, trauma, sunlight, resiratory infection, and fever. Stress-induced changes in immune function under such conditions may also increase the incidence and duration of viral reactivation and shedding.

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a DNA virus that infects more than 90% of the adult population worldwide and is the etiologic agent of infectious mononucleosis, is highly infectious. Normally, primary infection is a self-limiting disease leading to a lifelong persistence of EBV in the B-lymphocytes or epithelial cells of the oropharynx of infected individuals. EBV can be found in saliva after reactivation, which can be triggered by a variety of factors, perhaps including space flight. The physical and psychological stresses associated with the launch and landing of spacecraft, and living and working in the crowded, closed environment in microgravity, may result in viral reactivation and shedding.

The limited access to space requires the use of ground-based analogs of space flight. Environmental chambers have been used since the Skylab program (1973-1974) to simulate specific aspects of space flight. In the present study, the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to identify EBV DNA in saliva and CMV DNA in urine as a measure of viral shedding in response to 60-day (Phase IIa) and 91-day (Phase III) chamber studies of the Lunar-Mars Life Support Test Project (LMLSTP) at NASA Johnson Space Center.

APPROACH:
Viral shedding and reactivation patterns were studied for eight subjects (five male and three female) participating in the 60-day (Phase IIa) and 91-day (Phase III) chamber studies of the Lunar-Mars Life Support Test Project (LMLSTP) at NASA Johnson Space Center. Saliva samples were collected from the eight subjects before, during, and after chamber isolation. Blood and urine samples were collected once before entry and once after exit.

Samples from 11 healthy age-matched adults were collected as controls (ten males and one female). Saliva, urine, and blood samples (one of each) were collected from each of these subjects on day 1, day 7, and day 22 of the tests. This collection schedule closely approximates the pre- and postflight collection schedule of a 12-day Shuttle flight. One-time urine samples were also collected from 30 additional healthy age-matched subjects for CMV analysis.

Saliva samples were collected with Salivette kits, which consist of a cotton roll in a polypropylene vial. Subjects placed the roll in their mouth until it became saturated, and then returned the roll to the vial. Saliva samples were centrifuged immediately after collection and stored frozen at -70ºC. All samples collected from a given subject were analyzed simultaneously. Using a polymerase chain reaction assay, saliva samples were processed for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA and urine samples were analyzed for cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNA detection. Saliva (2 to 3 ml) and urine (3 ml) specimens were concentrated with a 100-KD filtration unit. DNA was extracted by a nonorganic extraction method, and EBV DNA was detected as described here briefly. The PCR primers were directed at the EBV polymerase accessory protein gene (BMRF1): P1, 5-GTC CAA GAG CCACCACAC CTG and P2, 5-biotin CCC AGA AGT ATA CGT GGT GAC GTA GA. These primers were used at a concentrationof 200 µM with 10 µM deoxynucleic acid triphosphates. PCR was optimized using buffer II with 2.5 mM MgCl2. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to a final concentration of 5%. AmpliGold (2.5 units per 100 µl reaction mixture) was added, and 5 µl of the purified DNA was added to 20 µl of the reaction mixture. The cycle parameters were 95ºC for 9 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94ºC for 15 sec, 61ºC for 15 sec, and 72ºC for 15 sec, with a final extension step at 72ºC for 5 min.

CMV DNA was detected using primers that target the major immediate early gene (P1, 5-TGT CCT CCC GCT CCT C and P2, biotin 5-ATG AAG GTC TTT GCC CAG TA). All reactions were carried out using a Perkin-Elmer GenAmp system 9600. An initial denaturation step of 94ºC for 9 min was followed by 40 cycles of 94ºC for 30 sec, 69ºC for 15 sec, 72ºC for 30 sec, 72ºC for 7 min, and a 4ºC hold. The amplified product was analyzed for the presence of EBV or CMV using the Digene Sharp Signal System according to the manufacturer's instructions.

EBV and CMV antibody titers from blood samples were determined by indirect immunofluorescence assay. Commercially prepared substrate slides and control sera were used for determining antibody (IgG) titers for viral capsid antigen (VCA) and early antigen (EA) of EBV, CMV, and measles (Bion Enterprises, Park Ridge, IL). Four-fold dilutions of plasma were prepared with phosphate-buffered saline. The endpoint titer was determined as the highest dilution of serum in which immunofluorescent-positive cells could be detected. All specimens were batch analyzed and read blind-coded.

The frequency of viral shedding in saliva and viral antibody titers in plasma from chamber study subjects and controls were tested for normality. One-way analysis of variance was performed to study significant differences across different times during the study.

The method of generalized estimating equations with a logit link was used to find significant difference between the phases (pre-, in-, and postchamber). The differences between sampling periods were considered significant if P < 0.05.

RESULTS:
A total of 418 saliva samples were collected from the eight subjects participating in the two chamber studies. Overall, EBV DNA was detected in 35% of the 418 total saliva samples collected from these subjects. The subjects in the 60-day chamber study had EBV DNA in 13% (range 0 to 31%) of their samples, and the subjects in the 91-day study had EBV DNA in 51% (range 33 to 81%) of their samples. During both studies, all but one subject (in the 60-day chamber study) shed EBV.

Sixteen urine samples collected from the subjects of both chamber studies before and after the in-chamber phase were analyzed for CMV DNA. Interestingly, not a single sample showed evidence of CMV DNA. The presence of CMV DNA in urine from the control group was also rare (1/81 samples).

Viral antibody titers for EBV and CMV measured in samples from all 8 subjects before and after the study were not significantly different. Therefore, the data were pooled for further analysis. No significant differences in EBV VCA, EBV EA, or CMV IgG antibody titers were observed before and after either of the studies. Interestingly, EBV antibody titers did not differ significantly from the controls. However, CMV antibody titers of subjects were greater than those of the control group before and after the study.

Our current finding of increased viral reactivation in closed chamber study subjects agrees with our previous Space Shuttle and Antarctic data. These results support and extend our previous observations that latent viral reactivation increases during space flight, which is consistent with a stress-induced decrease in immune function.
