Urine Monitoring Investigation

Record ID: UMS
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This investigation was designed to evaluate the Urine Monitoring System (UMS) and to monitor the fluid balance and urine biochemistry of the crew. The primary objectives of the Urine Monitoring Investigation were (1) to verify the operation of the UMS in the collection and sampling of urine in a zero gravity environment, (2) to perform an inflight calibration of the UMS, (3) to estimate the crewmembers’ ratio of fluid intake to urine output, and (4) to study the effects of microgravity on various biochemical constituents of urine. The data from the volume measurements and biochemical analysis of inflight samples will help extend the present understanding of the adaptive changes which affect the fluid, electrolyte, renal, and circulatory status of humans exposed to microgravity during space flight. Subsequent dedicated life sciences missions will incorporate the UMS in support of a number of experiments directed at studying the fluid redistribution hypothesis. This hypothesis focuses on the idea that three primary organ systems, namely, the circulatory system, the renal system, and the endocrine system are involved with the sudden loss of fluids and electrolytes that occurs during the immediate inflight period.

APPROACH:
The experiment protocol included preflight, inflight, and postflight procedures involving calibration and sampling activities. The preflight procedures included functional and calibration testing of the UMS in addition to several measurements of residual flush water. Preflight collection of urine samples started two days prior to launch and continued through the launch date. The inflight protocol consisted of the following activities: Calibration, Residual Volume Determination, Urine Volumetric and Sampling Measurements and Urine Volumetric Measurements. Premeasured aliquots of water and a dilute lithium chloride solution were used for the calibration and residual volume measurements, respectively. The calibration aliquots were divided into six groups which ranged in size from 60 to 720 grams. The residual volume (residual volume of flush water) determination used 180 gram aliquots and was essentially a cross-contamination check between sampling operations. Postflight analysis was performed on two samples taken from each of the residual volume measurements to determine the amount of dilution caused by rinse operations. The protocol for the urine volumetric measurements required crew members to record all water and food consumption in order to monitor their total water intake. The samples obtained from inflight urinations were preserved in a refrigerator/freezer and returned for postflight biochemical analysis. The postflight protocol called for volumetric measurements and sampling operations to be performed immediately after landing and for 3 days thereafter for comparison to preflight and inflight samples.

RESULTS:
The primary objectives involving the verification of operation, inflight calibration, and residual volume determinations were completed for this investigation. Twenty-four usable data points were obtained from the inflight calibration procedures. These points were used to establish a calibration curve which could be used to convert the UMS readout to mass (grams) for an unknown quantity of fluid introduced into the device. The confidence limits on the masses estimated by this procedure of inverse prediction were established by standard statistical methods. The 95 percent confidence interval averages ± 8 percent of the estimated mass while the 50 percent confidence interval averages ± 2 percent of the estimated mass. The mean residual volume estimated from the eight residual volume determinations was 25.9 ml with a standard error of 1.8 ml. This indicated that more time is necessary to flush the fluid lines in the UMS prior to taking a sample. Values for the physiological parameters of the urine samples were corrected to account for the residual volume dilution factor. Urine samples were collected for three days preflight and for four days postflight, however, the inflight protocol was limited to eight samples collected by a single crewmember over a 25-hour period. Operational difficulties involving insufficient air flow through the UMS prevented the inflight collection/sampling portion of the protocol from being completed. All samples were subjected to biochemical analysis. Biochemical analyses investigated changes in osmolality and differences in levels of creatinine, sodium, potassium, and chloride. Endocrine measurements investigated changes in levels of aldosterone and cortisol.
