Circadian Entrainment, Sleep-Wake Regulation and Performance during Space Flight

Record ID: NSBRI_5047
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The purpose of the study was to test three specific hypotheses aimed at evaluating entrainment of the human circadian pacemaker to longer-than-24-hour days. These hypotheses were based on the results of the investigators’ preliminary data which indicate that: (a) the period of the human circadian pacemaker after release from entrainment to the 24-hour day is near to but on average slightly longer than-24-hours, (b) the 24.65-hour Martian day length is outside the range of entrainment of the human circadian pacemaker in the presence of a weak environmental synchronizer, and (c) intermittent exposure to bright light is a cost effective means of resetting the human circadian pacemaker with respect to power use and astronaut time compared to continuous exposure to light.

APPROACH:
The investigators designed a 65-day long between subjects randomized clinical trial to test the three hypotheses. Twelve subjects were randomly assigned to either Cell A, or B, or C. Subjects assigned to Cell A and Cell B were studied in a longer-than-24-hour sleep-wake schedule in approximately 25 and 100 lux in the angle of gaze during wakefulness, respectively. Subjects assigned to Cell C were studied in a longer-than-24-hour sleep-wake schedule in approximately 25 to 100 lux in the angle of gaze during wakefulness, but in addition, they were exposed to two brief 45 minute pulses of bright light (approximately 10,000 lux) in the evening, separated by 1-hour of approximately 100 lux, as a countermeasure to entrain their pacemaker to the longer-than-24-hour day.

RESULTS:
Preliminary results indicate that subjects exposed to the two evening episodes of bright light demonstrate entrainment of their melatonin rhythms to the imposed sleep-wake cycle. They also reveal that lighting levels of approximately 25 lux (comparable to the intensities measured in the middeck for STS-95) were insufficient to maintain a normal phase angle of entrainment, and that approximately 100 lux (comparable to the intensities measured in the Spacehab for STS-95) was also insufficient to maintain a normal phase angle of entrainment in all subjects. Therefore, preliminary results indicate that two relatively brief (45 minutes each) exposures to bright light in the evening are effective in maintaining entrainment of the circadian pacemaker to longer-than-24 hour days, and would be effective as a countermeasure to circadian misalignment during space flight. The data also suggest that ordinary room light (100-150 lux in the angle of gaze) might be sufficient to maintain entrainment to the 24.65-hour solar day of Mars in a small number of astronauts, although at an abnormal phase angle. Both the model and preliminary data indicate that those astronauts who will have the greatest difficulty adapting to the 24.65 hour Martian day will be those with an endogenous circadian pacemaker that has a period shorter than 24 hours, which the investigators estimate represents approximately 25 percent of the population.
