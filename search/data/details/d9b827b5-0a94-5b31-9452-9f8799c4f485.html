Bone Mineral Loss and Recovery after Shuttle/Mir Flights

Record ID: 9401598
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Previous studies have shown that long-duration exposure to microgravity causes bone loss. In the absence of gravity, bone mass decreases in the load-bearing regions of the skeleton. This space condition mimics osteoporosis, a medical condition characterized by brittle bones. By learning more about the process of bone mineral loss and recovery, researchers hope to be able to implement effective countermeasures in space, and develop more effective treatments for those who suffer from bone disorders on earth.

In the absence of effective countermeasures, crewmembers will lose significant amounts of bone mineral, manifested as a decrease in bone mineral density assessed by dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA). Bone loss will show significant intra and inter-subject variability. Recovery of bone may take several months or more, and may be incomplete in some individuals. The degree and rate of bone recovery will depend in part on the amount of bone lost during flight, as well as on postflight muscular fitness.

Space flight without appropriate or effective countermeasures will also cause significant loss in muscle mass and strength, similar to losses experienced during horizontal 1-g bed rest. These muscle losses will be manifested as degraded performance or as decrements from preflight strength tests. Readaptation to the 1-g environment will result in rapid recovery of muscle mass and strength. Recovery will be complete by 6 months after return from microgravity.

Changes in the metabolism of bone can be assessed indirectly by measuring serum and urinary markers of bone metabolism and bone related hormones. Long-duration flight can be expected to increase total and ionized serum calcium, as well as urinary excretion of calcium. Levels of bone markers and bone related hormones indicate the altered metabolic activity of bone resulting from flight; postflight levels indicate the readaptation of the skeletal system to 1-G.

This investigation measured the space flight induced losses in bone mineral density and lean body mass of long-duration crewmembers and will determine the rate and extent of recovery after returning to earth. Preflight and postflight blood and urine samples were obtained to measure hormones involved in bone formation and resorption and other indicators of bone metabolism. Muscle strength testing was performed in conjunction with bone density measurements to study the relationship between muscular fitness and changes in bone density. Bone density and muscle strength measurements were obtained periodically for up to three years postflight to gain important information regarding the rate of bone recovery after return to earth's gravity.

The objectives of this study were to:

  1. Determine the regional losses in bone mineral density and lean body mass of the crewmembers of each of the four Phase 1B Shuttle-Mir flights
  2. Determine the regional rate and extent of recovery of the bone mineral and lean tissue in the same crewmembers
  3. Determine the muscle strength of the lower extremities and back before and after flight and relate this data to the bone loss during flight and to the degree and rate of bone recovery postflight
  4. Determine the levels of serum and urinary markers of bone metabolism before and after flight.

APPROACH:
To measure bone density, DEXA scans were obtained pre- and postflight using a Hologic QDR 2000 whole body densitometer (United States) or QDR 1000W densitometer (Russia). Bone mineral density data were obtained from scans of the whole body, lumbar spine, proximal femur (hip) and calcaneus (heel). Whole body and regional measurements of lean tissue mass (LTM) were obtained from the whole body scans.

Strength testing was performed pre- and postflight using a LIDO isokinetic dynamometer. Peak torque was measured for muscle groups in the legs and back. Testing time points were the same as for DEXA scans.

Serum and urinary markers of bone metabolism were measured pre- and postflight. Urine was collected over a 24-hour period; serum was collected by a fasting blood draw. Urine markers included total calcium, pyridinium cross-links, n-telopeptide, hydroxyproline, and creatinine. Serum markers included total and ionized calcium, pH, calcitonin, PTH, osteocalcin, total and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, and vitamin D. Testing time points were 30 and 7 days before launch, the day of landing, and 7, 14 and 180 days after landing.

PRELIMINARY RESULTS:
Preliminary DEXA findings on the seven crewmembers whose data have been analyzed to date (including six astronauts and one cosmonaut) indicate that the regional bone changes are similar to those documented in the 18 cosmonauts studied previously, both in terms of degree and in terms of "between-subject" and "within-subject" heterogeneity. Muscle strength results will be correlated with the bone density results during the 3-year recovery period. Recovery DEXA data are too limited to report at this time; these results and corresponding muscle strength changes will be reported at a later date. There are no preliminary bone marker findings at this time.
