The Actin Cytoskeleton: A Molecular Signature for Whole Blood Cell Cultures in the Bioreactor

Record ID: NNJ04HC82G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Studies of lymphocyte (T-cell) activation during space flight and in ground-based hypogravity model systems show a dramatic reduction in proliferative response to activating signals. The Biorack flight investigations demonstrate that this inhibition of T-cell activation is accompanied by dramatic alterations in the cytoskeletal response. Specifically, activation of human peripheral T-cells by bead-immobilized anti-TCR in microgravity culture fails to induce polarization of the Microtubule Organizing Center (MTOC) towards the cell-bead contact site. These results correlate with the inability of T-cells to express surface activation markers such as the receptor for IL-2 (CD25), an event that would certainly impair the proliferative response of T-cells. More recently the investigators have determined that exposure of the Jurkat T-cell line to hypergravity and microgravity culture can dramatically alter the state of the actin cytoskeleton and its response to stimulation. These findings have led to the following hypothesis: The actin cytoskeleton is a gravity sensitive element of cellular response and serves as a molecular signature for gravity sensitivity of cellular function.

A major goal of NASA’s cellular biotechnology program is to develop new technologies for the growth of three-dimensional mammalian tissues. Tissue engineering is an emerging field with enormous potential to restore, maintain, or enhance tissues and organs. Understanding of cellular, chemical, and physical responses of cells to stimuli is critical to successfully engineering tissue. A number of studies utilize microgravity culture and Bioreactor model systems for 3-D culture of cells, cell aggregates, and tissues, such as living skin equivalents, pancreatic islets, and engineered skeletal and cardiovascular tissues. While these studies are providing important data on the chemical and functional response of cells and tissues, little is known about the adaptation and response of important cellular cytoskeleton systems which play critical roles in signal transduction, functional response of cells, and tissue modeling.

The actin cytoskeleton plays a crucial role in signal transduction and activation response of human cells and is involved in many aspects of cellular function including orchestration of mechanical forces inside cells, signal transduction, and functional responses of cells. The principal aim of this proposal was to employ research at the cellular level to elucidate sensitivity of the actin cytoskeleton in human peripheral blood cells (T-cells, Neutrophils, and Monocytes) in the NASA developed HARV bioreactor, and in short-duration microgravity.

APPROACH:
Specific experiments will characterize the adaptation and response of the actin cytoskeleton in a number of primary human cells utilizing a novel technology that was developed to measure the polymerization state of the actin cytoskeleton in whole blood samples. The response of the actin cytoskeleton will be correlated with functional response of cellular subsets of whole blood samples (Neutrophils, T-cells, and Monocytes) cultured in (1) the HARV, (2) in short-duration microgravity on KC-135 parabolic flights, and (3) in unit gravity for control.

RESULTS:
In year one of this grant investigators developed a novel method for establishing whole blood cell cultures in the rotating HARV. A large number of experiments were devoted to evaluating various culture conditions and developing new methods for Autologous Plasma Supplementation of whole blood cultures. These investigations enabled them to develop superior culture environments for whole blood by increasing the buffering capacity of blood cultures in a simulated microgravity environment.

In year two and three of this grant they utilized the methods developed in year one to characterize the adaptation and response of circulating white blood cells to modeled microgravity, and their re-adaptation to 1-g upon return to static culture. In addition, they developed new methods for characterizing infection of human blood cells upon bacterial infection. These later investigations demonstrate the utility of this new method for identification of human blood infections and may be developed for advanced monitoring tool during long-duration space flight.

In summary, the investigations performed under this grant have provided deep insight into the adaptation and response of human circulating blood cells using the HARV model system. The technologies developed during this grant are providing new tools for monitoring immune cells and their functional responses in whole blood samples. The use of Actin signatures on long-duration space flight missions can provide early detection of cellular damage in flight crew prior to clinical manifestations. Earth-based applications of this technology include new diagnostic tools for the clinical assessment of human immune cells and monitoring of disease evolution in patients.
