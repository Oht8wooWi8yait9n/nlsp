Toxicological Assessment of Airborne Volatile Organic Compounds

Record ID: MSD007
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Astronauts living for an extended period of time in a closed environment, such as the Mir Space Station, are continuously exposed to pollutants in respirable air. Pollutants originate from material off-gassing, human metabolism, systems and payload leaks, and utility chemicals. If pollutant concentrations reach high levels, crewmembers may experience health problems and scientific experiments could be confounded. This experiment was designed to sample the Mir Space Station air at various locations and times in order to estimate the effects of airborne pollutants on crew health. The following were objectives of this experiment:

  • Determine if Mir air meets U.S. standards for crew health during long-term exposure to trace contaminants
  • Sample air using several types of hardware to compare similarity of sampling methods
  • Sample Mir Space Station air at multiple locations simultaneously to assess spatial variations in the Mir complex
  • Measure ethylene concentrations to determine if plant growth experiments could be affected.
  • Assess the rate of pollutant mixing after hatch opening between shuttle and Mir.

    APPROACH:
    In-flight air sampling was performed on NASA-Mir 2-7. Instantaneous air samples were obtained using grab sample containers (GSCs), and 24 hour time-averaged air samples were obtained using solid sorbent air samplers (SSASs). Formaldehyde badges were used to measure formaldehyde concentrations throughout Mir.

    The GSCs and SSASs were tested preflight at the Johnson Space Center (JSC) Toxicology Laboratory to ensure that each sampler was clean and could perform as expected. Formaldehyde badges were tested, reconfigured to fit the NASA flight configuration, and packaged for use during flight.

    During flight, GSC and SSAS samples were taken approximately every thirty days. After each sampling session, the date, time, and location were recorded. Once during each mission, a spatial variation assessment was performed by sampling multiple modules simultaneously. The formaldehyde badges were worn by the crewmembers and also placed in the individual modules.

    Upon return to Earth, the GSC samples were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) and a modified EPA TO-14 gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) method. The GC analysis was used to estimate ethylene concentrations. The SSAS samples were analyzed only via the GC/MS method. The filters were removed from the formaldehyde badges and extracted with formaldehyde-free water. The aqueous extracts were then analyzed for formaldehyde using a chromotropic acid-spectrophotometric method.

    RESULTS:
    The air quality on NASA-Mir 2-7 met, and exceeded, U.S. standards for human-rated spacecraft. However, the irritant group was slightly above a toxicity index of 1 during NASA-Mir 2, 6, and 7, and the carbon dioxide toxicity index was above 1 during NASA-Mir 2 and 3. The toxicity index was calculated for each toxicological group by summing the ratios of the measured concentrations to the space maximum allowable concentrations for each contaminant in a toxicological group. The high toxicity index of the irritant group was due in part to high concentrations of formaldehyde.

    As hypothesized, spatial variations of contaminants were small within the Mir complex for both SSAS and GSC samples. The variations were most likely caused by contaminants localized in the different modules. The rate of pollutant mixing after hatch opening between Shuttle and Mir could not be established because of too few samples. A comparison of the U.S. and Russian air sampling methods showed that the Russian AK-1 sorbent system samplers, on average, measured higher concentrations than did the U.S. samplers.

    During the course of this experiment, several opportunities arose that allowed scientists to study situations that had previously been unexamined. During NASA-Mir 4, a fire was caused by the solid fuel oxygen generator (SFOG); this presented an opportunity to study the contaminants released into the Mir atmosphere due to the fire, and also assess how well the life support system scrubs contaminants from the air. The life support system worked excellent and brought the pollutants associated with the fire back to baseline concentrations within 32 hours. During NASA-Mir 4 the investigators also had the opportunity to compare the results of the formaldehyde badges to those of the formaldehyde active sampler (FAS). Because of the difficulties involved with collecting inflight formaldehyde samples, conclusive results were not obtained. However, another opportunity arose to compare these two methods during the second phase of the Lunar Mars Life Support Test Project (LMLSTP). The results of this test verified the validity of the badges when compared to other methods.

    During NASA-Mir 5, all sampling hardware was lost due to the decompression of the Spektr module. The focus of the experiment then changed to analyzing how well the humidity control system would scrub a large concentration of ethylene glycol from the air. It was hypothesized that ethylene glycol present in Mir air due to a leak would be removed quickly. However, ethylene glycol detector tubes were used to measure ethylene concentrations, and it took one month for the concentration to drop below the 180-day SMAC level. Additionally, ethylene concentrations measured by the GSCs during the other NASA-Mir missions were determined to be high enough to affect plant growth experiments.

    The final unscheduled event occurred during a low temperature catalytic oxidizer (LTCO) burn. Improper operation of the LTCO caused dangerously high levels of carbon monoxide (CO) to be released into the Mir atmosphere. The combustion products analyzer (CPA) recorded high levels of CO, but because the crew appeared to be doing well after the incident, the high levels were thought to have been caused by hydrogen interference. Because this was such a serious toxicological incident, many steps have been taken to prevent a reoccurrence. One step, which will be used on the International Space Station (ISS), is the use of a new CPA that has much less cross sensitivity to hydrogen.
