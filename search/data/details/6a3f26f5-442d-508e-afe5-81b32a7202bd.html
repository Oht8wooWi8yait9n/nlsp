Water Microbiological Monitoring

Record ID: WMM
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Maintaining crew health during extended periods in space can be complicated by contamination of the general living environment. High standards for air, water, and radiation shielding quality have been established to ensure the continued health and safety of long duration crews. Water provided for crew consumption has either been launched from the ground or produced in flight by the recycling of humidity condensate. Water delivered from Earth can be tested for microbial contaminants before leaving the ground, but the water produced on orbit must be analyzed to determine its potability. The water must therefore be tested for the presence of microbial contamination, such as bacteria and fungi. In the past, this analysis has been done in Earth-based laboratories after returning to Earth. This investigation allowed water testing in-flight by the crew. The Water Microbiological Monitor (WMM) was one of several technology demonstrations planned for the NASA-Mir missions that were aimed at developing and testing in-flight, the sampling kits needed to support quality assurance for future space platforms. Having the capability to conduct the analysis during the mission gives both the crew and researchers feedback about the quality of the water in a timely manner.

The experiment objectives were to quantify bacteria in potable and hygiene water in a space environment, characterize the microbial population of a reclaimed water system, and have a water microbiology analysis system ready for the International Space Station (ISS). Findings from this investigation will help in redesigning water-sampling hardware and microbial procedures for future routine application on the ISS. Successful completion of the proposed investigation was expected to validate technology for in-flight detection, quantitation and limited identification of waterborne bacteria. Lessons learned from this technology demonstration will be applied toward the development of the ISS Crew Health Care System (CheCS), which will ensure the maintenance of high water quality standards.

APPROACH:
Water samples were scheduled for collection and processed on flight days 15, 55, 85, 115, and 135 during the NASA 3, 4 and 5 missions. Samples were collected in Teflon bags, and known volumes were processed through a microbial capture device (MCD) contained in the Water Experiment Kit (WEK) using a syringe pump assembly. Each sample was processed through three MCDs, one with 10 ml of water, a second with 50 ml, and the third with 100 ml. A modified R3A nutrient broth growth medium was added to the MCD by syringe to cultivate microorganisms trapped on the filter. Mir sampling locations included the galley hot and cold water ports (reclaimed water) and the SVO-ZV port (ground-supplied water). MCDs were incubated at 25-35 degrees Celsius in an area of the spacecraft chosen by the crewmember to promote bacterial growth.

Cultures were examined after 2 and 5 days of incubation for bacterial growth. Growth on the filters was matched to a bacterial colony density chart, and results were recorded in the flight data file and on the MCD using a "Sharpie" marker. Video and photographic documentation of the bacterial colonies were also performed. After examination on the second day, MCDs were returned to the incubator. After incubation was complete (at 5 days), samples were stowed at ambient temperature in a return kit and returned to Earth for complete analysis. Aerobic bacteria and fungi were identified using a Vitek Automated Identification System, Biolog Automated Identification System, or by classical means. Species not identified by these means were sent to a microbial reference laboratory for identification.

RESULTS:
This investigation verified the ability of the WMM to provide the means to detect and quantify bacteria in potable and hygiene water supplies during spaceflight. A modified formulation of growth medium, designed specifically for this investigation, allowed visualization of microbes trapped on the filter membrane of the MCD. Colony density results were converted to bacterial concentrations per unit of water. The results from the three sample volumes (10 ml, 50 ml, 100 ml) were inconclusive. Theoretically, the microbial count would be correspondingly higher in larger volumes of water. The results recorded by crewmembers did not hold to this pattern; in some cases, the MCDs processed with 10 ml of water had higher counts than the corresponding MCDs processed with 50 or 100 ml volumes. However, approximately half of the MCDs processed during the NASA 3 mission were processed incorrectly, as indicated by the presence of residual water in the MCDs. Proper processing required that all water be evacuated from the MCD before the growth medium was added. Failure to remove all of the water produced an aqueous suspension of organisms, which made accurate quantitation with this device impossible. Improved procedures and training techniques for subsequent missions seem to have corrected the incorrect processing of samples. None of the MCDs processed during the NASA 4 mission contained residual water. Human factors were another probable influence on the accuracy of results. The NASA 4 crewmember reported difficulty in interpreting what was or was not bacterial growth. Samples were not collected during the NASA 5 mission as scheduled, because the water experiment kit was lost when the Spektr module depressurized and was sealed off after Mir collided with a Progress resupply vehicle.

This experiment also provided insight into the microbial characterization of a reclaimed water system. Analyses of the hot water samples indicated that the treatment train and final heating step of the Mir system was able to maintain the microbial quality of water from this source at standards well below the proposed US level of 100 colony forming units (CFU)/100 ml set for the International Space Station. The bacterial content of the cold reclaimed water supply did not exceed 125 CFU/100 ml; this count was within the Russian acceptability limit. The SVO-ZV ground-water supply contained the highest bacterial levels, but did not exceed 300 CFU/100 ml. Both the cold and SVO-ZV counts were slightly above the proposed US acceptability limit for the International Space Station, 100 CFU/100ml. The types of organisms recovered were consistent with those seen in previous analyses of Shuttle water and those seen in typical ground-water supplies. All water supplies were judged safe to drink.

Although the full configuration of hardware needed to process the samples was somewhat awkward to handle, the goal of limited in-flight water analysis was achieved. Only minor problems were encountered with a small number of MCDs. The method of attaching Luer-lok fittings proved to be inadequate. These fittings occasionally became detached from the MCD during sample processing. A modification of this attachment method has been initiated.
