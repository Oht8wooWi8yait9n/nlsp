Factors Contributing to Food Acceptability and Consumption, Mood, and Stress on Long-term Space Missions

Record ID: NNX12AE56G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objectives 

The three inter-related foci in this study explore factors that contribute to the long-term acceptability of a diet, in terms of satisfaction with and sufficient consumption of the foods, as well as associated moods and stress levels. Prolonged exposure to stress responses can have deleterious effects on overall astronaut health and consequently on mission success. One of the things that can contribute to both health and stress is diet and food preparation. Understanding the relation between preparation effort and the acceptability and consumption of the prepared food, as well as effects on moods and stress, will inform decisions on the amount of time and types of activities crewmembers should allot to meal preparation. The study had 3 specific aims:

1. To design procedures for preparing and consuming meals that maximize food acceptability and lead to increased consumption and positive mood, while decreasing stress.
2. To identify foods that trigger positive emotions and to evaluate the effect of such ‘trigger foods’ on the amount consumed, moods, and stress levels of individuals who are under stress.
3. To increase people’s appreciation of the variety in their diet (without increasing the number of menu items).

Methods

To determine whether preparing food and/or having the ability to choose the ingredients in the food would improve mood and reduce stress (aim 1), the investigators recruited 118 participants (36% male) and divided them into four treatment groups: choice/prepare, no choice/prepare, choice/no prepare, and no choice/no prepare. For each treatment group, subjects were given stress tasks to induce physiological and psychological stress. After the stress task, mood related measurements were taken, and participants were given menus (differing based on choice grouping) and then later food to eat (prepared based on prepare grouping). Physiological and psychological measures of stress were taken again after the meal.

To identify foods that trigger positive emotions and to evaluate the effect of such ‘trigger foods’ on the amount consumed, moods, and stress levels of individuals who are under stress (aim 2), the investigators recruited 100 participants, 67 females, who first completed an online questionnaire to indicate their comfort foods and a variety of comparison foods. Then, during two lab sessions a week apart from each other (and at least a week after the online questionnaire, counterbalanced in order), participants watched films that induced a negative effect. In one session, participants were served their comfort food. In the other, participants were served an equally liked non-comfort food, a neutral food, or no food. Short-term mood changes were measured to determine psychological effects of these foods, rather than biochemical effects on mood from particular food components (e.g., sugars or vitamins). 

To determine whether boredom with repeatedly eaten foods could be curbed by increasing appreciation for variety in one’s diet (aim 3), participants repeatedly consumed granola bars over a 2-week period. Half the participants were reminded to think about recently eaten foods, and half were reminded to think about music they had recently heard. 
The researchers also decided to recruit people to keep 28-day food diaries to determine the degree of food variety in the diets of free-living individuals. 

Results

Aim 1 test results showed that preparation group had little effect on reducing stress or improving mood. However, increases in heart rate and decreases in systolic blood pressure were observed for those in the No Prepare groups compared to the Prepare groups. Those in the No Choice groups showed a greater decrease in anxiety, anger, and systolic blood pressure after the meal than those in the Choice groups. After the meal, heart rate increased more for those in the Choice versus the No Choice groups. Stress and negative measures related to mood decreased post meal overall. 

Tests developed to satisfy aim 2 showed that consuming comfort food after the film was no more effective at improving mood than other foods or eating nothing after the film. 

Aim 3 results showed the first intervention of having participants think about foods they had eaten in the previous week significantly increased subsequent intake and self-reported satisfaction and decreased self-reported boredom. They also tested whether there was a difference in the curbing of boredom between a group that was asked to think of musical artists they had listened to recently and a group that was asked to think about recently eaten foods. There was no significant difference in the liking of granola bars between these two groups. There was however an observed behavioral difference in the willingness to continue eating granola bars. The participants asked to think of food they had recently eaten took significantly more granola bars home at the end of the study than did participants in the music group.

The ISS component of this study known as "Astro-Palate" was discontinued after completion of part A leaving parts B and C incomplete. 

Part A of Astro-Palate: The objective of this study was to determine the role of menu choice on meal satisfaction. This was conducted once relatively early and then again later in the flight.

Part B of Astro-Palate: The objective of this study was to determine the effects of eating situation on mood. Crewmembers completed questionnaires before and after the meals. This would have been done relatively earlier and then again later in the flight. The first subject arrived at the ISS in 2014. Other subjects had agreed to participate and were in varying stages of completing the pre-flight questionnaires.

Part C of Astro-Palate: Objectives: 1) to determine whether eating foods thought to be capable of improving mood will improve moods of astronauts in space when they are in a negative mood and 2) to determine whether eating such foods immediately prior to situations generating negative mood will ameliorate that mood. Crewmembers would have completed questionnaires before and after specific snacks and tasks.
