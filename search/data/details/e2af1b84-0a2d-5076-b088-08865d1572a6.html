The Effect of Daily Activity and Age on Bone Density in Men

Record ID: ARCbedrest170
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The purpose of these studies was to determine the relationship between declining activity level with age and calcaneal density as well as in vivo calcaneal stresses from external and internal forces during walking and running

APPROACH:
The first study: A mathematical model previously derived using experimental data of exercise effects on bone mass was used to calculate the sensitivity of calcaneal bone density to age-related changes in body weight, walking speed, and daily walking duration over the age range of 30-70 years. Changes in body weight and walking speed with age were obtained from the literature. The assumption was made that walking provides the primary mechanical load stimulus to the calcaneus. The second study towards a simple Ground Reaction Force (GRF) measurement from external forces was guided by the hypothesis that the reciprocal of foot-ground contact time or the reciprocal of the stride-period-normalized contact time would predict the peak horizontal and vertical GRF during gait. GRF data was collected from 24 subjects while they walked or ran at a variety of speeds. The third study was directed toward a more detailed study of the internal forces from joints, muscles, and tissues during walking and running. It utilized force plate measurements and hi-speed cine-radiography data collected at the Herrick-Davis Motion Analysis Laboratory, Henry Ford Hospital, Detroit, MI. A finite element model coupled with contact of the foot was constructed to determine the forces acting on the calcaneus during walking and running. The GRF data (units in BW) was transformed to a reference system fixed on the foot with the forefoot and the Achilles tendon as the 2 points of contact.

RESULTS:
A decline in daily walking duration with age may be a major factor in the decrease in bone density up to the age of 50 years. A 39% reduction in daily walking from 30 to 50 years of age can account for a bone loss of 0.2% per year and a reduction of 79% over the same period can account for a bone loss of 0.8% per year. Past age 55, individuals tend to walk more slowly and lose BW, two factors that combined with reduced duration in walking, may accelerate bone loss. Collectively, the second and third studies showed that all parameters were significantly correlated to the reciprocal of foot-ground contact time and the reciprocal of stride-period-normalized contact time. The calculated force-time profiles of the joint contact, ligament, and Achilles tendon forces varied with the time history curve of the moment about the ankle joint. The forces acting on the calcaneus at peak loading during walking occur at 70-80% of stance and during running, earlier or at 60% of stance. The model predicted peak talocalcaneal and calcaneocuboid joint loads of 5.4 and 4.2 BW during walking and 11.1 and 7.9 BW during running. The maximum predicted Achilles tendon forces were 3.9 BW for walking and 7.7 BW for running. Conclusions: Temporal measures can predict force parameters of gait and may be an alternative to direct GRF measurements for determining daily histories of habitual lower limb loading quantities needed for a bone-remodeling stimulus. The bone-remodeling algorithm predicts that running can substantially increase calcaneal bone density. Large magnitude forces and calcaneal stresses are generated late in the stance phase, with maximum loads occurring at 70% of the stance phase during walking and at 60% of the stance phase during running, for the gait velocities analyzed. The trajectories of the principal stresses, during both walking and running, corresponded to each other and qualitatively to the trabecular architecture of the calcaneus.
