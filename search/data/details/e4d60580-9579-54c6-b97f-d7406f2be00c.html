Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The primary objective of this investigation was to measure selected hormone and electrolyte components of the blood early in space flight. The blood components selected reflect the status of fluid and electrolyte metabolism. Measurement of these variables may help investigators determine whether responses predicted by the fluid redistribution hypothesis occur. According to this hypothesis, losses of body fluid and electrolytes result from the headward shift of fluid brought about by exposure of the human body to weightlessness. Estimation of early changes previously was not possible because of inflight scheduling priorities and constraints. The most dramatic changes in the adaptive response to weightlessness are believed to occur in the first hours of a mission.

APPROACH:
The Inflight Blood Collection System was used to collect samples by venipuncture from 6 subjects on 2 missions 1 or 3 times before flight; on mission days 2, 4, and 6 during flight; and within 2 hours (R+0) and 3 days (R+3) after landing. The concentrations of electrolytes and other blood components were determined using established clinical laboratory procedures such as spectrophotometry and radioimmunoassay.

RESULTS:
The earliest inflight blood collection time of 31 hours may not have been early enough. High levels of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) may have been related to space motion sickness. Angiotensin I levels increased while aldosterone was suppressed at the inflight times measured. Cortisol secretion appeared to have been stimulated by high levels of adrenocorticotropic hormone. Most hormones increased at landing. Serum sodium decreased slightly during flight, and potassium decreased in the first 4 days but increased on flight days 6 and 7. The results, especially those for hormone levels, indicated that the fluid redistribution hypothesis does not explain changes in the regulation of body fluid and electrolytes during weightlessness. Renal function and other factors involved in fluid and electrolyte regulation, such as atrial natriuretic factor and prostaglandin E, need to be measured during flight.
