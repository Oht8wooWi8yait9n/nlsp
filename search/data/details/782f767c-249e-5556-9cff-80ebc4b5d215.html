Skeletal Responses to Long-Duration Simulated Microgravity in Male and Female Rats

Record ID: ARC00XX172
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVE:
Long duration spaceflight can cause severe bone loss, particularly at the lumbar spine and hip. The rate of bone loss in crewmembers while on the space station can be profound, and a significant increase in estimated fracture risk remains even one year after returning to Earth. In addition to microgravity, the natural aging process leads to declines in bone mass and also increases in fracture risk, which suggests that the combination of the two could have compounding effects on bone structure and integrity. Changes in bone structure and strength have unknown consequences and important implications for the skeletal health of crewmembers. In recent ISS missions, crewmembers have maintained bone mass better, presumably due to improved resistance exercise protocols coupled with adequate energy intake and vitamin D; however, no predictors of bone geometry or strength were evaluated. As NASA is preparing to send astronauts to the ISS and beyond for missions exceeding one year, it is critical to characterize and understand bone loss and recovery for longer duration spaceflight.

The proposed project will take advantage of the unique tissue sharing opportunity for obtaining frozen specimens from a UC Davis experiment that entails 90-day rat unloading and reloading studies. Specifically, the aims of this project are: 1) Determine structural and gene expression changes in cortical and cancellous bone in growing rats (3 month old) during long-duration simulated microgravity exposure and recovery. 2) Determine whether skeletal responses to long-duration simulated microgravity differ in males compared to females (3 month old). 3) Determine age effects (9 mo old) on temporal changes in skeletal structure and gene expression during extended simulated microgravity followed by recovery. We predict that osteoclastogenic and oxidative stress-related factors relates directly to the extent of bone decrements.

APPROACH:
To accomplish our Aims, we will determine how responses to simulated weightlessness vary in young (3 month old) versus adult (9 month old) male rats as a function of time, using 3D imaging (microcomputed tomography), mechanical testing, and molecular gene expression analyses. We also will determine if bone loss and recovery differ between male and female rats.

RESULTS:
This experiment is currently in progress. Results will be available at the conclusion of the study.
