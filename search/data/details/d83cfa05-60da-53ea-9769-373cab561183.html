Hardware Evaluation: International Space Station Portable Pulmonary Function System (PPFS)

Record ID: EORS_PPFS
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Human Research Program’s Integrated Research Plan specifies that acquisition of data regarding the changes in maximum exercise oxygen consumption (VO2max) during and following space flight is a high priority to support future space exploration endeavors. In order to support such measurements, a metabolic gas analysis system capable of being used in a spacecraft is required. One such device, known as the Pulmonary Function System, was developed for International Space Station (ISS) use and was validated in the Exercise Physiology Laboratory at NASA-Johnson Space Center (JSC). The PFS is currently being used to support studies of resting metabolism and cardiac function on board the ISS, but is not portable and is confined to a relatively immobile rack location. The exercise equipment on board the ISS changed locations since earlier expeditions, and the cycle ergometer used for exercise testing was moved to a different module than the PFS. Thus, the need for a portable metabolic gas analysis device for ISS use arose. The Portable Pulmonary Function System (PPFS) was developed by a contractor to the European Space Agency (ESA) to fill this need. The PPFS is a sized-optimized variant of the PFS which is capable of being used in any location on board the ISS that has available power and data connections.

Aerobic capacity (VO2pk) is determined by two factors: the body’s ability to deliver oxygen to the working muscles, and the muscles’ ability to extract that oxygen. In normal subjects, cardiac output, or the amount of blood the body is able to pump per minute, increases with increasing workload providing a relationship between cardiac output and VO2pk. During long-duration space flight and bed rest, changes in the cardiovascular system lead to a decrease in aerobic capacity.

The Exercise Physiology Laboratory prepared for an investigation designed to study the changes in VO2pk and cardiac output during space flight. The flight study is titled “Evaluation of Maximal Oxygen Uptake (VO2max) and Submaximal Estimates of VO2max Before, During and After Long Duration International Space Station Mission.” In order to measure these changes, a new portable metabolic gas analysis system, the Portable Pulmonary Function System (PPFS) was used to measure both VO2pk and cardiac output. The purpose of this study was to determine the accuracy of PPFS measurements.

APPROACH:
Subjects performed an initial peak cycle test protocol used to determine pre-flight VO2max for astronauts. The cycle ergometer used for all testing was a LODE Excalibur Sport (Groningen, NL). During the initial test, subjects with a body mass of > 65 kg, cycled for three minutes at 50, 100, and 150 Watts (W) followed by an increase of 25 W every minute until peak exertion was reached. If the subject’s body mass was < 65 kg, the protocol required cycling for 3 minutes at 50, 75, and 100 W, followed by an increase of 25 W every minute until peak exertion was attained. For all tests, the cycle work rate was increased according to the protocol until subjects indicated that they could no longer continue or could not maintain a pedal cadence of 75 revolutions per minute (rpm). During this initial test the ParvoMedics TrueOne© (ParvoMedics, Salt Lake City, UT) system was used to perform metabolic gas analysis. The VO2and work rate data from the test was used to prescribe the protocol for the second and third tests.

The second and third tests were trials designed for comparison of the metabolic gas analysis systems and cardiac output measurements. The testing protocol used was an extension to maximum of the ISS periodic fitness evaluation (PFE) test. The comparison tests consisted of three five minute stages designed to elicit 25%, 50%, and 75% of the individual’s previously determined VO2max. These three stages are identical to those of the ISS PFE test. These stages were followed by 25W/min work rate increases until subjects reached their maximum. Comparison tests were performed in a counterbalanced order, with one test performed using the ParvoMedics TrueOne © system paired with echocardiography (Phillips iE-33™) to measure Qc, and one test performed using the PPFS to obtain metabolic gas analysis data and Qc measurements using a re-breathing technique. These tests were conducted within one month after the initial test, and were separated by at least one week in order to minimize the potential effects of residual soreness or fatigue.

For all tests, prior to the start of exercise, subjects rested quietly for five minutes in the supine and the seated position while resting measurements of heart rate (HR) and rhythm and blood pressure (BP) were taken. HR and rhythm were measured electrocardiographically (Q-Stress, Quinton Instruments, Seattle, WA) and BP was measured using a mercurial sphygmomanometer and stethoscope. HR and rhythm and metabolic expired gas were measured continuously throughout the exercise test protocol. BP was measured one time each stage and ratings of perceived exertion were reported during the last 30 seconds of each stage. In the recovery period, BP was recorded and the subject was monitored for any adverse effects caused by maximal exertion.

RESULTS:
All of the metabolic gas analysis data, irrespective of the device used for measurement, fell within normal physiological ranges. Thus, there are no gross errors present in either the PPFS or the Parvomedics device. In general, the PPFS-measured absolute values of VO2 were lower than those measured by the Parvomedics system.
