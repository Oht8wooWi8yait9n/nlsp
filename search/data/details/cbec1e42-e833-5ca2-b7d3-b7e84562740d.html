Sleep Wake Actigraphy and Light Exposure on ISS12

Record ID: Sleep_ISS12
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
An inadequate quantity or quality of sleep may impair an astronaut’s ability to maintain a high level of cognitive performance and vigilance while operating and monitoring sophisticated instrumentation during space flight. One astronaut and one cosmonaut took part in a one-year International Space Station (ISS) mission. Given that the duration of this mission was essentially twice as long as the nominal ISS missions, investigators wanted to study is how the mind and body, including sleep and the circadian system, responded or adapted to that much time in space. This study estimated sleep and circadian alignment throughout the mission via actigraphy, photometry, and sleep logs. Wrist actigraphy and photometry provided a simple, objective, and noninvasive means of collecting data about the sleep/wake state as well as more specific information about various aspects of sleep. The actigraphy and photometry data were analyzed in conjunction with subjective sleep log information to evaluate multiple parameters related to sleep quality such as timing, length, latency, efficiency, continuity, and number of awakenings.

The objectives of this study were to test the following hypotheses:

  1. Long duration (one-year) space flight will result in disruption of sleep, as measured by wrist actigraphy, during a one-year ISS mission;
  2. Sleep disruption will be associated with light exposure that is inappropriately timed and/or of insufficient intensity to entrain crewmembers to the scheduled sleep-wake cycle;
  3. Sleep disruption and/or circadian rhythm misalignment will lead to subjective dissatisfaction with sleep and daytime alertness; and
  4. Long-duration (one-year) space flight will result in reduced sleep and sleep quality, as reported by sleep-wake diaries.

APPROACH:
Participants wore a small light-weight activity and light recording device called an Actiwatch Spectrum continuously throughout four terrestrial data collection intervals and throughout their space flight mission. Actigraphy and photometry data were collected for two weeks and occurred approximately one year and six months prior to launch (pre-flight L-365, L-180) and from eleven days prior to launch (pre-flight L-11) until launch day (L-0). Inflight, crewmembers donned the Spectrum as soon as possible when arriving on the ISS and doffed it before landing. Participants were given a Spectrum as soon as possible after the landing and post-flight data were collected for two weeks (R+0 through R+14). Terrestrial and inflight data were collected in one-minute epochs. There were no restrictions on participant behavior or countermeasure use during any of these data collection sessions. Data were downloaded following each terrestrial data collection sessions and monthly on the ISS.

Additionally, crewmembers were instructed to complete a daily sleep log within 15 minutes of awakening during all data collection intervals with the exception of ISS flights where crewmembers were scheduled to complete the log approximately every three weeks. The sleep log contained subjective ratings of alertness and fatigue, subjective quantification of sleep, and documentation of sleep disturbances and medication usage. Through a data sharing agreement, the investigators used medication logs from the crewmembers’ post-flight interviews with crew surgeons in order to compare with medications reported on the daily log.

Sleep duration was estimated in one-minute epochs from actigraphy data using Actiware Software. A modified sleep efficiency was calculated as total sleep divided by the time between actigraph-defined start sleep and end sleep times. Subjective sleep quality and alertness were measured on the 100 mm visual analog scales contained in the sleep log. Subjective sleep quality was assessed with the question “How did you sleep last night?” anchored at 0 with “poorly” and with “great” at 100. Alertness (daytime fatigue) was assessed with “How do you feel right now?” anchored at 0 with “sleepy” and with “alert” at 100. The number of days on which sleep medication was taken and the reasons for sleep disturbances were tabulated from the sleep log. At the end of the pre-flight scheduled L-360 and scheduled L-180 data collection and following the post-flight data collection, the crewmember performed a 30-minute debrief with investigators to share data with the crewmember and a structured interview format was used to illicit additional information on his or her sleep.

RESULTS:
On the one-year mission, there was considerable variation in sleep between the two crewmembers. On average, the mean nightly sleep duration of the two crewmembers on ISS12 was one hour more than the mean nightly sleep duration on the 2004-2011 shorter ISS missions. Several factors may have affected nightly sleep duration including less shifting of the sleep-wake cycle and thus, reduction in circadian misaligned days from 20% on the 2004-2011 missions to 3% on the one-year mission. During space flight, objective sleep efficiency was significantly correlated with reported sleep quality and alertness.
