Effect of Radiation on Human Peripheral Blood Dendritic Cells

Record ID: NAG91454
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objective of this study was to investigate the effect of proton, heavy ion, and gamma radiation on human dendritic cells (DCs) and their ability to process antigens and, thus, impact the immune system. Increased exposure of the body to ionizing radiation suppresses the formation of bone marrow tissue, which, in turn, suppresses development of blood cells within bone marrow. This affects the differentiation, maturation, and function of dendritic cells, which are believed to be the link between innate and cell-mediated immunity. Thus, it is suspected that the increased exposure to ionizing radiation during long-term space flight may severely impair the immune system unless appropriate countermeasures are taken.

APPROACH:
Cultured human monocyte-derived dendritic cells were exposed to proton-, heavy ion- and gamma-radiation. The life cycle and formation of DCs were studied by determining the effects of the radiation on the differentiation of non-lymphocyte white blood cell precursor cells into immature dendritic cells. Then, the ability of these immature DCs stimulate cellular immunity were determined. In addition, this study determined the ability of activated mature DCs to present antigen to T-lymphocytes (T-cells). Countermeasures to prevent the harmful effects of proton-, heavy ion- and gamma-radiation on myelosuppression and the subsequent immune suppression were also identified.

RESULTS:
The investigators have accomplished the following based on the data obtained from 12 healthy normal subjects: 1. Compared with un-irradiated PDC or MDC, there were no significant differences in the percentage of cytokine-producing PDC or MDC subject to low-dose g-radiation. 2. There is an irradiation dose dependent reduction of the mean channel fluorescence intensity (MFI) of intracellular IFN-a (P = 0.004) and TNF-a (P = 0.000) of the plasmacytoid DC upon stimulation with 5 uM of CL097. 3. The amount of IL-12 (p = 0.007) and TNF-a (p = 0.028) produced by individual MDC was significantly reduced upon the activation through TLR-8 with CL097 in an irradiation dose-dependent manner. In summary, the suppression of PDC to synthesize IFN-a and TNF-a by low-dose g-irradiation is unprecedented and suggests the possible attenuation of innate immune surveillance, whereas the reduced production of IL-12 and TNF-a by low-dose g-irradiated MDC might negatively impact the ability of MDC to mediate adaptive immunity and effector T-cell function. As DC are essential for the development of an immune response and clearance of microbial infection, low-dose g-radiation might pose a serious threat to long-term health in space.
