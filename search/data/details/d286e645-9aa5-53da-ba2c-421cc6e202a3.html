Light Flash Observations

Record ID: MA-106
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
During the Apollo missions 12-17 and Skylab missions 2-4, the crews observed and recorded the frequency and character of light flashes through their eyes. After approximately 17 minutes of dark adaptation, tiny dots and thin streaks of white light were seen at frequencies of approximately three events per minute by crewmembers when on translunar coast (TLC) or transearth coast (TEC) or over the polar part of Skylab orbits. When near the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA), where the proton flux was over 1000 times greater than in other parts of the orbit, the frequency increased to approximately nine events per minute for Skylab altitudes of 443 km.

Nearly 20 years ago, it was hypothesized that astronauts were likely to see flashes of light from cosmic ray particles when they were outside the shielding provided by the Earth’s magnetic field. The site of action of these particles, and whether cosmic particles actually caused the light flash phenomenon, were topics of conjecture in 1970, when this phenomenon was first reported. The causes for luminous phosphenes included ionizing radiation, mechanical pressure, electrical currents, magnetic fields, stimulation of the cerebral cortex, or pathological conditions of the Central Nervous System (CNS). In 1970, these phenomena were believed to be caused by ionizing heavy nuclei such as carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen, which transversed the spacecraft and the body when outside the Earth’s magnetic shielding. The conjecture that the major mechanism for these visual phenomena was Cerenkov radiation was controversial.

Simulation studies were used to determine whether carbon, nitrogen, oxygen or other ions with equivalent ionizing characteristics could cause the same phenomena seen by astronauts. The exact mechanism in which the retina was stimulated by ionizing particles traveling at velocities below those necessary for emission of Cerenkov light was unclear. Experiments in which nitrogen ions were stopped in the cerebral cortex showed that the brain and optic nerve were not stimulated. Several mechanisms existed by which cosmic particles could produce visual sensations. They were: 1) electrical excitation resulting in near-ultraviolet (UV) radiation in the vicinity of the retina, 2) ionization by cosmic ray particles through the retina, 3) local ionization by the products from a nonelastic collision of the high-velocity proton and carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen, or 4) a shock wave phenomenon from charged particles passing through the tissue matrix.

The purpose of the experiment was to ascertain quantitatively the frequency, character, latitudinal dependence, and identity of cosmic particles that caused the light flash phenomenon during the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project. The ultimate objective was the assessment of radiation hazards of long-term Earth-orbiting and interplanetary missions.

APPROACH:
Special features of the experiment included measurement of the characteristics of the cosmic-particle environment in the vicinity of the eye, measurement of dark adaptation, and continuous onboard accumulation of the light flash observations from astronauts and particle detectors throughout one complete 90-minute orbit.

Two revolutions of the Earth were devoted to this experiment. During revolution 110, the silicon telescope-spectrometer was deployed to measure the trajectory, atomic charge Z, and velocity of cosmic particles with a stopping power of 10 kilo-electron volts per micrometer or greater. During revolution 111, two crewmembers donned special masks in order to become completely dark-adapted. After approximately 10 minutes, the two observers reached dark adaptation and made continuous observations of visual sensations. At each event, a push-button signal from the observing astronaut was recorded on the digital tape and the verbal description was recorded on the onboard tape recorder. The third crewmember operated the experiment control unit, which received data from silicon detectors and silver chloride AgCl(Cd) crystals. The crystals were used to register particle tracks in four sectors of orbit, those corresponding to the northern latitudes, equatorial latitudes, the SAA, and the southern latitudes. After the flight, the digital tapes, voice tapes, detector boxes, and masks were returned to the investigators.

The digital tapes were transcribed, and each event was related to the orbital position of the spacecraft. Events from the silicon detectors, pushbuttons and control unit were read from the digital tape by a specially constructed microprocessor. The push-button events and time marks were related to the verbal reports. The energy and trajectory information were inadequate because of a paucity of recorded events.

The AgCl(Cd) crystals were transported to the University of Frankfurt, Germany, where they had been produced and calibrated. The crystals were analyzed for the abundance of tracks produced by protons, alpha particles, or heavier ions.

The characteristics of the cosmic particle environment inside the spacecraft were determined by converting the spectrum and abundance of particles outside the spacecraft to the number of particles with various stopping powers and residual ranges, using aluminum shielding data for 512 sectors of solid angle around the spacecraft.

RESULTS:
There were 82 visual events reported by two crewmembers during a 90-minute orbit at an altitude of 225 kilometers (km).

The results of the light flash experiment indicated that the abundance of ions that caused the light flash phenomena was minimal between latitudes 30° N and 30° S. The frequency of light flashes between latitudes 30° N/50° N and 30° S/50° S was 25 times higher than noted in the equatorial latitudes. Increased flash phenomena were not reported through the SAA, as was expected after reviewing a Skylab 4 report of increased activity through the SAA. However, at the 225-km altitude of Apollo-Soyuz, the proton flux was much less than that of the Skylab 4 altitude of 443 kilometers. Also, the shielding of the Apollo spacecraft was greater than that of Skylab. This distribution of flashes was correlated with the distribution of cosmic particles with stopping power greater that 15 kilo-electron volts per micrometer in the eye.

The frequency of reported light flash events was strongly dependent on latitude. At the points of closest approach to the geomagnetic poles, the observed events occurred at one to two per minute; however no events occurred near the equator. These observations were consistent with the change in abundance of cosmic particles with latitude that was also noted during the Skylab 4 mission. The increased rate of visual events near the south geomagnetic pole can be explained by increased dark adaptation of the crewmembers. The observed event rate correlated with a cosmic particle abundance for heavy particles with energy losses > 5 kilo-electron volts per micrometer.

The AgCl(Cd) detector results suggested that 60 events of 15 kilo-electron volts per micrometer or greater would be observed during three-minute intervals in the northernmost portions of the orbit. The highest observed event rate at high latitudes was only 14 events per three minutes near the south magnetic pole. This rate implied a 23-percent efficiency for the detector. However, near the equator, where only a few events were observed, the detectors recorded the equivalent average of 73 events per four cm2/minute. An explanation of this inconsistency was that the fading time from the heavy ions was long relative to the orbit sectors. Thus, identification of the heavy particle environment in each sector could not be expected. Also, particles interacting in the crystals hours before the experiment might have been included in the event.

Assuming the complete retina target area of four cm2, the efficiency of visualizing the events was 36 percent for the northernmost sector. In the SAA sector, the detector efficiency diminished to eight percent. Thus, the AgCl(Cd) detector data did not correlate well with either the observed event rate or the calculated abundances.

There were no reports of high frequency throughout the SAA, as was expected from a previous Skylab 4 report of markedly increased flash activity. At the 225-km altitude of Apollo-Soyuz, the proton flux of particles was 14 times less than at the 443-km altitude of Skylab 4. Also, the aluminum shielding of the Apollo command module was much greater than that of Skylab.

The more favored hypothesis for the events seen in the SAA, particularly at higher altitudes, was the mechanism of proton nonelastic nuclear collision with carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in tissues of the retina. This mechanism was similar to that proposed for neutron interactions, which also cause discrete flashes. If the average cross-section for interactions such as (p, p’, 3a) on carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen was assumed to be approximately 450 millibars in the proton energy range of 175 million electron volts (MeV) or less, only one event per three minutes would occur during Apollo-Soyuz altitude of 225 km. Under the reduced shielding conditions of Skylab 4, 51 times more protons would interact with the eye tissues of one observer, or 26 events might be observed during a three-minute interval. The average rate was 28 events per three minutes on Skylab 4. Thus, the nuclear collision hypothesis for the origin of light flashes in the SAA had some validity.
