Molecular Characterization of Transmissible Chromosome Aberrations Produced by Ions of Intermediate and High Atomic Number

Record ID: 80NSSC21K0679
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Extremely low fluences of mixed-field galactic cosmic radiation (GCR) increase the risk of carcinogenesis following manned space flight. Additionally, the charged particles encountered in deep space vary enormously in terms of energy, ionization density, and the relative biological effects these particles produce. The cocktail of ions composing GCR cannot be produced to effectively duplicate a biologically meaningful, experimental setting on Earth. Instead, representative aspects of GCR, such as single charged particle tracks, can be used to simulate the deep space environment and its effects on human cells. Chromosome aberrations are used to estimate dose for individuals exposed to unknown magnitude and/or quality of radiation, and are considered the “gold standard” for biodosimetry.
Very little is known about the molecular biology of chromosome aberration formation by ionizing radiation and how these processes relate to changes in linear energy transfer (LET)/track structure. This experiment seeks to introduce an experimental model to sequence DNA at the breakpoint junctions of radiation-induced chromosome exchanges in human cells for the first time and investigate the role that track structure plays in the outcome of chromosome misrepair, particularly as it relates to microhomology-mediated nonhomologous end-joining (mmNHEJ). This will be accomplished using two specific aims.
1) Isolate and cytogenetically characterize human fibroblast cell clones that represent the survival and clonal expansion of single cells exposed to gamma rays, 56Fe and 7Li ions.
2) Characterize clones through the use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) in order to determine the nature of the breakpoint junctions identified in Specific Aim 1 and determine which major DNA repair pathways are involved.

APPROACH:
This experiment used subclones (K-1 lineage) of BJ-1 hTERT-immortalized human fibroblast cells, which are noted for their high plating efficiency, robust growth, and karyotypic stability. Confluent K-1 cultures were exposed to 1.5 MeV/amu 7Li and 630 MeV/amu 56Fe ions. Subsequently, single cell suspensions of irradiated cultures were plated at low density and allowed to form colonies. Clones exposed to 137Cs gamma rays were included as a low LET comparison.
To address Specific Aim 1, macroscopic colonies were randomly selected to isolate clonal descendants that stably carry radiation-induced translocations and/or inversions. Chromosome-type interchanges (translocations) were identified using 24-color multicolor fluorescent in situ hybridization (mFISH). The same clones were analyzed for intrachanges (inversions) by applying directional genomic hybridization (dGH) to chromosomes 1, 2, and 3. Analyzing these large chromosomes increased the probability of locating inversions compared to previous methods which applied dGH singly to either chromosome 3 or chromosome 10.
To meet Specific Aim 2, the researchers applied an NGS approach to DNA collected from the same colonies to generate genomic libraries that were used to further characterize radiation-induced structural variants (SVs), including translocations, inversions, and deletions, and their locations across the chromosomes. Small fragments (=400 bp) putatively containing chromosome breakpoints were identified using paired-end sequencing and bioinformatic techniques. Meerkat software was used to identify precise breakpoints using local alignment of the split reads that map to the breakpoint region for regions with split read support. To further investigate breakpoint regions without split read support, the researchers generated PCR fragments for the corresponding discordant reads identified by the software and sequenced fragments of anticipated size using Sanger methods. During the course of the experiment, it was found that long sequencing reads (~20 kb) with single-molecule, real-time (SMRT) sequencing would yield higher fidelity data for the identification of SVs. This method was used to sequence the DNA of six clones, including clones exposed to gamma radiation and 56Fe ions, and the parent (K-1) cell line.

RESULTS:
One important key step to understanding the process of chromosomal exchange aberration formation is molecular analysis of breakpoint junctions formed as the result of translocations and inversions. Further, investigating these chromosome aberrations and their related pathways will help to elucidate the mechanisms underlying cancer processes. As a result of this experiment, the researchers published the first sequence-level characterization of a known radiation-induced translocation in human cells using massively parallel sequencing. Further, a 4bp microhomology identified through this study supports the researchers’ hypothesis that such rearrangements are characteristic of mmNHEJ repair/misrepair pathways.
Analysis of the breakpoint junctions identified in chromosomes 3 and 4 revealed that both were located in regions of repetitive DNA. Repeat elements cause difficulties for NGS approaches, particularly methods utilizing short-read mate-pair approaches which can produce ambiguous alignments across repetitive sequences, resulting in a large number of false-positive calls to the reference genome. SMRT sequencing, however, allowed the researchers to identify all the translocations that were cytogenetically observed with only a few false-positive calls. One cytogenetically observed inversion on chromosome 3 was unable to be recovered by all three sequencing approaches (short-read paired end, mate pair, and SMRT), leading the researchers to conclude that a technical issue exists that likely either prevents (1) replication by DNA polymersases or (2) incorporation of nucleotides during sequencing and, subsequently, the identification of this breakpoint.
