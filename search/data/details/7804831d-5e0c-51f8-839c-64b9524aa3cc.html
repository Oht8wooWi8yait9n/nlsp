Experiments

Dynamics of Calcium Metabolism and Bone Tissue

Record ID: 2.1.2
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The effect of space flight on the skeletal system is one of the most critical issues that needs to be resolved to assure crew well-being during extended duration missions. Due to the absence of weight bearing loads and other factors, bone mineral is lost during space flight. There is significant evidence of the negative impact of microgravity on bone homeostasis. Negative calcium balance has been noted previously in extended duration space flights (84-day Skylab) as well as following bed rest (simulated microgravity). The negative balance has been corroborated with increased excretion of calcium in the urine and feces, as well as decreased calcium absorption.

In addition, the hydroxylated metabolites of vitamin D (e.g., calcidiol, 25(0H)D3 and calcitriol, 1,25(0H)2D3) have been shown to play an important role in calcium homeostasis, primarily at the level of intestinal absorption of dietary calcium. Vitamin D intake is particularly important during extended duration space flights due to the decreased in vivo production of vitamin D through skin exposure to ultraviolet light.

This study was designed to assess changes in several indices of bone and calcium homeostasis during extended-duration space flight and after return to earth. Specific objectives were to: a) determine the effects of microgravity and diet on calcium absorption/kinetics; b) determine the effect of microgravity on total and ionized serum calcium; c) evaluate bone/calcium homeostasis during space flight by measuring regulatory hormones such as parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin, vitamin D metabolites, osteocalcin, and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase; d) determine changes in bone density following flight in order to assess the impact and recovery from flight; and e) determine the accretion of bone mass following extended duration space flight.

APPROACH:
Three astronaut subjects participated in this experiment. Calcium absorption and kinetics were determined before, during, and after the mission. Other related measurements included the monitoring of bone density, bone and calcium regulating hormones, and urinary markers of bone metabolism. Postflight studies were designed to examine the recovery of bone mineral loss during the mission, as well as re-adaptation of bone regulating hormones and calcium metabolism after space flight.

Baseline specimen collection was performed over three five-day periods before and after flight on all three subjects. Blood, urine, feces and saliva were collected from 150 to 145 days before launch (L-150 to L-145), L-60 to L-55, and L-35 to L-33. Postflight samples were collected in two sessions beginning immediately after landing until six days postflight (R+0-6) and R+9-15. Additional data were also collected on R+75 and R+115. Samples were analyzed for 43 Ca and 46 Ca calcium isotopes, vitamin D metabolites, and indices of bone metabolism, e.g., pyridinoline, hydroxyproline:creatinine ratio.

Inflight blood, urine and saliva collections were performed two times in flight on Mir 18 (mission day 12 (MD 12)) and MD 93, and on flight day 5 (FD 5) on STS-71. A fourth inflight session, planned for MD 70 was not performed. A total of 32 blood samples was collected aboard Mir and 25 samples on board the Shuttle. All blood samples were aliquoted and frozen at -20 degrees Celsius for postflight analysis except for blood ionized calcium and osmolality (pH), which were determined in "real time" with a portable clinical blood analyzer (PCBA.) Inflight urine samples were collected using void-by-void techniques. Urine collection devices (UCD) were configured such that: a) a known volume of lithium chloride was included as a volume marker, and b) syringe aliquots could be withdrawn and stored appropriately. Inflight urine samples were either frozen at -20 degrees Celsius or preserved with thimerosal or thymol. Overall, 51 urine samples were collected aboard Mir and 22 samples aboard STS-71. Prior to each calcium absorption and kinetics study, urine samples were collected as soon as possible postsleep. Crewmembers were instructed to avoid food and fluids at least 8 hours prior to urine collection and at least 15 minutes before saliva collection.

Calcium absorption and kinetics were examined using a double stable isotope method. After baseline blood, urine, saliva and stool (pre/post sessions only) collection, subjects ingested 125 mg calcium carbonate (43Ca) as soon after sleep as possible. This was followed 1 hour +/- 15 minutes later by an 8 mg intravenous injection of labeled calcium chloride (46Ca). Crewmembers were to be fasting and unexercised for at least 10 hours prior to testing. Blood was collected 24 hours +/- 1 hour after the 46Ca injection. The blood was allowed to clot at +4 degrees Celsius for a minimum of 1 hour to a maximum of 3 hours prior to centrifugation. The separated plasma was stored for calcium and bone regulatory hormone determinations (PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D metabolites, and osteocalcin) following return to earth. Calcium isotopic enrichments in biological samples were determined using thermal ionization mass spectrometry. Absorption data were determined using the ratio of the two isotopes.

All foods, fluids and medications consumed were recorded in flight using a Bar Code Reader and/or recorded in a log book. The intake of certain dietary elements, especially calcium and Vitamin D, was evaluated postflight as part of the calcium absorption and kinetics determinations. This enabled investigators to understand the impact of dietary calcium on bone loss. The type and duration of exercise were also logged using the Bar Code Reader.

Bone density measurements were made preflight and postflight using Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA). Whole body DEXA scans were used to calculate total body and regional bone mass. Any associated loss of bone mass during the study was observed by this same technique. Three sessions were scheduled preflight for baseline data. Postflight, bone density determinations were to be made during the first week after landing and at 3 months, 6 months, and once every 6 months until recovery (but no longer than three years). Calcium absorption/kinetics were determined at landing and monthly thereafter (concurrent with bone density determinations).

RESULTS:
Analysis of hormone levels in blood serum revealed that concentrations of intact PTH were reduced almost 30% during flight compared to preflight. Calcidiol (25-0H Vitamin D) decreased during winter training in Russia (compared to autumn in Houston), and decreased further during flight. Calcitriol ((1,25(0H)2 Vitamin D) concentrations were also reduced almost 30% during flight. Osteocalcin tended to decrease during winter, and was increased or unchanged during flight. Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase concentrations were an average of 39% lower on flight day 14 and 11% lower on flight day 110 than preflight values. Serum total calcium was unchanged, the ionized fraction increased slightly. Venous whole blood pH did not change.

Despite incomplete urine collections inflight and postflight, investigators concluded that urinary calcium excretion was higher during flight than before. Urinary collagen metabolite excretion (n-telopeptide and pyridinium crosslinks) was almost 40% greater than the preflight level. Calcium absorption decreased during (38+/-18% below preflight values on flight day 110) and after flight (56+/-9% below preflight on landing day, n=2; and 29+/-28% below preflight on return +6 days). Three months postflight calcium absorption had returned to 4+/-20% below preflight values. These data showed that inflight bone loss was associated with increased resorption and decreased formation. Calcium balance was further altered by decreased PTH activation of calcidiol, with subsequent decreased calcium absorption.
