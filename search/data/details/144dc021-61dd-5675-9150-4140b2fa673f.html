Genotypic and phenotypic responses of Candida albicans to spaceflight

Record ID: ARC00XX140
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Modeling the microgravity environment, using rotating suspension culture bioreactors, has allowed investigation of eukaryotic cellular responses in a ground-based model and has provided insights into the impact of spaceflight on cellular physiology. Extrapolation of results from modeled systems to that expected in space is useful and informative, but evaluating the impact of actual space exposure strengthens the validity and interpretation of data from both environments. Using the Candida albicans (C. albicans) yeast system, it is demonstrated that simulated microgravity affects differential expression of several groups of genes, including those related to morphogenic transformation, environmental sensing and ergosterol biosynthesis; induces morphologic changes such as filamentation, biofilm formation and colony morphology; and results in an increased resistance to the antimicrobial agent Amphotericin B. The constellation of these physiological responses is consistent with a potential increase in the virulence of this opportunistic yeast pathogen. The focus of the current proposal is to characterize physiological outcome measures of C. albicans following exposure to flight on the International Space Station (ISS). The overall objective of this experiment is to evaluate the response of C. albicans to spaceflight in order to 1) predict the potential health risk posed by this opportunistic pathogen in the spaceflight environment, and 2) to further characterize the phenotypic and genotypic response of a eukaryotic system to conditions of microgravity.

APPROACH:
The Micro-6 experiment will provide new insights into better management and treatment of Candida infections. Candida albicans was chosen since extensive studies have provided a broad set of benchmarks, including the sequence of the entire genome, against which results can be compared. By comparing the cells grown in microgravity to cells grown in normal gravity, the susceptibility of the yeast to an antimicrobial agent will be examined. The equipment consists of BioServe's flight certified hardware: Group Activation Packs (GAPs) stored in a Commercial Generic Bioprocessing Apparatus (CGBA), an incubator capable of controlling the temperature between 8ºC and 37ºC. Each GAP contains eight Fluid Processing Apparatus (FPA) inserts designed to meet the unique requirement of mixing fluids in microgravity. Each FPA contains an isolated amount of the microbial culture of Candida, a growth medium, and a termination reagent or fixative. The experiment will be initiated by increasing the incubator temperature to 30ºC and activating the FPAs by mixing the Candida with a growth medium. After 24 or 50 hours, depending on the sample, the experiment will be terminated by combining a fixative agent to effectively stop the growth of the yeast cultures. After the yeast return from the three-week flight aboard the space station, changes to the genetics within the organism will be examined.

RESULTS:
A primary concern was the survival of the yeast stored in water until experimental processing and then, for the appropriate experiments, survival during return to Earth. As predicted based on our ground-based assessment, the yeast survived the pre-experimental-processing storage in water as well as the post-experiment storage during the trip back to Earth. The next basic analysis was to assess whether yeast grow more quickly when exposed to the microgravity environment, as has been documented for some prokaryotes. Our data would suggest that there is not a significant difference in growth between ground and flight samples (data not shown) although design of appropriate ground control conditions is difficult due to sedimentation or employing a rotation device. Perhaps more interesting is the suggestive, but not significant data, that the longer cells are exposed to flight, the slower they are likely to grow relative to ground.

Our preliminary LSMMG studies demonstrated that cells acquired a complex and highly convoluted colony morphology following growth in the HARV. In the Micro-6 study, colonies with similar morphology as was observed during LSMMG where not observed in the live samples when plated upon return to the Montana laboratory. Actually, the colonies derived from flight cells were more normal than those derived from ground based samples. Either exposure to microgravity was not long enough to induce similar changes (phenotype takes 5-8 days of continuous culture in HARV to observe, whereas cells in flight only grew 1.5-2 days), the phenotype reverted during the 4 days of gravity exposure before the analysis could be performed (although the phenotype was relatively irreversible when induced by LSMMG), or the response to microgravity is different than the response to LSMMG.

Our ground-based studies demonstrated that yeast grown under LSMMG conditions were more resistant to the effects of the antifungal agent Amphotericin B. For this reason, we evaluated the ability of cells to grow in the presence of the antifungal agent while on the ISS. Cells were then fixed on station and cell growth was quantified upon sample return. In addition, viable cells were exposed to AB upon sample return and cell viability was analyzed by flow cytometry. Flight samples were significantly more resistant to the highest dose (2.4 mg/L) of drug, similar to the results obtained in our LSMMG studies. Although less conclusive, both in-flight and post-flight samples showed a trend toward increased resistance at lower concentrations of drug (1.2 mg/L) as compared to ground controls.
