Assessment of Endurance Capacity by Gas Exchange and Heart Rate Kinetics During Physical Training

Record ID: EKE
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The preservation of aerobic capacity is a major goal of exercise countermeasures during weightlessness. The effectiveness and the time-consumption of the training depend on the appropriate choice of exercise protocols, and it is necessary to monitor endurance capacity throughout space flight. A widely used measurement for endurance capacity is maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max) determined by incremental exercise tests until exhaustion.

Main objectives:
1) Development of a diagnostic tool for the assessment of endurance capacity from respiratory and cardiovascular kinetics in response to changes in exercise intensity.
2) Development of a physiological model to explore the delay and distortion of muscle VO2 signals during their travel to the lungs.

APPROACH:
Investigators introduced specific exercise tests (EKE) during pre- and postflight baseline data collection (BDC) in addition to the regular pre-flight testing and NASA sponsored VO2max study. The data from both the maximal and sub-maximal exercise tests were utilized to analyze VO2 max and heart rate kinetics both in time and frequency: low exercise intensities to determine oxygen uptake kinetics, intermediate workloads to study the onset of blood lactate accumulation, and the individual limit of performance capacity at peak. During these special EKE BDC sessions, the crewmember performed a long exercise protocol while breathing through the Pulmonary Function System (PFS) for breath analysis. Heart rate and blood pressure were monitored, as well as the workload.

RESULTS:
The EKE tests performed by crewmembers before and after space flight show a significant change in oxygen uptake (VO2) muscle kinetics while there were very little changes to VO2 respiratory kinetics. The kinetics of heart rate were not affected by microgravity during space flight. However, the absolute values of heart rate during the low and high step were significantly influenced by the prolonged stay on the International Space Station (ISS). Cardiac output changed significantly but stroke volume did not. Significant changes from pre- to post flight weren’t seen in VO2max, however, a significant increase was noted during the post flight period. In addition, significant changes were noted in workload max during the pre- to post flight period.
