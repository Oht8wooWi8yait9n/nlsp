Occupant Protection Data Mining and Modeling Project

Record ID: Occupant_Protection
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Current NASA Occupant Protection standards and requirements are based on extrapolations of biodynamic models, which were based on human tests performed during pre-Shuttle human flight programs. In these tests, occupants were in different suit and seat configurations than are expected for the Multi-Purpose Crew Vehicle (MPCV) and Commercial Crew programs. As a result, there is limited statistical validity to the Occupant Protection standards. Furthermore, the current standards and requirements have not been validated in relevant space flight suit and seat configurations or expected loading conditions.

To address the limitations of the current NASA standards, this study addressed the following two objectives: 1) Develop a finite element (FE) model of a Test Device for Human Occupant Restraint (THOR) Anthropomorphic Test Device (ATD), and 2) Conduct data mining of existing human injury and response data using the THOR FE model.

APPROACH:
In order to develop updated standards, adequate injury assessment tools must be chosen and developed. In the case of dynamic loads, the THOR ATD was chosen as the appropriate human surrogate. For the data mining portion of the task, recreation of the conditions of each impact case was needed to determine injury risk. Since physical recreation of each case is not feasible, a numerical model of the THOR ATD was desired. An existing THOR FE model was refined and validated. To supplement available THOR ATD validation data, additional THOR ATD testing was conducted at two facilities and ATD response data was collected. The FE model responses were then be assessed against the physical ATD responses. Once the ATD model was validated, it was used for the data mining portion of the study.

Because analogous space flight injury biomechanics data are very limited, data mining of other analogous environments were used. These datasets were chosen as they have similarities with the landing environment expected in future vehicles. The existing human injury and response data from other sources include historical military volunteer testing, automotive Crash Injury Research Engineering Network (CIREN), IndyCar impacts, and NASCAR impacts. These data sources can allow better extrapolation of the ATD responses to off-nominal conditions above the nominal range that can safely be tested in humans. These elements were used to develop injury risk functions for each of the injury metrics measured from the ATD.

RESULTS:
The first objective of this study was to assess the THOR for use by NASA in developing and validating occupant protection standards. THOR was impact tested in various orientations and multiple peak acceleration levels, and responses to these impacts differed from human responses under identical impact conditions and do not replicate human bracing for impact. As part of this study, a FE model of the THOR was developed, calibrated, and optimized. The FE model proved a good match to the ATD. The FE model simulations (at a wide range of impacts that military subjects participated in) historically revealed that the FE model correlated to lower-peak acceleration impacts better than to higher-peak accelerations.

The second objective of this study was to mine existing human injury and exposure data. The U.S. Air Force maintains thousands of human-impact-test results. Data collected from 1976 to 2004 have been downloaded and converted into a user-friendly MATLAB data structure format. The team created injury assessment reference values (IARVs) for several metrics mined from existing injury literature. The IARVs for the spine and chest deflection subsequently were refined based upon statistical models of combined non-injurious and injurious literature data. This work culminated in the development of an acceptable risk of injury from space flight, which included a newly developed operationally relevant injury scale.
