Protein Turnover During Space Flight

Record ID: 9501048
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objective of this experiment was to investigate the effects of space flight on whole-body protein metabolism by simultaneously assessing protein turnover and hormonal concentrations. Results from this experiment would provide crucial information for the establishment of a viable ground-based model for protein metabolism. Such a model would provide researchers the capability to test potential countermeasures and determine nutritional requirements for future extended-duration missions, as well as conferring benefits to healthy and metabolically stressed individuals on Earth.

APPROACH:
Whole-body protein turnover, including muscle protein breakdown, was assessed in two subjects before, during and after flight. Blood and urine samples were collected during all phases of the study. Hormonal and biochemical concentrations were measured to better understand neuroendocrine indices of protein metabolism during space flight. Food, fluid, drug intake, and amount and type of exercise were monitored. Body composition was assessed before and after flight. Preflight data were compared with inflight data to determine the effect of microgravity on each subject. Postflight data were used to monitor the readaptation process.

Blood: Blood samples were collected preflight, inflight and postflight during several 72-hour periods. These collection periods began 60 days before launch (L-60), L-35, on flight day 3 (FD3), FD6, 5 days after flight (R+5), and R+14. The protocol was similar for all collection sessions. Baseline specimens were drawn as soon as possible after awakening and before the first meal. Five milliliters (ml) of blood were drawn via butterfly into a serum separator (SST) VacutainerTM tube. An additional 2.7 ml blood sample was collected via monovette syringe for fasting glucose and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) determination with a commercial portable clinical blood analyzer (PCBA). After the fasting blood draw, each subject ingested 15N-alanine (approximately 333 micromole/kg) in capsule form.

Twelve hours after 15N-alanine ingestion, a second butterfly was inserted into the opposite arm from the initial blood draw. The subject was infused with 3-methyl-d3-histidine (1.3 micromole/kg to measure muscle protein breakdown. This was followed by a 5 ml flush of sterile saline to insure that all the tracer was delivered into the subject's circulatory system. Ten minutes later, a third butterfly was inserted in the contralateral arm and 5 ml of blood were drawn into a SST VacutainerTM. Additional blood draws (5 ml into SST) were collected 24, 48, and 72 hours after 15N-alanine ingestion. During these times, an additional 2.7 ml blood sample was collected via monovette syringe for fasting glucose and BUN determination by PCBA.

In flight, blood samples were centrifuged and stored frozen until returned for analysis. The time of inflight blood draws was recorded using a bar-code reader; relative time of day was also known (i.e., hours after awakening). After flight, specimens were analyzed for the 15N enrichments of urea and ammonia by gas chromatography / mass spectrometry. Muscle protein breakdown was determined by computer modeling of the concentration of 3-methyl-d3-histidine circulating in the blood. Serum cortisol and insulin were measured by radioimmunoassay to assess the endocrine regulation of protein metabolism and turnover.

Urine: Urine samples were collected as separate voids. Subjects were provided with small coolers and ice packs to store samples. Sample bottles were collected by the research team at least twice a day. Samples were logged and void volumes measured. Each sample was analyzed to determine protein turnover. Aliquots were removed from each sample and frozen. These frozen aliquots were used to construct 24-hour pools to determine total nitrogen excretion and nitrogen balance. Data collection took place over several 24-hour periods beginning with the first void prior to the 15N-alanine ingestion at L-60, L-35, R+5, and R+14.

During flight, urine voids were collected for 24-hour periods beginning on FD3 and on FD6 in specially configured urine collection bags. Each collection bag contained a known amount of lithium chloride (LiCl) as a volume marker. One aliquot was obtained from each void and stored frozen for processing after return to Earth. All urine aliquots were logged using a bar code reader. After flight, the urine volume per void was calculated via the LiCl marker and all samples were analyzed individually to determine protein turnover. The aliquots from FD3 and FD6 were used to construct separate 24-hour pools to determine total nitrogen excretion and nitrogen balance.

Urea and ammonia 15N enrichments were determined in all specimens by gas chromatography/mass spectrometry after flight. Although ammonia analysis depended on the ability to maintain frozen samples until return to Earth, it provided the added advantage of calculating protein turnover by averaging a rapid and smaller pool (ammonia) with a larger and slower (urea) pool. Total nitrogen (needed to determine body nitrogen balance) was determined from 24-hour urine pools by the Kjeldahl method.

Dietary Intake: Prepared and weighed meals were provided by certified dietitians during the 72 hour periods at L-60 to L-58, L-35 to L-33, R+5 to R+7, and R+14 to R+16 following the 15N-alanine ingestion. Three-day monitoring allowed any abrupt dietary changes that might affect protein turnover to be identified. In addition, subjects were asked to record any medications or supplements taken, duration and type of exercise, and phase of the menstrual cycle (if applicable).

During space flight, food/fluid/drug/exercise intakes were recorded and monitored by means of a bar-code reader during two 3-day periods: from awakening on FD3 to FD5 (inclusive) and, similarly, from FD6 to FD8 (inclusive). The bar code device had an integrated laser that allowed the user to scan items using only one hand. The bar-code reader logged the following information: subject ID, Mission Elapsed Time (MET), and food item (and lot number). This information was stored in the bar-code reader's internal memory to be retrieved after return to Earth.

Body Composition: Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was used to determine body composition (lean body mass) preflight and postflight. Whole-body scans were obtained on L-60 and L-35 and again on R+2, R+5, and R+14. Leg scans were subdivided at the knee into upper and lower regions to allow preflight-to-postflight comparisons of locomotor muscles.

RESULTS:
Space flight results in a down regulation of protein turnover; however, protein breakdown exceeds protein synthesis. In addition, hormonal changes which accompany space flight are conducive to a net loss of muscle and body protein. Nutritional, exercise or pharmacological interventions which promote net protein synthesis must be considered.
