HCAAM VNSCOR: Responsive Multimodal Human-Automation Communication for Augmenting Human Situation Awareness in Nominal and Off-Nominal Scenarios

Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES
Crew extravehicular activity (EVA) is limited on spaceflight missions. Multiple, small robotic spacecrafts with varying levels of autonomy are needed to perform tasks that might have been completed by an astronaut (e.g., an exterior surface inspection or repair). Crews on long duration exploration missions (LDEM) will have less access to ground support during task operations. As a result, they will need to process more information and communicate with autonomous robots effectively to ensure tasks are progressing safely and on schedule.
The objective of these studies are to investigate the use of virtual reality (VR) multimodal interface displays and communication pathways for improving human-robot communication, situation awareness (SA), trust, and task performance. This will lead to developing guidelines for designing human-robot system interactions that enable operational performance for crews on spaceflight missions.
 
The specific aims are to:
 
1) Develop a simulation testbed for examining communication between human-robot teams.
 
2) Develop a hardware testbed for examining communication between human-robot teams.
 
3) Evaluate human SA, trust, and task performance within a short duration and long-duration ground-based study (simulation and/or hardware) through testing various interface communication modalities and information displays.
 
4) (Option) Perform follow up studies for additional parameters of interest that could be tested using the study set up. Additional parameters include timing and persistence of information, gesture command mapping, or varying the levels of robot automation
 
5) (Option) Extend the ground-based study to a flight demonstration aboard the International Space Station (ISS)

APPROACH
 
Proposed ground testbed will simulate an LDEM inspection task. In some trials, the human subjects maneuver free-flying vehicles to obtain video imagery of the space craft exterior. In other trials the human subjects will only perform visual inspection of previously collected imagery.
 
The satellites used in the simulation study are modeled after the Synchronized Position Hold Engage Reorient Experimental Satellites (SPHERES). SPHERES are small free-floating autonomous satellites that have been used to study precision navigation and maneuvering in zero gravity experiments aboard the ISS as well as in ground-based experiments. These satellites are predecessors to the newer Astrobees satellites, which are now on the ISS.
 
For the ground-based hardware testbed, we integrate small drones to simulate the 3D positioning of a small satellite for a ground-environment. Video imagery captured by cameras on the drones will be used for automatic scene reconstruction to enhance astronaut perception of the environment. This creates a 3D model of the environment which can then by manipulated in the AR display for navigation and to obtain feedback of the surroundings.
For the simulation studies, subjects will be asked to navigate one or more semi-autonomous satellites to perform exterior inspection of a simulated “station.” Simulations will include surface anomalies that are critical and non-critical that must be discovered. Subjects will be able to control the direction of the satellites by using a joystick, computer keypad, or gesture (depending on the study phase).
 
Three visual interfaces will be examined: (1) The computer screen (CS nominal) mode provides the camera images obtained from the satellites and cameras on the station mock-up on computer monitors, (2) The computer screen exocentric mode (CS exocentric) provides an additional exocentric view on a separate computer monitor, and (3) AR environment (AR exocentric) which presents a scaled 3D model of the station mock-up to the subject built from the satellite camera images. Optional studies will include levels of automation, additional command inputs, and added environmental complexities.
 
Short-term studies will collect data over a span of two days, recording measures such as task completion time, number of anomalies detected, trust, mental workload, and SA. Long-term studies include testing within HERA campaigns, which will be 45 days long. Here additional analysis will examine how human-robot interaction strategies change with longer exposures.

RESULTS

The PI team performed 5 studies associated with this research program. The U-M Inspection Study and the HERA Inspection study used the simulation testbed for the inspection task. This simulation testbed was then extended to address additional research questions for the Docking Study. These studies addressed Aim 3. Aim 4 included 2 studies that evaluated the influence of the AR display modality on sensorimotor performance. The studies that addressed Aim 4 were the Sensorimotor Study and the Dynamic and Operational Task Study.
Both the HERA and U-M inspection studies support decreased detection accuracy using AR for this task and increase in detection accuracy during Synchronous interactions. Additional interactions with the simulation did not improve the AR outcomes to match that of the 2D display. Factors influencing the decrease in performance of AR for this task include the reduced contrast and resolution of the presented image, as well as reliability of hand interactions.
The UM docking study formalized the construct of path legibility, which presented a way to incorporate human interpretation of the motion plan into control systems, potentially supporting monitor situation awareness by influencing the monitor's takeover decision-making. The motion plan of a robotic agent is another communication pathway that can be used to convey information and support human decision making.
The UM sensorimotor study considered how holographic targets impact users’ accuracy, precision, movement time, throughput, and error rates when compared to a touchscreen device. Four distinct AR errors were discovered with varying frequencies of occurrence. The highest frequency errors were misalignments across all three axes (70.4% of errors), implying that the fingertip was not aligned in the button plane or depth axis. The second highest frequency error were misalignments in the depth axis (20.7% of errors). The tactile feedback and physical touch limits offered by the touchscreen may have supported the improved accuracy over AR where the finger was free to travel through the target.
In the UM Dynamic Operational Tasks study, the participants were able to complete both tasks in AR and meaningful measures of postural control were obtained, indicating that although different baseline performance is obtained, AR may be a useful instrumentation solution with embedded sensors to evaluate a variety of populations for postural control. The differences observed support the need for implementation- specific baselines. Participants adopted strategies similar to sensorimotor impaired crewmembers, with increased task time and reduced head and torso angular velocities. Contrastingly, participants exhibited increased head pitch angles, which is typically reduced in astronauts postflight.
