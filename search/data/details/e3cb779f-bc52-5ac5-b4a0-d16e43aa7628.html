Biomechanics of Movement During Locomotion

Record ID: 4.2.4a
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
During space flight, adaptive processes recalibrate the central nervous system to permit new sensorimotor strategies to emerge in microgravity. However, the adaptive state achieved on orbit is inappropriate for a 1-G environment, which leads to postural and gait instabilities upon return to Earth. These instabilities have been reported by both U.S. astronauts and Russian cosmonauts even after short duration (5-10 days) space flights. Subjects experienced a turning sensation while attempting to walk a straight path, encountered sudden loss of postural stability especially when rounding corners, perceived exaggerated pitch and rolling head movements while walking, and experienced sudden loss of orientation in unstructured visual environments. In addition, oscillopsia and disorienting illusions of self-motion and surround-motion occurred during head movement induced by locomotion.

The objectives of this study were to (1) determine whether eye-head-trunk coordination strategies, which occur during terrestrial locomotion, were modified following long-duration space flight and (2) ascertain whether these changes were associated with disturbances in gaze control, lower limb kinematics and muscle activity patterns of the leg during locomotion. This information will aid in the design and evaluation of sensorimotor countermeasures against the deleterious effects of long-duration space flight.

APPROACH:
During each test session, subjects were required to walk and run on a motorized treadmill while visually fixating on a centrally located Earth-fixed target (light-emitting diode, LED) positioned either 2 meters (FAR target) or 30 centimeters (NEAR target) from the subject, and at the height of subject perceived eye level. Both FAR and NEAR visual targets were placed on stands and viewed within the context of a full-field visual scene consisting of the laboratory surroundings. During fixation of the FAR target, a wall was visible approximately 0.5 meters behind the LED. For the NEAR target, the wall was 2.2 meters behind the LED. This task was designed to investigate postflight alterations in eye-head-trunk coordination of gaze stabilization during locomotion and determine whether these changes induced commensurate alterations in muscle activity patterns of the legs and lower limb kinematics.

Three trials were performed by the subjects while walking/running on the treadmill. The trials, which lasted 20 seconds each, included slow walking (4 km/hr), fast walking (6.4 km/hr), and running (9.6 km/hr). All trials were repeated three times while fixating on a FAR target. Fast walking was also repeated three additional times while focusing on a NEAR target. These trials were scheduled for 120, 45 and 10 days preflight and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12 days postflight.

Before beginning the test, the subject straddled the treadmill track while the treadmill speed was increased. When the treadmill track reached the required speed, the subject was free to begin walking. A few strides were permitted for the subject to become comfortable with the speed of the treadmill and to attain a steady gait. After a verbal "ready" indication from the subject, data collection began, and the subject continued to walk/run while visually fixating on the target. The subject was instructed to fixate on the target for the full duration of the trial. To prevent falling, each subject wore a torso harness that was attached to an overhead frame. During nominal treadmill performance, this harness provided no support and did not interfere with the natural movement of the head or limbs.

To measure head and trunk movements, passive retro-reflective markers, which served as tracking guides, were affixed to the vertex, occipital, and right temporal positions of the head, on the seventh cervical vertebrae (C7) of the neck, and on the sacrum. Leg movements were measured by placing markers on the feet, ankles, knees and hips. The movements of these markers were simultaneously recorded with four video cameras that sampled concurrent video images at 60 Hz. The position of each marker was uniquely determined using a video-based motion analysis system. Subjects were outfitted with shorts, sleeveless shirts, athletic socks, and running shoes with electronic force sensors attached to the heels and toes so that times of heel strike and toe off could be determined.

Marker trajectories were low-pass filtered at 10 Hz using a finite impulse response filter with a Hamming window. Movement of the head in the sagittal plane (pitch) was characterized by the angle between the horizontal and the line connecting the vertex and occipital markers. Vertical (z-axis) trunk translation was determined from the displacement of the marker placed on the C7 vertebrae.

Surface electrodes were applied above and below the left eye, and behind the left ear to measure compensatory vertical eye movements using standard DC electro-oculographic (EOG) methods. To measure muscle activity patterns of the legs, preamplifier surface electromyogram (EMG) electrodes were placed on the subject's rectus femoris, biceps femoris, tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius in parallel to the muscle fibers. The electrodes were attached with hypoallergenic tape and then covered with elastic leg wraps to prevent movement on the skin. The analog EMG data were bandpassed at 30-300 Hz before being digitized at 752 Hz. Foot-switch information, also sampled at 752 Hz, was stored within the EMG data files.

Compensatory pitch head movement waveforms were subjected to Fourier Analysis, and the amplitude of the predominant frequency was determined. Each 20-second trial was divided into 4 epochs of 4 seconds duration. The frequency spectra of each 4-second epoch was then calculated separately. Thus, for each subject in three walking sessions, twelve individual epochs were analyzed and the predominant peak determined, which allowed a mean peak amplitude to be determined for each subject.

RESULTS:
In general, results were consistent throughout the Mir 18 and Mir 19 studies. Crewmembers showed significant alterations in head-trunk coordination following long-duration space flight. This change in head-trunk coordination disrupted gaze stabilization during locomotion and may have led to disorganization and descending control of locomotor function. Crewmembers also showed some disruption in lower limb muscle activation patterns during locomotion that exceeded the disruption seen in Shuttle crewmembers. In addition, microgravity induced alterations in head-trunk coordination during locomotion may play a central role in astronaut locomotor dysfunction which occurs following space flight.

During preflight testing, pitch head movements acted in a compensatory fashion to oppose vertical trunk translation during locomotion: as the trunk translated upward, the head pitched forward/downward, thereby maintaining target fixation. Following space flight, Fourier Spectral Analysis indicated a significant increase in pitch head amplitude during locomotion. Test subjects B, D and E showed a significant reduction in the predominant frequency amplitude while fast walking (6.4 km/hr) that persisted through the postflight data collection period. However, subjects B and D demonstrated a recovery trend during the postflight testing period. Alternatively, subject A showed an increase in amplitude across all walking/target conditions. Subject C exhibited no discernible pre/post change; however, it should be noted that his first postflight data session occurred five days after landing, possibly after most of the recovery had occurred. Mir crewmembers exhibited a higher incidence and a longer lasting modification in head movement control after long-duration flight than did crewmembers of relatively shorter duration Shuttle flights.

Pearson correlation coefficients, used to compare preflight and postflight muscle activation patterns, were generally very high. Similarly high correlations were observed for the left lower limb musculature. These high correlations indicated that by the time postflight testing was conducted, the sensorimotor system was able to generate the lower limb neuromusculature activation waveforms, which permitted effective treadmill locomotion. Moreover, the subjects were able to maintain the preflight levels of activation symmetry between the right and left lower limb musculature, which was required to continue movement on the treadmill.

Despite great similarity in the phasic features of the muscle activation wave forms, analysis of the relative activation amplitudes indicated definite changes in motor control characteristics. Investigators have focused their analysis of relative activation amplitude around the behavioral events of heel strike and toe off because these events involve large transfers of energy, which if not controlled properly could result in the loss of locomotor stability. Although patterns of change were not consistent across subjects A, B and C, all displayed changes in the relative amplitude relationships among the lower limb musculature. Two Mir 18 subjects displayed greater postflight increases in the relative amplitude of the biceps femoris (BF) around heel strike relative to changes observed in the rectus femoris (RF). This pattern of postflight change indicated that the knee extensor/hip flexor musculature must generate more musculature tension relative to the RF compared to preflight patterns. Another example was the increase in relative activation of the gastrocnemius (GA) and tibialis anterior (TA) around toe-off. This increase in both antagonists suggested a generalized relative increase in ankle joint stiffness. Given that effective toe-off involves rapid changes from a large plantar flexor moment (GA activation and TA inhibition) to a large dorsiflexor moment (TA activation and GA inhibition), a generalized increase in ankle stiffness was not an optimal control strategy. Subjects D and E showed the greatest postflight changes in the activity pattern of the RF while fast walking. The findings observed in the Mir 18 and Mir 19 subjects are consistent with those found after short-duration Shuttle missions. In general, the consistent findings of alterations in the relative amplitude relationships across the lower limb musculature indicate the precise neuromusculature control necessary for maximally efficient locomotion was disrupted after long-duration space flight.

Analysis of lower limb kinematics during locomotion indicated altered coordination patterns during the load acceptance phase of the gait cycle. The implication of this finding was that the normal energy modulation occurring during heel strike may be altered leading to increased energy transmission to the head causing postflight visual-vestibular disturbances.

Concerns regarding postflight walking and head control include management of the shock wave created at the moment of foot contact, and the transmission of this shock wave to the head. Evidence suggests that inappropriate shock wave transmission to the head while walking could interfere with head and gaze control, resulting in a compromise of dynamic visual acuity and possibly a disruption in descending vestibular control of locomotion. To address this issue in crewmember performance, analyses were focused on the lower limb joint kinematic data on the load acceptance phase of each stride as defined by the time period calculated from right heel strike to the following maximum of knee angle flexion. For this functionally defined time period, the total joint angular displacement for the ankle, knee and hip joints were calculated, as well as the average joint angular velocity for the knee and hip joints.
