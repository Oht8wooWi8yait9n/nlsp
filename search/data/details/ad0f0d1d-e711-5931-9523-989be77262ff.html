Adaptation to Space Motion Sickness by Means of Prism Goggles Training

Record ID: NAS917458
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Nearly a century before astronauts first flew into orbit, people who donned inverting lenses reported dizziness, disorientation, miss-reaching for objects, and great queasiness, especially during rapid head movements. In the 1980’s, a ground-based motion sickness study conducted for the U.S. Air Force demonstrated the similar symptoms when subjects wore an up-down reversing prism. It was observed, however, that eye-hand coordination improved significantly after only one hour.

The objective of this experiment was to determine if astronauts’ experiences with space motion sickness were similar, in certain respects, to the experiences of a prism-wearer on earth. If the experiences shared some similarity, and because adaptation to prism-wearing has been demonstrated, it was hoped that astronauts could be pre-adapted before space flight using up-down reversing prisms. Adaptation would occur prior to flight by means of training on earth.

APPROACH:
Subjects were assessed for susceptibility to motion sickness, using a Visual Motion Sickness Rating Scale (VMSRS) developed by the principal investigator, performing head movement/eye fixation tests for up to six minutes while wearing a pair of up-down reversing prism glasses.

During the experimental phase, the training battery consisted of the following tasks and the VMSRS, and was given to the subject in the order listed below:

  • One Leg Balance Test
  • VMSRS
  • Eye-Hand Coordination Test: stack 8 small cubes
  • VMSRS
  • Gross Motor Coordination Test: stack large cubes
  • VMSRS
  • Catch ball on pendulum
  • VMSRS
  • Head Movement/Eye Fixation 80 bpm
  • VMSRS
  • Calisthenics
  • VMSRS

    Subjects were asked to rate their perceptions of dizziness, queasiness, and body unsteadiness on a 5-point VMSRS scale ranging from #1 (“I feel fine”) to #5 (“I am extremely dizzy/queasy/unsteady”) several times during this sequence of tasks. No one was asked to proceed to the point of illness. If at any time a subject reported #3 (“I feel moderately dizzy or I feel moderately queasy”) rating assessment, testing would stop with the subject being judged susceptible.

    After these tasks, the subjects were interviewed individually. Each was asked to compare, in detail, their in-flight experiences with the prism goggles. The determination that an astronaut experienced motion sickness was made when the astronauts: (1) said they were ill, and their description of symptoms fit the motion sickness profile; (2) took medication to alleviate severe symptoms; or (3) vomited.
