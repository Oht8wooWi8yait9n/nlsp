Mechanism of Clustered DNA Double-strand Break Repair in Response to HZE Particles in Human Cells

Record ID: NNX13AD57G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
DNA damage and consequent mutations initiate the multi-step carcinogenic process. Substantial evidence indicates that high charge and energy (HZE) particles, component of galactic cosmic rays, induced clustered DNA damages are difficult to repair, and in some instances are irreparable. This has been associated with the increased relative biological effectiveness of chromosomal aberrations, mutagenesis, and carcinogenesis. However, it is unclear why clustered lesions are difficult to repair, and the mechanisms by which unrepaired clustered DNA lesions trigger carcinogenic events are not known. The goals of this research were to utilize the unique capability to quantitatively estimate the ability of cells to repair clustered DNA lesions in response to a spectrum of HZE particles, and to dissect the genetic factors that may potentially be involved in the processing of complex DNA lesions.

The specific aims of this study were to test the following hypothesis:

  1. The cellular ability to repair HZE particles induced clustered DNA damage is modulated by radiation quality and cell cycle stages, utilizing newly established in situ and imaging technology.
  2. Homologous recombination plays a key function in facilitating the repair of double-strand breaks (DCBs) associated with clustered DNA lesions, using key homologous recombination factors deficient cells.
  3. Fanconi Anemia (FA) complex may orchestrate the repair of complex DSBs induced by HZE particles, using a panel of FA pathway deficient human cells.

APPROACH:
Investigators studied the effects of radiation on human cells using their newly developed in situ and image technology. In addition they assessed the effects of Fe particles irradiation on DNA and chromosomal instability in human cells.

RESULTS:
Investigators identified a novel mechanism by which space radiation triggers immune signaling in human cells. They conclude that the immune signaling initiated by the space radiation will contribute to the carcinogenesis risks. In regards to RAD51, investigators found it’s critical for complex DSB repair in S/G2 cells in response to Fe-particle irradiation, it blocks MRE11-mediated degradation of nascent DNA strands in response to Fe-particle exposure, its depletion upregulates expression of innate immune signaling genes by accumulating self-DNA in the cytoplasm of S/G2 phase cells, and it suppresses chromosome breakages following Fe-particle exposure.

No datasets exist for this study. A final report was archived.
