Renal Stone Risk Assessment During Long Duration Space Flight

Record ID: 2.1.3
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to microgravity alters human physiology. A number of the recognized changes are expected to contribute to an altered urinary environment that in turn influences the potential for renal stone formation. During previous missions, the most obvious changes observed in astronauts were an increased excretion of calcium and a negative calcium balance. Both are thought to be the result of altered bone resorption processes. Other changes included increased urinary phosphate and decreased urinary output, urinary osmolality, and magnesium and citrate concentrations. All of these changes were expected to increase the urinary supersaturation of stone-forming salts and increase the risk for renal stone formation.

Data collected during short-term Shuttle flights (4-14 days duration) indicated an increase in the potential for renal stone formation, particularly for calcium oxalate and uric acid stones. Some of the renal stone risk indices also appeared to be influenced by mission duration. Therefore, it was hypothesized that long-term mission durations further increase the potential risk of renal stone formation.

To quantify the inflight and postflight risk for renal stone formation, the investigation had three objectives:

    (1) To determine the effects of exposure to microgravity on the potential for renal stone formation.
    (2) To determine the effect of mission duration on the potential risk for renal stone formation.
    (3) To determine how long after flight the increased risk exists for renal stone formation.

APPROACH:
All three Mir-18 crewmembers participated in this investigation which involved 24-hour urine collections and 48-hour diet monitoring periods.

The three crewmembers collected urine voids over a 24-hour period three times during the preflight period (L-148, L-58 and L-34), and following landing on R+0, R+6, R+10 and R+13. Inflight urine samples were collected on Mission Day (MD) 14, MD 93 and MD 110. Inflight, individual urine voids were collected into a urine collection device containing 1 ml lithium chloride as a volume marker. Two 7 ml urine aliquots were drawn into syringe tubes containing 0.05% thymol or 0.1% thimerosal, mixed well and stored at ambient temperatures until return to Earth for analyses. Following the return of the samples, urine volumes were determined from the diluted lithium concentration. Twenty-four-hour urine pools were constructed from the individual voids and samples were analyzed for osmolality, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, sulfate, phosphorus, magnesium, potassium and creatinine.

Pre- and postflight, the subjects collected 24-hour urine specimens. The pooled urine was stored chilled at approximately 4 degrees Celsius. After total volume and pH were recorded, two 10 ml aliquots were removed for biochemical analysis. The first sample was analyzed for creatinine, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium and uric acid. The second 10 ml aliquot was acidified with 6M hydrochloric acid (HCl) and analyzed for calcium, magnesium, citrate, oxalate and sulfate. All samples were processed immediately or stored at -20 degrees Celsius until analysis.

From the measurements, the relative urinary supersaturation of the stone forming salts were calculated and renal stone risk was estimated. The derived parameters were urinary supersaturation of calcium oxalate, brushite (calcium phosphate), sodium urate, struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) and uric acid saturation.

Detailed diet logs were maintained by each crewmember for 24 hours prior to urine collection and during the 24-hour urine collection period to assess the effect of dietary factors on the potential for renal stone formation.

RESULTS:
The overall quality of the preflight, inflight and postflight urine data were very good. Some of the individual inflight urine samples appeared to have bacterial contamination, which might have an effect on the quality of the assessment of renal stone risk. Postflight, urine samples collected on R+0 and R+1 by one crewmember were not labeled and were lost for analyses.

Inflight dietary monitoring was incomplete. Complete dietary data were received from only 2 inflight days out of the 9 inflight monitoring days that were requested. The Bar Code Reader (BCR) was not used as intended and not all dietary items were scanned. Also, a large percentage of dietary items consumed during the monitoring period were from previous missions and were not bar-code labeled. These items were not entered manually into the log book by the crew. Due to the incompleteness of the dietary database, the effects of environmental factors on the potential risk of renal stone formation are difficult to describe.

Conclusions from the Mir 18 mission can be summarized as follows:

    1. Calcium excretion was increased while urinary output was decreased. Both are believed to be a result of the exposure to microgravity. Both effects increase the risk of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stone formation.

    2. It was difficult to describe the effects of long-duration exposure to microgravity based on the Mir 18 results alone. Data analyses showed that an exaggerated risk existed even during the preflight period.

    3. The urinary output was overall very low and returned to preflight levels by R+6. Urinary calcium phosphate (brushite) values returned to preflight levels at R+10 but the risk of calcium oxalate stone formation remained in the high risk range throughout the postflight period.

A considerable analyte variation was present between the subjects participating in this investigation making general assumptions on the potential risk of renal stone formation difficult at this time. Mir 18 data sets will be combined with data from future NASA-Mir missions for more complete renal stone risk assessment.
