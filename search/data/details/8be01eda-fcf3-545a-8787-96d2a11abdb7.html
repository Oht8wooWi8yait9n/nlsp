In-Flight Evaluation of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory Electronic Nose (ENOSE)

Record ID: DTO 687
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Early detection of contaminants such as solvents and coolant, combustion, and metabolic products is necessary since air supply onboard space vehicles is limited and not easily replaced. Because contaminants may build up over time, it is important for crew health that air quality is monitored. Present air quality monitoring equipment has certain limitations that may be addressed by the Electronic Nose (ENose).

The ENose device operates on principles similar to those of the human nose and is designed to monitor changes in the atmosphere to which it is exposed. It consists of an array of several different thin film polymer sensors that are dispersed with carbon and have been shown to respond to a number of both organic and inorganic compounds. Individual sensor films are not specific to a particular gas; the use of an array of different sensor films allows gases and gas mixtures to be uniquely identified by the pattern of measured changes in the electrical resistance of the sensor. It is a rapid response and recovery sensor which can detect single and multiple compounds down to the ppm range. An ENose prototype sensor was put into the air revitalization line of the Early Human Test Initiative chamber at Johnson Space Center (JSC) where it collected data for 49 days and thus was demonstrated in a realistic ground environment. Operation in microgravity is now necessary to demonstrate the ability of the ENose to distinguish between events which are a danger to crew health, such as a coolant leak, and those which are not, such as humidity increase or cooking odors. A poor understanding of the mechanism of sensing and a lack of a proven sensor model raises questions regarding microgravity operation and increases the need for microgravity demonstration.

Theories do exist as to the mechanism for changes in the polymer films in the presence of gas but it is not possible to predict that the mechanism and operation is unchanged in microgravity. This study is intended to demonstrate the technology in microgravity and a successful demonstration will serve as a milestone for further development of a miniature, portable, and real-time air monitoring instrument.

APPROACH:
The ENose unit was deployed 2.5 hours after launch on the forward lockers in order to receive air intake into the air revitalization system; it was connected to a pre-routed cable and activated for seven flight days of continuous operation. The docking days with Space Station Mir were included. Once each day a Grab Sample was taken in close proximity to the ENose and then an alcohol wipe was waved near the ENose for approximately one minute as an event marker. The time was recorded in a log.

RESULTS:
On STS-95, the E-nose operated for 6 days and was able to accurately determine changes in humidity, confirmed by an independent humidity monitor in the Shuttle cabin. In addition, the research team would have liked to monitor the ten common contaminants that the E-nose was trained to smell, but fortunately for the crew, none of those were detected, confirmed with air samples brought back from flight.
