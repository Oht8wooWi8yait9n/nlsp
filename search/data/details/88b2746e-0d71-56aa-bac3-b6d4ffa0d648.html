Crews Health: Investigation on Reduced Operability

Record ID: CHIRO
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The CHIRO program has been established to provide early data within a wide range of researches on human upper limb behaviour and performances. The aim of this particular aspect of the research is to monitor the impairment of muscle function in weightlessness, if any, and to clarify its physiopathology through an analysis of the different steps of electro-mechanical mechanisms of muscle contraction events. The observations relevant to this activity will be carried out during the grip maintenance of held objects, for example during grasping or lifting tasks, by comparing the performance of the weightless conditions to those recorded on ground, before and after the execution of the orbital flight.

APPROACH:
The main elements of the CHIRO payload are the Pinch Force Dynamometer (PFD) (Figure 1) and the HandGrip Dynamometer (HGD) (Figure 2). These elements are based on those developed for NASAs Human Research Facility (HRF). The HGD is a hand held device capable of measuring instantaneous hand strength (between 40 and 1000 Newtons) as a function of time for periods of up to 300 seconds. The principle components of the HGD are a transducer capable of measuring the handgrip force, instrumentation amplifier, and associated cables. Dynamic voltage representing instantaneous hand strength is taken from the output of the instrumentation amplifier and sent to a laptop computer or to a data acquisition system for data manipulation, display, correlation with other data sources, and/or storage.

The PFD is a hand held device capable of measuring instantaneous strength (between 0 and 270 Newtons) of the thumb and opposing finger or groupings of fingers as a function of time for periods of up to 300 sec. The principle components of the PFD are a pinch force transducer, instrumentation amplifier, and associated cables. Dynamic voltage representing instantaneous finger(s) pinch strength as a function of time will be taken from the output of the instrumentation amplifier and sent to a laptop computer or to a data acquisition system for data manipulation, display, correlation with other data sources, and/or storage.

In both instruments the measurement of the applied compression force is taken by using a beam load cell based on a full bridge strain gauge that is sensitive to the transverse shearing stress applied to the beam. Two protocols of experimentation will be carried out.

Protocol 1: In each session, the subject is tested on force isometric tracking. A sequence of force profiles are displayed on a computer monitor, and the subjects task is to reproduce them with his dominant hand quickly and accurately by using the Handgrip dynamometer. Each profile is presented for 10 s, allowing the subject to recognize and replicate it, and the screen is then blanked for 2 s, so that the hand can return to a common starting position (set at the beginning of the session). Each session also includes an isometric tracking task with the non-dominant hand. The subject is also instructed to keep a cursor centred on the screen by increasing or decreasing the force, which they exert on a pinch force dynamometer with their nondominant hand, while the cursor position is perturbed by a first-order instable element. This task is presented over 1.5 minutes, to collect a sufficient amount of data. The two tasks are also presented concurrently in each session. The subject is instructed to assign top priority to the task carried out with the dominant hand, and perform the concurrent tracking task to the extent possible; thus, a subsidiary-task paradigm according to dual-task terminology is used. Dual-task testing takes as long as the presentation of all the force profiles for no more than 10 minutes. Total session duration is therefore about 25 minutes including rest breaks, but excluding set-up and stowage time.

Protocol 2: After calibration of the set-up, the astronaut is asked to perform the following tasks. First, maximum grip and pinch forces that the astronaut is able to produce are recorded at 100Hz over a 30s time epoch. The PC immediately computes the mean and standard deviation (SD) of these force values (Fmax for grip and Fmax for pinch). Fmax values are used to scale the sine waves for the tracking task (so as to take into account possible changes in max force output during weightless exposure). Then, the astronaut is asked to perform the tracking tasks. The force tracked with either the grip or the pinch dynamometer varies sinusoidally. Sine waves of 2 different amplitudes (10 and 25% of Fmax, to avoid fatigue) and 7 different frequencies (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1, 2, 4 Hz) are outputted according to a pseudo-random table previously stored in memory. The astronaut is asked to track the sine wave as fast and accurately as possible. The target force and the astronauts output force are displayed in real time on the PC display. The whole protocol is performed twice before launch (L-90 and L-30), 3 times in flight (Flight day (FD)2, FD5, FD8) and upon return to earth (R+0, R+1, R+5, R+10). Fmax for grip and pinch are compared statistically (ANOVA, paired t-tests) between pre-flight, in-flight and post flight conditions. As for the sine wave task, Fourier analyses are performed to compute gain and phase of the response output relative to the target force input.

RESULTS:
The upper limb is used for locomotion in a microgravity environment. Fatigue can impact the hand, musculo-skeletal degradation can be recognized in the upper limb. Specific protocols on the upper limb can facilitate the studies on learning mechanisms for the motor control. The results of such experiments can be transferred to the Earth, useful for treatment of subjects with local traumas or diseases of the Central Nervous System.
