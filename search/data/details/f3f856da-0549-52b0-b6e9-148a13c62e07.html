Effects of Prolonged Exposure to Space Radiation on Carcinogenesis and Neuroendocrine Differentiation in Human Prostate Models

Record ID: NNX10AC13G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The deleterious effects of radiation encountered in space present a significant obstacle in space exploration. Unfortunately, due to the significant differences between space radiation and radiation encountered on Earth, the risks of cancer development following exposure to space radiation cannot be reliably predicted based solely on epidemiological data collected on Earth.

This project will study the effects of space radiation on development and/or progression of prostate cancer. Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in men in the United States and the second leading cause of cancer deaths. It is also the most often diagnosed type of cancer in male astronauts. However, the connection between exposure to space radiation and the development of prostate cancer is not yet known. Several lines of evidence suggest that conversion of normal prostate or prostate cancer cells into cells secreting cell growth hormones, so called neuroendocrine cells, stimulate cancer development and/or progression. Strikingly, such conversion was recently reported in prostate cells exposed to prolonged irradiation. The low doses and long exposure times used in that study are not dissimilar to the doses and exposure times encountered during space travel.

Therefore, it is postulated that space radiation could increase the risk of prostate cancer development through the transformation of prostate cells into neuroendocrine cells during space flights. Since neuroendocrine cells seem to play an important role in the development of other important and common types of solid tumors, such as breast, lung, or colon, the insight gained during this study should be also applicable to these cancers. The results obtained during the proposed studies will allow NASA to better estimate the prostate cancer health risk(s) of space exploration, as well as provide clues that will allow the development of novel preventative measures to protect astronauts.

The specific aims of this research are to:

1. Determine if dose, dose rate and the quality of space radiation have differential effects on carcinogenesis in prostate cells.

2. Determine if prolonged exposure to space radiation can induce neuroendocrine differentiation in prostate cancer cells.

3. Determine if carcinogenesis/neoplastic transformation in prostate cells exposed to space radiation is influenced by the presence of neuroendocrine cells.

APPROACH:
Two cell lines were used. RWPE-1 is a normal epithelial line and LNCaP is an androgen dependent early stage prostate cancer line. Cells in the exponential phase of growth were exposed to either to X-rays (220 kV, at the Department of Radiation Oncology, University of Virginia) or to protons (1000 MeV/n), oxygen ions (200 MeV/n) or titanium ions (1100 MeV/n), all at the NASA Space Radiation Laboratory (Brookhaven National Laboratory, Upton, NY).

The effect of irradiation on cell survival was measured by clonogenic cell survival assay. In some experiments, cells were pre-treated with neurotensin (NT, 100 nM/1h) and/or SR48692 inhibitor (SR, 1µM/24h) before irradiation. The effect of irradiation on cells' ability to survive and proliferate under attachment-independent conditions was measured by soft agar (transformation) colony formation assay. Levels of protein expression and/or post-translational modifications were assessed by standard Western blotting technique. The expression of genes of interest was assessed by semi-quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) and/or quantitative RT-PCR using a Bio-Rad CFX96 quantitative PCR instrument.

RESULTS:
The data obtained in this project demonstrate that radiation dose and quality have clear differential effects not just on DNA damage and cell killing, but more importantly on the initiation of prostate carcinogenesis. While it is not possible to irradiate cells at the low fluences that would be experienced by astronauts during space exploration, the data also demonstrate that fractionated irradiations, but not a single acute exposure, induce mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition or neuroendocrine differentiation of normal prostate cells and prostate cancer cells. Further, this work defines signaling pathways and genetic alterations that effect these molecular and phenotypic changes that are involved in radiation-induced prostate cancer initiation and progression. As such, this project increases the mechanistic understanding of the fundamental biological processes involved in space radiation-induced carcinogenesis and provide NASA with important information regarding the potential increase risk of prostate cancer as a result of space radiation exposure.
