Predicting Vulnerability to Performance Impairment from Sleep Loss

Record ID: NAG91161
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Studies by NASA have shown that astronauts obtain only approximately 6 hours of sleep per day on average during space flight missions. Laboratory experiments have revealed that within a few days, such reduction of sleep can result in enhanced probability of performance failure. This vulnerability may be exposed during emergencies in space—due to equipment failure, mission timeline problems, or other stressors—involving extended mission demands and further sleep loss. However, there are substantial inter-individual differences in the magnitude of performance failure during extended wakefulness. This investigation of human vulnerability to performance failure is the first quantitative study of the biological and neurobehavioral factors that discriminate and predict these inter-individual differences.

The study consisted of repeated assessment of neurobehavioral vulnerability in a wide range of performance functions for n=21 subjects during 36 hours of laboratory-based sleep deprivation with relatively high work demands, on 3 separate occasions. These deprivations were preceded by either a week of sleep satiation (12 hours in bed per day) or a week of sleep restriction (6 hours in bed per day). This design served to address the following four specific aims: To determine the extent to which individual performance responses to extended mission demands with prolonged wakefulness reflect (1) recent sleep-wake history, and/or (2) trait-like neurobehavioral vulnerability, and/or (3) sensitization or adaptation as a consequence of previous exposure; and (4) to identify potential biological, behavioral, psychological, and/or demographic predictors of vulnerability to performance failure for future prospective evaluation and countermeasure development.

This project addressed fundamental issues about the sources and predictability of the large inter-individual differences in performance failure during sustained work involving sleep loss. The project had two broad goals: Determine the extent to which performance responses to sustained mission demands with prolonged wakefulness reflect state-like vulnerability that occurs due to variation in recent sleep-wake history, and/or stable, trait-like neurobehavioral vulnerability that occurs as a consequence of enduring biological factors, and/or evolving vulnerability due to prior exposure to sleep loss (i.e., sensitization or adaptation); Identify potential biological, behavioral, psychological, and/or demographic predictors of vulnerability to performance failure during sustained mission demands with prolonged wakefulness for subsequent prospective evaluation.

The investigation had the following four specific aims:

1. Quantify the extent to which the magnitude of performance failure during extended waking performance is dependent on variation in recent sleep-wake behavioral history. We hypothesized that a significant proportion of the variance in the performance response to 36 hours of sustained work (i.e., sleep loss) would be associated with prior sleep history. We compared performance during a 36-hour sleep deprivation period following a week of sleep satiation (12 hours time in bed per night) to performance during a 36-hour sleep deprivation period following a week of sleep restriction (6 hours time in bed per night). Numerous reports have shown that during space flight, sleep is reduced to 6 hours per day on average. By itself, this cumulative sleep loss may already lead to human error (see Figure 1). However, when it is followed by sustained mission demands with prolonged wakefulness, such performance failure may endanger both the mission and the crew. In order to effectively target countermeasures of performance failure, it is important to quantify the effect of sleep history on the vulnerability to performance deficits during extended work demands in space.
2. Quantify the extent to which the magnitude of performance failure during extended waking performance involves a trait response. We hypothesized that a significant proportion of the variance in the performance response to 36 hours of extended work involving sleep loss would be reproduced within subjects over two identical laboratory experimental sessions each preceded by a week of sleep satiation (i.e., 12 hours time in bed per night). If vulnerability to performance failure appears to be predominantly trait-dependent, then this may have consequences for astronaut selection for specific tasks in space (e.g., Extra-Vehicular Activities).
3. Quantify the effect of repeated exposure to extended waking performance demands on vulnerability to performance deficits. We hypothesized that repeated exposure to extended performance demands involving sleep loss would have a sensitizing effect, that is, performance deficits will become worse when sustained work with prolonged wakefulness is experienced repeatedly. Support for this hypothesis would indicate that adaptation to sustained mission demands cannot be practiced, and that gaining experience may not be a valid countermeasure, which may have consequences for astronaut training programs.
4. Identify predictors of vulnerability to performance failure during extended waking performance. This specific aim, which is exploratory and hypothesis generating, is addressed by entering a range of biological and behavioral variables as potential predictors into a regression equation of the individual performance deficits associated with sustained work with prolonged wakefulness (i.e., 36 hours of sleep loss with high workload in the laboratory).

APPROACH:
In this study, n=21 subjects served as their own controls through a design in which extensive neurobehavioral and physiological assessments were made during three replications of extended, 36-hour waking performance, as part of three 69-hour in-laboratory experimental sessions. The amount of sleep allowed during the week prior to each laboratory session was varied in counterbalanced order between a satiated condition (i.e., 12 hours time in bed) and a restricted condition (i.e., 6 hours time in bed). Research subjects also underwent two screening sessions and a 29-hour adaptation session in the laboratory before entering the experimental phase of the study, and a follow-up session to conclude the experiment. The study was designed with the following considerations:

1. Inter-individual differences in performance failure are exposed during extended work involving sleep loss, such as can occur under emergency circumstances in space; hence, a 36-hour sleep deprivation experiment was the paradigm of choice for this investigation.
2. Sleep history (satiated versus restricted) was carefully controlled in the week prior to the 36 hours of sleep deprivation in order to address specific aim 1. The 36-hour sleep deprivation was repeated in order to use subjects as their own controls in comparing performance failure following the satiated versus the restricted sleep conditions.
3. The 36-hour sleep deprivation following the satiated sleep condition was repeated in order to assess the stability (i.e., trait-like component) of the performance response to extended work involving sleep loss, as specified in specific aim 2, and independently of specific aim 1.
4. Counterbalancing the order of the two 36-hour sleep deprivation experiments following sleep satiation and the single 36-hour sleep deprivation experiment following the restricted sleep condition ensured that the sensitizing effect of repeated exposure to extended work involving sleep loss, as specified in specific aim 3, could be assessed independently of specific aims 1 and 2.
5. The 36 hours of sleep deprivation were conducted inside a controlled laboratory environment, so as to eliminate external sources of interference (masking) on neurobehavioral and physiological measures.

Subjects came to the laboratory for a 29-hour adaptation session. In this session, they got used to sleeping in the laboratory while being monitored polysomnographically (EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG), and they practiced the neurobehavioral performance battery. Subjects began the experiment at 17:00 hours, and at 20:00 hours they practiced the performance tests, which were fully explained to them by the research staff. At 22:00 hours they went to bed for a 12-hour baseline sleep period, which was recorded polysomnographically. At 10:00 hours the next morning, directly after getting up, subjects underwent performance testing. The performance testing then occurred every 2 hours until 20:00 hours. At 22:00 hours, subjects were sent home.

After 2 weeks, subjects returned to the laboratory for their first 67-hour session with 36 hours of extended work involving sleep deprivation. The first 29 hours of this session were identical to the adaptation session. However, after the baseline sleep period, wakefulness was maintained for 36 hours (i.e., total sleep deprivation) with 1-hour performance testing every 2 hours, to simulate circumstances of extended work involving sleep loss as can occur during emergencies in space. During this period, specially trained staff kept the subjects awake when necessary, inducing mild social activity such as casual conversation. Before each performance test bout, subjects were urged to perform to their best level, and compensatory effort was encouraged. After the 36 hours of maintained wakefulness, at 22:00 hours the next day, the subjects went to bed for a 12-hour recovery sleep opportunity that was recorded polysomnographically. Subjects got up at 10:00 the next morning and were sent home at around noon.

The 67-hour laboratory session was repeated after 2 weeks, and was held a third time after another 2 weeks. Each sleep deprivation session was preceded by one week of carefully controlled sleep opportunities at home. In the two conditions of satiated prior sleep, subjects stayed in bed from 22:00 hours until 10:00 hours each day (i.e., a 12-hour sleep opportunity). However, in the condition of restricted prior sleep, the subjects’ sleep opportunities during the week preceding the laboratory session, were restricted to the period from 04:00 hours until 10:00 hours (i.e., a 6-hour sleep opportunity). This restriction was also applied to the baseline sleep in the laboratory. The 6-hour sleep restriction preceded only one of the three 67-hour laboratory sessions in a randomized, counterbalanced order. Thus, 7 of the 21 subjects encountered the prior sleep restriction before their first sleep deprivation session; for 7 subjects the second session was preceded by restricted sleep, and for the remaining 7 subjects the last session was preceded by restricted sleep. The 6-hour sleep restriction simulated the sleep duration commonly experienced by astronauts during space-flight missions.

Compliance with times in bed at home (prior to the laboratory sessions) was verified by means of a sleep-wake log and wrist actigraphy. Furthermore, subjects called a time-stamped telephone recorder each day to report their bedtimes. The conditions in the laboratory were strictly controlled in terms of ambient light (less than 50 lux) and temperature (21?C), social interactions, and performance test schedules. Meals were given at 4-hour intervals (clock times 03:00, 07:00, 11:00, 15:00, 19:00, 23:00) during wakefulness. Food was controlled in terms of calories and nutritients (proteins, fats, carbohydrates), and the amount of food subjects received during the 36 hours of extended wakefulness matched their normal 2-day calorie requirement. No caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine intake were allowed.

All in-laboratory research was performed in the General Clinical Research Center (GCRC) of the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. The GCRC has light, sound, and temperature controlled rooms within which the laboratory portions of the study were conducted. The GCRC research nursing staff provided supervision of the subjects during sleep periods, and the nurses assisted in critical portions of subject screening. In addition, subjects were provided with meals controlled for nutrients and calories through the GCRC metabolic kitchen. GCRC support of the study was at no cost to the project.

State-of-the-art equipment was used for recording of polysomnography (EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG), actigraphy, core body temperature, and light exposure. In addition, a variety of neurobehavioral performance assays was administered every 2 hours of wakefulness in the laboratory, through a computerized system that was invented by the principal investigator for this specific purpose. Data reduction and analysis took place during and after each experimental session, so that quality control and tentative data analysis were possible immediately after completion of each subject’s experimental procedures.

In year 2 of the project, the study was enhanced when storage of a small sample (up to 10ml) of research subjects’ whole blood for future genetic analysis was approved by the IRBs of the University of Pennsylvania and Johnson Space Center. If in the future subjects consent to the use of their stored blood for genetic analysis, this may allow for the linking of predictors of vulnerability for the effects of extended wakefulness to genetic background information. Thus, the potential to gain scientific knowledge from the project may be enhanced significantly even after the expiration of the funding period.

RESULTS:
In summary, the results of the analyses for specific aims 1–3 contradicted the prevailing hypothesis that inter-individual differences in the response to sleep deprivation could be explained by sleep history and/or stochastic fluctuations alone. Sleep history and error variance contribute only marginally to the inter-individual differences in vulnerability to performance impairment during extended wakefulness, while the dominant determinant was stable, trait-like variability among individuals. Furthermore, it was found that repeated exposure to total sleep deprivation causes sensitization to the effects of sleep loss on sustained attention performance.

Specific aim 4, which will focus on the identification of predictors of the trait-like inter-individual differences in vulnerability to performance failure during extended wakefulness, cannot yet be addressed, because it requires the completion of all processing of a multitude of data acquired from the subjects during the study. They are not an essential outcome of the present study; rather, they will serve as preliminary data for a future study aiming to identify variables underlying the currently established trait-like inter-individual differences in vulnerability to neurobehavioral performance impairment from extended wakefulness.
