Biomechanical Evaluation of Locomotion on the Russian BD-1 Treadmill in a Weightless Environment (KC-135)

Record ID: ROI_BD1
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
A treadmill is used onboard the International Space Station (ISS) to allow locomotive exercise. During locomotion in microgravity, crewmembers wear a harness and tether in order to maintain contact with the treadmill. The tether provides an external load (EL) that pulls the crewmember back towards the treadmill, creating impact-loading between the treadmill and foot. The amount of EL is adjustable and can be determined by the crewmember. Due to possibility of breakdowns of the current treadmill in use on the ISS, and owing to the criticality of this mode of exercise, the Russian designed Begovaya Dorozhka (BD-1) treadmill can be used as a contingency device. The BD-1 treadmill is a non-motorized device and was the focus of this investigation.

The purpose of this investigation was to evaluate locomotion on the BD-1 non-motorized treadmill under three conditions: microgravity (0-G) without a positioning harness (PH), normal gravity (1-G) without a PH, and 1-G with a PH. Outcome measures evaluated included the applied external load (EL), vertical ground reaction force (GRF), the ability to achieve specific locomotion speeds, and joint kinematics. The goal of this research was to better understand how locomotion and the related forces experienced by the musculoskeletal system during non-motorized treadmill locomotion in microgravity compare to locomotion on the same device in 1-G. Four hypotheses were tested during this investigation:

  1. The ability to achieve specific locomotion speeds will not be affected by gravitational or PH condition.
  2. The dynamic EL during locomotion in 0-G will be affected by subject anthropometrics (body measurements/proportions).
  3. The peak vertical GRF produced will be different in 0-G compared to 1-G for either PH configuration at the same absolute speeds.
  4. Gait parameters and locomotion kinematics, including contact time, cadence, joint displacements and velocities, and forward lean will be different in 0-G compared to 1-G for either PH configuration at the same absolute speeds.

APPROACH:
Eight subjects (5 men, 3 women, 33.3 ± 177 6.0 years, 174.3 ± 8.6 cm; 75.1 ± 11.7 kg) ran at 5, 8, and 14 km/h during parabolic flight onboard the KC-135 aircraft and in 1-G. During parabolic flight 0-G trials, EL was applied using the internal BD-1 loading system and subjects walked or ran while gripping a stationary bar in front of their bodies with both hands. During 1-G trials, subjects completed each locomotion speed under two conditions: gripping a stationary bar and using a PH. Treadmill speed, external load (EL; 0-G only), gait timing parameters, vertical ground reaction forces (GRF), and kinematics data were collected. All statistical analyses were completed within each speed.

RESULTS:
1) Subjects were able to attain speeds of 5 km/h and 8 km/h in 0-G, but had difficulty attaining 14 km/h. This suggests that they may have difficulty obtaining sprinting speeds near and above 13 km/h in 0-G. It is unclear if the difficulty was due to individual subject characteristics, such as anaerobic fitness or leg strength, power, or endurance, or if it was due to the gravitational environment. Recommendation: Speeds greater than 13 km/h should be prescribed with the advance knowledge that they may be difficult to attain and/or maintain.

2) Mean total dynamic EL was approximately 520 N (116.9 lbs) and did not vary with locomotion speed. The EL for an individual was related to their heel to greater trochanter length (GTL) and the harness extender strap setting (HE). Recommendation: The approximate EL can be found as: EL (in kg) = 24.43 + 0.45 × GTL (in cm) - 0.38×HE (in cm).

3) Contact time and cadence did not differ between 0-G and in 1-G with or without the restraint strap (RS). Contact times were similar to those found during earlier motorized treadmill evaluations, but cadences appeared to be greater. Peak vertical GRF and impulse also did not differ within speeds between 0-G and in 1-G with or without the RS. Vertical GRF trajectories revealed the absence of an impact peak, suggesting that the foot has already begun to move rearward prior to heel strike. Peak GRF appeared to be greater at each speed as compared to similar speeds on a motorized treadmill reported elsewhere, but impulses were similar. Recommendation: Subjects will generate greater peak forces on the BD-1, probably due to the need to propel the treadmill. Exercise prescription should take increased use of the hip and knee musculature into account during BD-1 exercise.

4) Subjects appeared to perform gait with an increased forward lean, minimal hip extension, 50-90 degrees of knee flexion, and varying ranges of ankle flexion. Range of motion (ROM) at each joint was not affected by gravitational or RS condition, but there were differences in trends between subjects. Hip ROM remains similar but knee ROM appears to increase and ankle ROM appears to decrease as locomotion speed increases. Recommendation: When prescribing exercise onboard ISS, hip extensor range of motion should be addressed. In addition, when utilizing locomotor exercise at higher speeds, ankle flexor/extensor exercises should be used to supplement the possible reduced use of this musculature.

CONCLUSIONS
This study has shown that locomotion on the BD-1 treadmill is not markedly different in 0-G or 1-G. The use of a PH reduces the amount of forward lean, but does not affect joint excursion or vertical GRF measures. Because of the non-motorized nature of the treadmill, the subjects must provide considerably greater hip, knee and ankle extensor forces during the propulsive motion. The differences in joint excursions and required forces to run on the BD-1 may require an adjustment in the overall exercise prescription, including prescriptions for other exercise modalities, provided to crewmembers to accommodate for the long term physiological effects of training upon the device, which are not yet understood.
