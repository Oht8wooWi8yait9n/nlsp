Spatial Reorientation of Sensorimotor Balance Control in Altered Gravity

Record ID: DSO 635
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Sensorimotor deficits following space flight are more pronounced after landing when the head is actively tilted with respect to the trunk. This suggests that central vestibular processing shifts from a gravitational frame of reference to a head frame of reference in microgravity. A major effect of such changes is a significant postural instability. The purpose of this study was to examine adaptive changes in spatial processing for balance control following space flight by incorporating static and dynamic tilts that dissociate head and gravity reference frames.

The first specific aim was to examine adaptive changes in the spatial reference frame used for coding orientation and motion as a function of space flight. By examining the effects of head tilt on balance control following space flight, the investigators tested their hypothesis that there is a reorientation of central vestibular processing from a gravitational frame of reference to a head frame of reference as a function of adaptation to microgravity. The second specific aim was to examine the feasibility of altering the re-adaptation process following space flight by providing discordant canal-otolith-somatosensory stimuli using short-radius pitch centrifugation. This hypothesis was tested by comparing postural performance before and immediately after the centrifuge runs performed on R+3, the normal return-to-duty day.

APPROACH:
This study was conducted on eleven astronauts participating in their first space flight mission during one familiarization training session (including standard posturography testing), three pre-flight data sessions, and four post-flight data sessions on R+0, 2, 3 and 7 or 8 days. Each test session included posture measurements, and three sessions (L-60, L-30 and R+3) included additional posture measurements immediately after exposure to short-radius centrifugation.

In preparation for the flight study, postural performance was measured in 12 healthy adult humans during static and dynamic (0.14, 0.33, 0.6 Hz) head tilts of ±30° in the pitch and roll planes. These same twelve subjects also participated in a separate centrifuge control study during which the superimposed sinusoidal oscillation varied over this same frequency range. Eleven subjects were also oscillated at 0.14, 0.3, or 0.6 Hz without centrifugation with the interaural axis positioned either 0.5m off-axis and over the axis of rotation. Finally, a normative study obtained repeated postural measures in healthy age, gender, and height-matched control subjects using the same timeline scheduling as their astronaut counterparts.

RESULTS:
Postural stability on R+0 was substantially decreased for conditions with absent visual and altered proprioceptive feedback. On landing day, all 11 subjects fell on at least one sway-referenced trial with active head movements. Limited exposure to similar pitch centrifugation profiles on R+3; however, did not substantially disrupt postural performance in any of the subjects tested in this study. The decrease in postural performance during head tilts is consistent with the hypothesis that changes in the central vestibular processing of otolith input contributes to the disruption of balance control following microgravity transitions.

A better understanding of the nature of sensorimotor recovery from short-duration space flights will help in understanding the mechanisms of adaptation of balance control. The results of this research will be used by the NASA research and operational community to evaluate the feasibility of using short-arm centrifugation as an in-flight sensorimotor countermeasure. The results will also be of use to clinical and military researchers interested in adaptive changes in spatial processing to altered gravito-inertial environments, and the fragility of balance control adaptive processes. Findings of the ground-based study on static and dynamic head movements during computerized dynamic posturography may help to improve the sensitivity of the test paradigm in selected clinical populations.
