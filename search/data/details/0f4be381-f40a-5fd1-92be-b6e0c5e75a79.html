Predicting Sensorimotor Adaptation to Altered Gravity by Measuring Vestibular Perceptual Thresholds (Postdoctoral Fellowship)

Record ID: NCC958PF03502
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Astronauts must maintain appropriate sensorimotor function for tasks such as piloted landing or docking, as well as vehicle egress and extra-vehicular activity. However, the altered gravity environments regularly experienced during space exploration missions require sensorimotor adaptation. While nearly all astronauts eventually adapt there appears to be substantial individual differences in how quickly and effectively this adaptation occurs which are not well understood. It is thought that sensory conflict – unexpected sensory signals – when sustained, result in adaptation. However, even in an unchanged environment sensory signals are noisy and will lead to sensory conflict. The central nervous system must avoid inappropriately adapting to noisy sensory signals when the environment has not changed. Presumably if the conflict is consistent and sustained, the central nervous system can become more certain the conflict is due to a change in the environment and not created by sensory noise.

The investigators hypothesized that vestibular perceptual “noise” is a limiting factor in sensorimotor adaptation. If noise levels are high, a more sustained sensory conflict is required before adaptation is appropriate. The investigators have experience measuring perceptual “noise” through vestibular perceptual thresholds (i.e. how small of a stimulus can be correctly identified). Substantial inter-individual differences exist in vestibular perceptual thresholds which may help explain the poorly understood differences in altered gravity adaptation. Thus, the investigators aimed to experimentally test whether individuals with higher “noise” levels, as measured by perceptual thresholds, are indeed more challenged to adapt to an altered gravity environment. A hyper-gravity environment on the Eccentric Rotator centrifuge is utilized as an altered gravity test-bed to study adaptation. Future experiments may extend to study hypo- and microgravity adaptation environments.

APPROACH:
Operationally relevant tilt perception and manual control tasks served as metrics to track adaptation. The hypothesis was tested in a series of three experiments in which the adaptation stimulus was advanced from being highly controlled by the experimenter to more operationally relevant. Investigators aimed to deliver a prediction tool to identify individuals which may have difficulty adapting to altered gravity environments and may benefit from sensorimotor training. The tool required less than one hour of test time and involved benign vestibular perceptual threshold tests. The set of experiments will greatly enhance the understanding of the drivers and limiting factors for sensorimotor adaptation to altered gravity environments, and may inform the development of sensorimotor training countermeasures.

RESULTS:
In an altered gravity environment of 0.5 G centripetal acceleration created by a short-radius centrifuge (i.e. "hypo-gravity" like on the moon or Mars), subjects significantly underestimated their whole-body roll tilt, compared to fairly accurate tilt perception in 1 G of centripetal acceleration. Furthermore, with approximately 20 minutes of exposure with veridical visual feedback, the amount of underestimation significantly decayed (i.e. tilt perception became more accurate, suggesting an appropriate adaptive change). However, there were substantial inter-individual differences in 1) how much underestimation of roll tilt occurred upon initial exposure to 0.5 G, and 2) how much adaptation occurred over a fixed period of time in 0.5 G. Investigators also found, as has been previously observed, substantial inter-individual differences in the same group of subjects' vestibular perceptual thresholds (i.e. how small of a motion can reliable be perceived in the dark). As they had hypothesized, they found significant correlations across subjects between vestibular perceptual thresholds and how much perceptual adaptation occurred in 0.5 G. Specifically, individuals with lower thresholds for inter-aural translations 1 second in duration or lower 5 second long roll tilt thresholds had more perceptual adaptation in the altered gravity environment. Since these threshold measures are known to depend upon otolith sensory noise levels, their findings suggest lower otolith noise levels permit a higher adaptive capacity to altered gravity environments. The investigators conclude that vestibular perceptual thresholds may be a useful predictor for the substantial inter-individual differences anecdotally observed in sensorimotor adaptation to spaceflight-induced gravity transitions, warranting further investigation.
