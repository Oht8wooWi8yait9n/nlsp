Cognitive Aid Design Using Augmented Reality to Support Attention

Record ID: 80NSSC20K1715
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJCTIVES:
A cognitive aid is an accessory used to alleviate mental demand or to direct attention, decisions, and action towards the completion of a task. Aids improve performance by providing decision support, information, attentional guidance, and memory support to obviate human perceptual, cognitive, and communication limitations. Augmented reality (AR) cognitive aid research and applications tend to focus on the addition of visual elements to the environment, such as virtual objects, labels, highlights, or navigation arrows. Auditory augmentations are similarly additive, with sound or voice overlays triggered by interfaces, operator location, or other physical world events.

Another form of AR, diminished reality (DR), focuses on subtracting elements from the environment, both auditory and visual. This method of capturing and supporting attention works by de-emphasizing nuisance portions of the task or environment. Focusing operator attention via de-emphasis is crucial, as the human ability to ignore or inhibit responses to distracting stimuli decreases with fatigue, environmental exposure, and information overload - all scenarios likely in long-term spaceflight. With the advent of augmented reality, it is possible to de-emphasize visual and auditory elements of the task and environment. Though there are products that can de-emphasize ambient sound (e.g., “hearables”), there is no research on using de-emphasis to aid attentional focus. There is also no research on how de-emphasis should look or react to the intent of the operator, necessitating the design of new methods of human-computer interaction.

This study will investigate the design and benefit of AR in a cognitive aid for medical equipment setup via the following aims:
1. Design and create a visual and auditory de-emphasized AR user-centered prototype, and
2. Develop DR technology guidelines for future use in cognitive aids.

APPROACH:
Two experiments were conducted to understand the effects of universal and selective (context-aware) diminishment of distraction on performance, situational awareness, and subjective experience of workload in a novel assembly task. In both experiments, participants used procedural instructions to assemble a medical ventilator for use during a simulated emergency in virtual reality. In both experiments, the type of diminishment was manipulated within participants, so that each participant experienced universal attenuation of visual and auditory distraction, context-aware diminishment of the same, and a control condition with all distractions present. In the first experiment, participants experienced universal attenuation of distraction, via blurring of their peripheral vision and lowered volume for all audio. In the second experiment, attenuation was context-aware: only non-task objects were made less visible and only unimportant off-task audio was dampened.

RESULTS:
In Experiment 1, workload was perceived to be lower in the Universal DR condition compared to the control. In Experiment 2, JSC participants perceived lower workload in both DR conditions compared to control. In many studies, perceived workload changes when performance does not because participants are able to put forth the effort needed to perform even when the task is extremely difficult.. The JSC participants, with their longer work histories and experience in problem-solving for ambiguous tasks, were likely able to keep up their performance even in the control condition, though they were also sensitive to how much harder they had to work to do so.

Both graduate student and JSC participant groups were highly educated; however, the JSC participants also had work experience in science and technology. The differences in results between the experiments was the lack of performance differences by condition for the JSC participants, while those STEM graduate programs accomplished more steps in the Context-Aware condition compared to Control and had lower errors in the Universal DR condition compared to control. The same was true for situational awareness measures: diminishment harmed situational awareness for label locations and knowledge of the external concurrent emergency for the STEM graduate students, but not for those at JSC. One explanation might be the smaller sample size of JSC participants in Experiment 2, though the within-subject design meant that both experiments were adequately powered. However, even when ignoring significance, there were no trends toward performance differences in Experiment 2.
