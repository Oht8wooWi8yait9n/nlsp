A Multi-Media, Computer-Based, Self-Directed, Autonomous, Stress and Anxiety-Management Countermeasure

Record ID: NCC958NBPF01605
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Stress and anxiety-related problems before, during, and after space missions can seriously compromise crew efficiency and safety. The Institute of Medicine report of Earth-analog environments (e.g., Antarctica) suggests a prevalence rate of serious behavioral problems of up to 13% per person per year, highlighting the potential behavioral problems that could occur on a three-year space mission.
Specific Aims:
1. Develop a self-directed, autonomous, interactive multimedia program to train astronauts how to recognize, assess, detect, prevent, and manage stress on extended space flights. The countermeasure would be principally used in pre-flight training, with booster sessions or interventions available on orbit as needed. In this model of implementation, the skills learned prior to a mission would help to "inoculate" fliers to severe stress reactions, thereby enhancing flight-task performance and crew health, safety and efficiency, and build resilience. The countermeasure, called "Stress Management and Resilience Training for Optimal Performance" or SMART-OP, will incorporate empirically supported stress anxiety management strategies delivered by interactive media.
2. Evaluate the efficacy of the program with an astronaut-relevant sample in a randomized controlled trial. The final product will be a computer-based stress management and resilience training program that could be used both by flight crews and ground-personnel to prepare for stress during training, while on mission, or post-mission.

APPROACH:
Potential participants had to be free of any significant medical conditions, and endorse a certain level of stress based on their score on a widely used self-report questionnaire (Perceived Stress Scale). Investigators selected a cutoff score of 0.5 SD above the mean score for a community norm on the PSS. The research team screened 164 individuals and 85 were deemed ineligible. Subjects were randomized to one of the two study groups; the SMART-OP group who received computerized stress management (CSM) or to an Attention Control (AC) who watched videos and read information on stress management. SMART-OP contained six 30 to 45-minute sessions that taught stress management skills in the areas of education, emotion/physiological regulation, cognitive flexibility, and behavioral actions to cope with stressful situations. The skills were taught in an interactive and engaging manner.

RESULTS:
To date, investigators have completed 30 post-assessments. A sub-sample (N=14) of SMART-OP user ratings of usefulness (1-7 scale with higher scores indicating greater usefulness) of program activities shows that they rated them very positively (M=5.37, SD= 1.2) and higher, on average, than the control group rated their activities (M=4.25, SD=1.56). While this is only a small sub-sample, it provides some initial positive feedback on the usefulness of the countermeasure.
