Summary / Description

The Expedition Nine crew began their mission with the launch of the Russian Soyuz TMA-4 spacecraft on April 18, 2004 from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan. Expedition Commander Gennady Padalka and Flight Engineer Mike Fincke were joined aboard the Soyuz by European Space Agency (ESA) cosmonaut Andre Kuipers of the Netherlands. Kuipers spent nine days aboard the International Space Station (ISS) performing scientific experiments under a commercial contract between ESA and the Federal Space Agency (of Russia). Kuipers returned to Earth on April 29, 2004, with the Expedition Eight crew.

During the six months in orbit, Padalka and Fincke monitored the arrival of two Russian Progress resupply cargo ships filled with food, fuel, water, and supplies. They also upgraded the software in the station computers. Also on the crew's agenda was work with the station's robotic arm, Canadarm2. Robotics work focused on observations of the station's exterior, maintaining operator proficiency and completing the schedule of on-orbit checkout requirements that were developed to fully characterize the performance of the robotic system.

Two spacewalks were planned during Expedition Nine by Padalka and Fincke. The spacewalks were designed to continue the external outfitting of the Zvezda Service Module and to install cameras, communications gear, and navigational aids to Zvezda for the arrival of the European Space Agency's unpiloted Automated Transfer Vehicle (ATV).

Station operations and maintenance took up a considerable share of the time for the two-person crew. But science continued, as did science-focused education activities and Earth observations. Experiments make use of the microgravity environment in the Destiny Laboratory and the orientation of the station to conduct investigations in a variety of disciplines. Those fields include life sciences, physics and chemistry, and their applications in materials and manufacturing processes. The station is also used to study the Earth - its environment, climate, geology, oceanography, and more. Indeed, Earth observations were expected to occupy a relatively large share of this crew's time for scientific activity. U.S., Russian, and Partner experiments and hardware on board the ISS used nearly 300 hours of crew time, which was prioritized and scheduled as time permitted surrounding the spacewalks, Progress dockings, medical operations, and system maintenance activities. The science team at the Payload Operations Center at the Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, AL, operated some experiments without crew input, and other experiments were designed to function autonomously. In addition, some Expedition Eight science activities were continued. Many of the Expedition Nine Russian science experiments were delivered on the ISS Progress 13 resupply vehicle.

Research continued during Expedition Nine in the fields of life sciences, space product development, physical sciences, and space flight utilization. Much of the research was carried out with scientific research facilities and samples already on board the ISS.

Life sciences experiments continuing into Expedition Nine from earlier missions were:

  • Advanced Diagnostic Ultrasound in Microgravity (ADUM) involves crewmembers conducting on-orbit ultrasound exams on one another to determine the accuracy of using ultrasound to diagnose certain types of on-orbit injuries and to assess whether ultrasound is a feasible option for monitoring inflight bone alterations.
  • Behavioral Issues Associated with Isolation and Confinement: Review and Analysis of Astronaut Journals uses journals kept by the crew and surveys to study the effect of isolation to obtain quantitative data on the importance of different behavioral issues in long-duration crews. Results will help design equipment and procedures to allow astronauts to best cope with isolation and long duration spaceflight.
  • Chromosomal Aberrations in Blood Lymphocytes of Astronauts (Chromosome) will study space radiation on humans. The expected results will provide a better knowledge of the genetic risk of astronauts in space and can help to optimize radiation shielding.
  • Interactions to identify and characterize interpersonal and cultural factors that may affect crew and ground support personnel performance during space station missions.
  • Effect of Prolonged Spaceflight on Human Skeletal Muscle (Biopsy), pre- and postflight tests on crewmembers that will help to determine the progression and extent of functional and structural change in limb skeletal muscle during prolonged space flight.
  • Mobility, a pre- and postflight investigation designed to study the changes in posture and gait after long-duration space flight.

The Expedition Nine crew returned to Earth on October 23, 2004, aboard the Soyuz TMA-4 spacecraft.
