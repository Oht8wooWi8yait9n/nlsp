Cancer Induction by Repeated Doses of High-LET Radiation

Record ID: NAG91024
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Past research has shown that radiation is a strong carcinogen. Vitamin A, a retinoid, can reduce the cancers caused by radiation in rat skin. This ground-based study focused on identifying genes whose expression is either induced or blocked by radiation and on the counteraction of retinyl acetate on those changes in gene expression.

Although retinoids, which are chemical derivatives of vitamin A, have been shown to counteract carcinogenic effects in several animal models, the molecular mechanisms behind the retinoid inhibition of radiation-induced cancer are not established. To better understand the mechanisms, this project focused on how a non-toxic naturally occurring retinoid in the diet called retinyl acetate, or vitamin A acetate, alters gene expression upon exposure of rat skin to radiation. This study may apply to the identification of genes involved in the broad mechanism of the retinoid role in skin cancer, such as its influence on cell proliferation, cell differentiation and apoptosis, which is also known as programmed cell death. Although these and other cellular responses to ionizing radiation are protective, if the responses are insufficient or overwhelmed, genetic errors may accumulate in cells. Those cells then divide, and cancer could eventually occur.

Preliminary findings with low Linear Energy Transfer (LET) radiation showed that dietary retinyl acetate counteracts radiation-induced expression in hundreds of genes. The most prominently affected genes were markers of cell proliferation in keratinocytes suggesting that the retinoid prevented radiation-induced increases of cell proliferation genes. No information is available on the effect of high LET radiation such as ƒ56/ƒFe on gene expression in skin keratinocytes. It is of interest to obtain information on gene expression patterns following skin exposure to the ƒ56/ƒFe beam in rats on normal diet and diet with retinyl acetate supplement. Similarities and differences between high and low LET radiation could then be analyzed. Earlier results suggested that the vitamin A retinoid is almost as effective against induction of skin tumors induced by ƒ56/ƒFe as by those induced by low LET electron radiation, thus gene expression studies are of interest.

One approach to study genes of interest is to find the genes that are altered by radiation and vitamin A in opposite ways. The expression of such genes may change in response to radiation exposure. These same genes may be expressed in the opposite manner upon vitamin A exposure without the radiation. In addition, some genes may not be affected by the vitamin A treatment without the radiation, but, if radiation is given and it causes changes, the vitamin A may be able to reverse those changes even though an effect of vitamin A alone is not observed. Organization of the genes into groups with similar functions in the cell can enable researchers to glean insight on whether certain functions of the cell are affected by radiation and, possibly, the counteraction of vitamin A.

APPROACH:
Eight rats that were 23 days old were divided into two dietary groups of four rats each. One dietary group was fed the regular food while the second group received the same food mixed with 250 ppm vitamin A acetate supplement. After one week, all of the rats received nine Gray and eighteen Gray of low LET electron radiation on two different dorsal skin patches about the size of 1.5 cm by 2 cm. A separate dorsal patch that was not exposed to the radiation was analyzed as a control. Each rat then had three patches of skin that were evaluated. One patch received zero radiation, one received nine Gray and the other received eighteen Gray. Twenty-four hours or seven days after the irradiation, the rats were euthanized, and the irradiated skin and non-irradiated skin sections were excised and pooled according to the radiation dose and vitamin A condition.

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from the skin samples and reverse transcribed to synthesize a special type of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). This particular DNA, called cDNA, was comprised only of the genes that were expressed at the time of analysis. The reverse transcription utilized a primer, a short segment of nucleic acid, which hybridized adjacent to the RNA polymerase promoter region. Double stranded cDNAs were then transcribed into a cRNA probe that was labeled with biotin through in vitro transcription as suggested by the gene chip manufacturer.

The cRNAs were differentially displayed using a rat genome gene chip. Each chip contained 8740 specific probes for rat genes. They represented every known functional category of genes. With this chip, expression all 8740 genes and gene groups were examined in one hybridization. The function of the chip was to recognize the genes that were expressed under the radiation and retinyl acid conditions. The gene chip identified by name those genes that were expressed and recognized. Northern blotting and reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmed the genes that were differentially displayed, those that were identified when the probe bound to the specific genes on the rat genome gene chip. Finally, the patterns of the gene expression alterations from the radiation and vitamin A were categorized. Affected genes were grouped by their cellular functions to focus on pathways that are involved in the process of carcinogenesis by radiation and its reversal by retinyl acetate.

The gene chip analysis was not used for the eighteen Gray plus vitamin A treatment. Thus, five skin samples were analyzed for gene expression under low LET radiation. The five samples were from rats with normal diet and zero Gray radiation, normal diet with nine Gray radiation, normal diet with eighteen Gray radiation, vitamin A diet with zero Gray radiation and vitamin A diet with nine Gray radiation exposure.

For a study of carcinogenesis, the skin of approximately 200 rats was exposed to high LET radiation, a ƒ56/ƒFe beam. The rats were observed weekly for four weeks, then at six-week intervals thereafter. All skin abnormalities were noted, photographed and identified for histological diagnosis. The effects of multiple doses of high-LET ƒ56/ƒFe radiation in comparison to single doses of argon ion radiation were examined.

A different experiment utilizing the high LET radiation was to obtain information on gene expression patterns in a manner similar to that of the low LET electron radiation experiment. Rats were grouped into groups of three, with only twelve rats irradiated. One group of rats, the control group, received no treatment. Another group received vitamin A dietary retinoid and no radiation. Of the twelve irradiated rats, one group received vitamin A and three Gray of ƒ56/ƒFe high LET radiation. Another group received vitamin A plus 1.5 Gy ƒ56/ƒFe. Two groups of irradiated rats had no vitamin A supplement; one of these received three Gray of ƒ56/ƒFe and the other 1.5 Gy. At 24 hours or seven days after radiation exposure, the rats were euthanized, and the irradiated tissue was excised and frozen in liquid nitrogen for future analysis of RNA expression patterns with the gene chip technology.

RESULTS:
Retinol concentration in the skin was measured in rats with both diets. By seven days after the retinyl acid diet was begun, the retinol had accumulated in the skin but not in the blood of the rats that were given the supplement. The skin concentration of retinol in rats on that diet was 4.5-fold higher than in the rats that did not consume the retinyl acid diet. Plasma retinol levels were steady with or without the retinyl acid diet.

Gene expression signals ranged from zero to 278,710, with an average of 4217. Signal strength above 1000 was considered significant. For the five experimental groups, normal diet at zero, nine and eighteen Gray and vitamin A diet at zero and nine Gray, 50.4% of the genes were considered positive in at least one of the groups and the remaining 49.6% were considered as not significantly expressed in any of the five gene chips. Thus, 4402 of the 8740 genes were altered in expression for at least one of the five conditions, and 4338 of the genes did not show an alteration in expression in any of the five conditions.

Genes with induction or inhibition were compared among experimental conditions. Normal diet with nine Gray radiation versus normal diet with no radiation showed 188 altered genes. Eighteen Gray radiation versus normal diet with no radiation gave 92 altered genes, with 54 of those genes also altered under the nine Gray of radiation versus zero Gray, showing a radiation response dependent on dose. Eighteen Gray versus nine Gray of radiation with a normal diet showed 77 altered genes. For vitamin A supplemented diet, the vitamin A with no radiation compared to normal diet with no radiation showed 231 altered genes. Nine Gray of radiation on normal diet versus nine Gray of radiation with the vitamin A diet showed 58 altered genes.

There were 56 genes that showed at least 2.5 fold change between nine Gray and nine Gray with the vitamin A diet gene chips, and also had consistency in the other chips. In one group, genes were repressed by vitamin A alone and were induced by radiation, and the radiation induction was reduced by the vitamin A retinoid. In a different group, vitamin A alone did not change the expression in comparison to control but the combination of vitamin A and radiation reverted the radiation back to control levels. Other genes were inhibited by radiation, but were reversed to control level by vitamin A. The genes that showed the greatest reversal of radiation effects by vitamin A were a keratin gene, ornithine decarboxylase, an intracellular calcium-binding protein and a gene that is similar to an intracellular calcium-binding protein.

Genes that share similar functions were grouped together to learn if any groups were more susceptible to the radiation or the mitigating effects of vitamin A. The genes were checked to see if a significant change of greater than two-fold in expression levels were observed. Several functional groups did not show any genes with significant changes after radiation with or without vitamin A treatments. Tumor suppressor genes and genes related to apoptosis were two of the functional gene groups of interest that showed no changes in expression. The gene chips contained 16 cytokines and only one, Cyclin G, showed a greater than two-fold change between nine Gray with normal diet and nine Gray with vitamin A supplement.

The top one hundred genes with the greatest alteration in expression between nine Gray versus zero Gray normal diet and between vitamin A with no radiation versus zero Gray normal diet were grouped into one category. As a control, one hundred random genes from the expressed control sample were assigned into different functional categories. The genes affected most by the radiation or the vitamin A belonged to the categories of differentiation, stress response, inflammation or immune related and nucleic acid metabolism.

Northern blots, which analyzed RNA, showed a similar trend of expression as the gene chip for the keratin gene, a putative marker of proliferating basal cells. Although the Northern blot supports the gene chip results, the expression ratios differed between the two techniques for expression analysis. On the gene chip, the keratin gene showed 11.7-fold increase in expression with radiation versus the zero radiation. The vitamin A treatment (vitamin A with radiation) reversed the expression back to the control level. On the Northern blot, the increase in the radiation over control was only 4.9-fold rather than 11.7-fold, and the vitamin A reduced the expression to 2.2-fold above the control level rather than control level. The increase in gene expression from radiation that was reversible by vitamin A suggested that the vitamin A may have inhibited the dedifferentiating effects of radiation, and restored the normal differentiation status of the rat skin. Another laboratory technique, PCR, was also used to confirm gene chip results, and the gene chip expression pattern of the involucrin gene was confirmed by RT-PCR.

For the carcinogenesis study in which rat skin was exposed to radiation and the rats observed for tumors, a strong tendency was observed for the tumors to be inhibited by the presence of 250 ppm retinyl acetate in the food. Multiple doses of high LET ƒ56/ƒFe ion radiation in comparison to single doses of argon ion radiation were examined and indications were that multiple exposures produced more carcinomas than expected from the summation of single dose responses. Cancer induced by high LET radiation was indicated to be possibly as preventable by retinyl acetate in the diet as low LET radiation was in previous experiments.

Most tumors found in rats that had been irradiated with the ƒ56/ƒFe beam appeared to be fibromas. Single doses of ƒ56/ƒFe ions were somewhat more effective than argon in producing tumors at 4.5 Gy but not at three Gray or nine Gray. The 250 ppm of dietary retinyl acetate reduced the incidence of tumors to approximately 30% of the incidence found in groups that did not have the supplement in the diet.

Beneficial effects of vitamin A have been observed upon exposure to both high LET radiation and low LET electron radiation. Vitamin A reduced tumor incidence in rats exposed to high LET radiation. Under low LET radiation exposure, vitamin A blocked the expression of genes associated with increased proliferation and inflammation, and accelerated the expression of genes associated with epidermal differentiation. Some fibroblast or macrophage migration or invasion responses may have been inhibited as well. Vitamin A promoted epidermal differentiation on top of the abnormal differentiation from the radiation. Thus, it possibly protected the skin from cancer by preventing damaged cells from producing more cells and by promoting their death. Inhibition of epidermal cell proliferation and stimulation of differentiation limited the number of irradiated cells that could pass the genetic damage to future cells.
