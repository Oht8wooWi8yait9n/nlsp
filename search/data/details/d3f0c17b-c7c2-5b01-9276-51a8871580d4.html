Spaceflight Effects on Structural Stability and Genetic Information Transfer in Plasmid pIJ702 of Actinomycetes Streptomyces lividans 66

Record ID: Plasmid-F3
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The Foton M3 mission provided an opportunity to repeat and enhance the experiment flown on Foton M2 and expand knowledge of how living systems respond to the unique environment of space, i.e. microgravity and space radiation. These experiments have provided information on both chromosomal and plasmid gene expression as well as genetic stability. Streptomyces spp. may serve as a sensitive indicator of the effects of spaceflight on microorganisms due to their inherent genetic instability: the effects of longduration spaceflight missions may be inferred from the fundamental knowledge obtained during relatively short-term spaceflight exposure of Streptomyces specimens during the Foton M-3 flight. Detection of genetic changes in Streptomyces exposed to spaceflight factors, e.g. radiation and microgravity, would suggest that similar effects may occur in other microorganisms, especially with prolonged exposure to the same factors in long duration spaceflight. Genetic alterations (mutations) may result in enhanced virulence of pathogenic bacteria. They may also lead to changes in treatment approaches for infections, e.g. the microbes may become more or less susceptible to some antibiotics.

APPROACH:
The approach is integrated in the Results section below.

RESULTS:
For the Foton experiments, an increase in the proportion of Streptomyces lividans strain 66 clones that retained and expessed plasmid pIJ702 genes for thiostrepton resistance and melanin production in spaceflight cultures suggested that there was selection of melanin-producing clones. This may be related to the protective effects of melanin in response to spaceflight stress factors such as radiation and microgravity. With Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus pumilis, Escherichia coli, and Lactobacillus casei DNA was isolated from flight and ground control cultures, analyzed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and the putative mutations identified by denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE). These analyses detected revealed putative mutants in the E. coli flight and control cultures compared with stock of the same strain, but there were no differences between flight and ground samples in three amplicons. While PCR products from different species amplified by the same primer pair migrated similarly on an agarose gel, they migrate at different rates in DGGE because of their differing guanine/cytosine ratios, but no evidence of mutations was detected between any of the flight and ground culture pairs examined. Arginine and proline prototrophs from flight and ground cultures of S. coelicolor plated on selective media yielded a variety of pigmented isolates. PCR amplification of DNA from the two parental strains SC-236 and SC-3110 demonstrated the detection of Arginine, phenylalanine and proline gene sequences. Amplification of genes for arginine, phenylalanine and proline synthesis was demonstrated in the parent strains, but difficulties with amplifying these genes in revertants prevented further analysis.

Based on the Foton data, there is no evidence of spaceflight-related genetic changes in the bacteria studied. This may be attributed to the relatively low radiation dose received by the microorganisms during the Foton flights. Mutation rates, indicated by plasmid instability, for S. lividans flight and ground samples were similar and reflect the inherent genetic variability of this species.
