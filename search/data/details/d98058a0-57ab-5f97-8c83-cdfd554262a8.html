In-flight Sleep Analysis

Record ID: M008
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Early in the space program, attention was directed to the possibility of monitoring brain function during space flight. The in flight sleep analysis experiment conducted on Gemini 7 was the first attempt to record electroencephalograms (EEG) during orbital flight. The primary purpose of the experiment was to obtain objective and precise information concerning the number, duration, and depth of sleep periods in flight on one test subject. The EEG would measure any deviations from the normal sleep pattern and pinpoint any factors that may interfere with sleep in an orbiting spacecraft. This was important since several astronauts on previous flights had reported difficulty sleeping. In addition, the experiment was also to determine the effects, if any, of weightlessness on the electrical activity of the brain.

APPROACH:
Multi-channel EEG data were recorded preflight on subjects A and B of Gemini 7 during all stages of sleep and during the waking state. These recordings were used as baseline for comparison with simulation recordings and the in flight records. During flight, EEG measurements were collected only from subject A.

Prior to the day of launch, the helmet liner of test subject A was perforated at four electrode sites. The helmet liner was fitted to the subject's head, and perforations in the liner were used to mark the electrode sites. This procedure assured an exact correspondence between the helmet liner perforations and the electrodes attached to the scalp. Four electrodes, filled with electrode paste, were attached to the scalp sites using adhesive. The impedance of each electrode was tested with reference to the other three electrodes using an impedance tester. All electrodes were found to have an impedance of less than 1000 ohms.

Preliminary studies of 200 control subjects showed that four stages of sleep (i.e., stage 1 sleep, stage 2 sleep, stage 3 sleep and stage 4 sleep) could be differentiated and identified from a single pair of electrodes placed on the scalp. Placing the electrodes along the midline of the head was found to attain the fewest possible artifacts from muscle activity. Thus, researchers chose the midline-center and midline-occipital sites as the primary sites for EEG channel 1 electrode placement. Weight and space limitations permitted only one more EEG recording channel. A duplicate of the first electrode pair was used (channel 2) but displaced a few centimeters to the left of the midline. Such electrode placements would reveal essentially the same information as the midline pair. This choice was made (rather than obtaining data from another area) to provide for the possibility that one or more of the electrodes of one pair might be dislodged or become defective.

The recording system consisted of two miniature-transistorized amplifiers, carried by the subject in the pockets of his underwear, and a small biomedical tape recorder inside the spacecraft. The tape recorder, running at slow speed, was capable of recording 100 hours of data continuously.

EEG recording was to be continuous throughout the first 4 days of the Gemini 7 flight. During these 4 days, subject A was to keep his helmet on unless significant discomfort or other factors necessitated its removal.

RESULTS:
The electrodes dislodged from the subject A's scalp before 100 hours had elapsed, but 54 hours and 43 minutes of interpretable EEG data were obtained. Most of the data were of excellent quality from the standpoint of visual interpretation. The astronaut was alert and active during the major part (77.5%) of the recording. Temporary interruptions in the interpretability of the EEG data were produced by periods of rhythmic chewing motions during meals and while chewing gum.

Two periods of sleep occurred during the recording. The EEG patterns during sleep were distinctive and, since the astronaut was quiet, no artifact obscured the recording. Each minute of sleep was classified according to the four sleep stages, and was plotted graphically against time. An attempt was made to correlate the transient arousals from sleep with various environmental factors, such as suit and cabin temperature variations, cabin pressure variations, and the night-day light shifts inside the spacecraft. In spite of the astronauts' report that they were able to exclude sunlight from the spacecraft completely, the shadow history was the only fact showing a suggestive relationship with arousal. Of the total one-minute epochs in the two sleep periods, 26.4% contained some arousal-type EEG activity (eyes-open and resting). Of the sleep minutes recorded while the spacecraft was in sunlight, 35.2% showed some arousal, whereas only 9.3% minutes showed arousal while the spacecraft was in the Earth's shadow.

During the first in flight sleep period, subject A was awakened after 2 hours 45 minutes, but the first part of the characteristic cyclic alterations in sleep levels could be seen. The sleep period contained significant fluctuations between light sleep and arousal, with occasional brief episodes of stage 3 for the first 80 minutes. At that time, stage 4 sleep was reached. However, in less than 15 minutes abrupt arousal occurred, and sleep was terminated.

On the second day (33 hours and 10 minutes after liftoff), the subject again closed his eyes and immediately showed evidence of drowsiness. Within 34 minutes he was in the deepest level of sleep (stage 4). During this long period of sleep, there were cyclic alterations in sleep level similar to those that occurred during a full night of sleep under normal conditions. Generally, after the first period of stage 4 sleep has been obtained, each successive swing toward deeper sleep only reaches successively lighter levels of sleep. However, during the second night of sleep, the subject A attained and maintained stage 4 sleep for 20 minutes or more at three different times after the first episode. This increase in the number of stage 4 periods was speculated to be a manifestation of sleep deprivation during the first 24 hours of flight.

After approximately 7 hours of sleep, a partial arousal from stage 4 occurred. After a brief period (12 minutes) of fluctuation between sleep stages 2 and 3, the subject remained in a condition fluctuating between drowsiness and stage 1 sleep until a condition of full arousal was reached approximately 1.5 hours later. Periods of rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep could not be determined with certainty from the data because of the absence of electro-oculogram (EOG) measurements and because REM sleep is very similar in character to stage 1 sleep. However, for relatively long periods during sleep that occurred on the second day, two patterns were recorded that resembled a mixture of certain characteristics of stage 1 and stage 2 sleep. The patterns resembled some of the activity observed in REM sleep.

The experiment clearly demonstrated the feasibility of recording EEG during space flight. Although the technique employed and the experiment hardware were both elementary, it was possible to obtain precise information regarding the duration, depth, and number of sleep periods in flight. Researchers hoped that the potential of EEG as an analytical tool to study brain function would be continued on future flights.
