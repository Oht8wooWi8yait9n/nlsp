Integration of Neural Cardiovascular Control in Space

Record ID: 9301294
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The Integration of Neural Cardiovascular Control in Space experiment was one of four autonomic nervous system experiments performed during the 1998 Neurolab mission. This experiment focused on how the autonomic nervous system and circulatory system are integrated. During previous missions, alterations in cardiovascular control have been noted inflight. With the key role that this system plays in survival, it is important that it be better understood for the safety and health of the astronauts. One of the goals of this experiment is to do just that - contribute countermeasures for improved crew health in space.

Through the study of cardiopulmonary receptors, information will be collected on the mechanisms mediating cardiovascular adaption to microgravity and quantitative aspects on general human cardiovascular regulatory mechanisms. Medically, this experiment will provide useful reference material for both the evaluation of patients with autonomic dysfunction, and for future life sciences research in cardiovascular and regulatory physiology.

Together, this comprehensive set of measurements should help solve the puzzle of why astronauts have problems with blood pressure control after space flight. The answer is important not only for helping astronauts to re-enter gravity safely after being in space, but also for helping patients who suffer with diseases of blood pressure control systems.

APPROACH:
Throughout the autonomic team's experiment sequence, cardiovascular parameters were measured, including blood pressure and heart rate. An estimation of blood flow to the brain was also estimated via the non-invasive transcranial Doppler (TCD) technique. The TCD emits high-frequency sound waves used to show how blood flow to the brain is regulated. This assessment is important because not all people who have orthostatic intolerance after space flight experience a drop in blood pressure. Instead, an astronaut may have a problem with brain blood flow control which Doppler measurements can reveal.

While these measurements were taken, tests were conducted to activate the body's blood pressure control system and raise blood pressure. A standard test was to place a subject’s hand in ice water to elicit a response, but in space the astronaut’s hand was placed in an insulated glove at about -12.5 degrees Celsius. Called the cold pressor glove, it is used in conjunction with cuff blood pressure during its two minute use and recovery period. Another test had the subject squeeze a handgrip to 40% of his/her maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) force. As soon as the handgrip force dropped below 32% of MVC (i.e., 80% of target force) for more than a second, the cuff was inflated to a suprasystolic pressure to occlude circulation and the subject was prompted to release the handgrip. Occlusion continued for two minutes, and then the cuff was deflated during a two-minute recovery period.

RESULTS:
All three nominally scheduled sessions were performed, one session early (Flight Day 2), the second session mid (Flight Days 7 and 8), and the third session late (Flight Days 12 and 13) in flight. The protocol was repeated for two subjects on Flight Day 10, due to a signal loss that occurred in the mid-mission session. Additional sessions were performed on Flight Days 2 and 8 by one subject on the early mission session.

In this study, the hypothesis that space flight impairs cerebral autoregulation was studied in six astronauts approximately 72 and 23 days before, after 1 and 2 weeks in space (n = 4), on landing day, and 1 day after the 16-day Neurolab space shuttle mission. Beat-by-beat changes of photoplethysmographic mean arterial pressure and transcranial Doppler middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity were measured during 5 minutes of spontaneous breathing, 30 mmHg lower body suction to simulate standing in space, and 10 minutes of 60 degree passive upright tilt on Earth. Dynamic cerebral autoregulation was quantified by analysis of the transfer function between spontaneous changes of mean arterial pressure and cerebral artery blood flow velocity, in the very low- (0.02-0.07 Hz), low- (0.07-0.20 Hz) and high-frequency (0.20-0.35 Hz) ranges. Resting middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity did not change significantly from preflight values during or after space flight. Reductions of cerebral blood flow velocity during lower body suction were significant before space flight (P < 0.05, repeated measures ANOVA), but not during or after space flight. Absolute and percentage reductions of mean (+/- s.e.m.) cerebral blood flow velocity after 10 minutes upright tilt were smaller after than before space flight (absolute, -4 +/- 3 cm per sec after versus -14 +/- 3 cm per sec before, P = 0.001; and percentage, -8.0 +/- 4.8% after versus -24.8 +/- 4.4% before, P < 0.05), consistent with improved rather than impaired cerebral blood flow regulation. Low-frequency gain decreased significantly (P < 0.05) by 26, 23 and 27% after 1 and 2 weeks in space and on landing day, respectively, compared with preflight values, which is also consistent with improved autoregulation. It can be concluded that human cerebral autoregulation is preserved, and possibly even improved, by short-duration space flight.
