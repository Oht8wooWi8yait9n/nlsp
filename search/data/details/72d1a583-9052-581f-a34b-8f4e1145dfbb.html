Microbial Interaction in the Mir Space Station Environment

Record ID: 9401703
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Transfer of infectious agents between crewmembers became problematic early in both the Soviet and U.S. space programs. During Apollo missions 7, 8 and 9, microbial associated illnesses were frequently observed. In later missions (Apollo 10, 11 and 12), although no microbe related illnesses were reported, changes in microbial flora of crewmembers were seen in postflight samples. As a result, preflight quarantine procedures were implemented beginning with the Apollo 14 mission. This approach was effective, and no microbial associated problems were reported with subsequent flights. Nevertheless, microbes with pathogenic potential (e.g. Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Candida albicans) were isolated from astronauts in these later Apollo missions.

With the advent of the U.S. Space Shuttle program, since preflight quarantine practices continue and flights are of shorter duration, less emphasis is placed on microbial associated problems. These problems are likely to become more severe in the longer, more complex missions contemplated for the future. The long-term goal of this project was to understand the nature of microbial behavior in closed, long-duration space flight systems.

The main objectives were:
1) To use pulsed field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) as a genomic fingerprinting technique and Staphylococcus as a target organism to:
a. Measure the degree of microbial transfer between crewmembers
b. Determine the origin of microorganisms isolated from the spacecraft environment (e.g. which crewmember did the organism come from or was it preflight contamination)
c. Determine to what extent endogenous or acquired microbes of crewmembers increase or persist after landing.
2) Using a set of previously characterized Staphylococcus samples, assess the limits of simpler and faster fingerprinting methods using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and develop a strategy for using a primary assay.
3) To extend these results to other microorganisms of interest in long-duration missions.
4) To evaluate these assays in ground-based systems where additional monitoring was possible.
5) To determine the nature of the DNA variation being measured by the fingerprinting method.

APPROACH:
Samples of microbial flora were taken by the Microbiology Department at Johnson Space Center (JSC) during preflight training (starting approximately 5 months before launch), postflight (up to two weeks after landing), and by the crew during the mission. Samples were taken from several body locations on the crew and from environmental locations on board the Mir and Space Shuttle. These samples were processed by the JSC Microbiology Department to isolate and identify microorganisms that were present. Individual microbial isolates were then archived. Staphylococcus aureus isolates from several missions, as well as previously characterized control isolates from local hospitals, were subjected to DNA fingerprinting procedures. The two procedures used were restriction fragment length polymorphism mapping by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (RFLP-PFGE) and PCR analysis using repeated sequence primers (REP-PCR). These methods were compared using both control and experimental samples.

In addition, REP-PCR was performed on a well-characterized strain of Escherichia coli (whose chromosomal DNA sequence has been completely determined) and amplified fragments were cloned and sequenced. The sequences were analyzed to determine their origin in the genome.

All samples were processed multiple times for DNA fingerprinting to ensure high quality and reproducibility of results.

RESULTS:
A sufficiently large number of isolates of Staphylococcus aureus were obtained to allow conclusive testing of the methods and observation of microbial transfer. However, it is believed that sampling of each individual was not exhaustive enough to determine if there might be other strains of S. aureus that were not recovered. Such thorough sampling was not practicable under the sampling constraints, but does not seriously detract from the study.

Method Development: The REP-PCR technology was found to be nearly as sensitive as RFLP-PFGE. At the same time, REP-PCR was less demanding and faster. The REP-PCR technology was determined to be an excellent alternative to RFLP-PFGE. Since REP-PCR was less demanding and faster, it should be used as the initial screen in the analysis of large numbers of samples. Samples that are different can readily be distinguished by this approach. Samples that cannot be distinguished by REP-PCR can then be analyzed by RFLP-PFGE analysis, which offers increased sensitivity. Although this is more laborious, this is not a limitation when only a small number of samples are being evaluated.

Microbial Transfer: Clear examples of S. aureus transfer between crewmembers were found during the 9-12 month periods of interaction between crewmembers. This was qualitatively a higher incidence than found previously on short-term Shuttle missions. Some crewmembers were found to carry multiple strains of S. aureus, others did not carry detectable S. aureus. Some crewmembers seemed more susceptible to colonization by S. aureus than others. The greater prevalence of microbial transfer during the Mir missions as compared to the shorter term Shuttle missions argues that this phenomenon should be considered in designing other long-term missions. Differences between crewmembers as carriers could be determined ahead of time and appropriate steps could be taken to reduce individual microbial loads, possibly minimizing microbial transfer.

Candidate Strains: Analysis of the total isolates of microorganisms from STS-71, Mir 18, Mir 19 and NASA 3/Mir 22 demonstrated a number of other microbes, both bacteria and fungi, that could be transferred between crewmembers or between the crew and environment. REP-PCR methodology was shown to be applicable to several of these strains (E. coli, Corynebacteria spp., Bacillus pumilis and Enterococcus spp.). The presence of other possible candidates for microbial transfer suggests the issue may go beyond the behavior of S. aureus. The applicability of REP-PCR to the analysis of these strains suggests that further studies could readily clarify how often transfer occurs.

Molecular Basis of REP-PCR: Sequence analysis of E. coli DNA fragments generated by REP-PCR showed that in over 90% of the cases, the fragments were generated by simple PCR. Rarely was the fragment generated by crossover PCR following binding of primers at non-continuous sites. The fact that the fragments generated by REP-PCR were primarily accurate reflections of the underlying genome structure indicated that no overly sensitive steps were involved (such as would be the case in crossover PCR) and that this method was most likely to be very reliable. Therefore, REP-PCR would be a possible target for the modification of instrumentation and assay conditions for use in flight, especially on long missions where returning samples to Earth would not be possible.

Overall, this study demonstrated that microbial transfer is likely to be more of an issue for long-term exposure situations than for most current NASA missions. Moreover, a reliable and convenient method for tracking microbes by DNA fingerprinting was developed and implemented.
