Stability of Pharmacotherapeutic Compounds

Record ID: SMO_Stability_Pharm
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Analysis of certain medications returned from the Space Shuttle and the International Space Station (ISS) have indicated that some pharmaceuticals are significantly degraded in space, and this may compromise treatments and the health of crews on future exploration missions. Data gathered from past Space Shuttle missions suggest that some of the medications packed in the Shuttle's medical pack degrade even after relatively brief periods (less than 20 days) of space flight. The observed degradation included both physical and chemical characteristics of medicine formulations. Physical and chemical instability of medications could render treatments ineffective for assurance of optimal crew health during long duration space exploration missions. An evaluation of subjective data on medications used by crewmembers during space flight indicated that 8% of all treatments administered in the Space Shuttle program were reported ineffective. Pharmaceutical instability may modify effectiveness and safety, and is one possible cause of the ineffectiveness of treatments.

The objective of this project was to identify the pharmaceuticals that degrade in space by collecting data on the magnitude and time course of degradation of select pharmaceuticals. This investigation studied the effects of radiation in space on complex organic molecules, such as vitamins and other compounds in food and medicine. This could help researchers develop more stable and reliable pharmaceutical and nutritional countermeasures suitable for future long-duration missions to the moon and Mars.

APPROACH:
The pharmaceutical kits contained 35 types of pharmaceutical drugs, including tablets, ointments, suppositories, and injectable solutions. In addition to the food and drug items, the kits were equipped with two temperature loggers and a dosimeter to measure radiation.

RESULTS:
There was no crew interaction with the contents of the kits, which were configured to prevent them from being inadvertently opened. One of the kits was immediately returned on STS-122 after 13 days, while the other three remained aboard ISS for 353, 596, and 880 days of spaceflight. Each kit was returned on subsequent Shuttle flights. The flight kits were analyzed in parallel with the eight ground control kits, identical to those flown.

Results from this investigation designed to examine changes in chemical content and physical attributes of dosage forms stored aboard space missions suggest that there may be differences with respect to potency and rate of degradation of formulations stored in space compared to those on the ground. Some APIs and formulations may be more sensitive than others, and specific chemical and formulation characteristics may influence the stability of medications in space. Cumulative low dose radiation and dispensers used for solid dosages in space appear to influence stability of pharmaceuticals in space.
