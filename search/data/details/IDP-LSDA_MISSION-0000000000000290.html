Summary / Description

The SpaceX CRS-9 (SpaceX_9) mission was the ninth Commercial Resupply Services flight to the International Space Station (ISS). The Falcon 9 rocket carrying the Dragon spacecraft launched from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Air Force Station in Florida on July 18, 2016. Packed into the Dragon were nearly 5000 pounds of cargo. Along with crew supplies, tools, and spare parts, Dragon’s cargo included an International Docking Adapter required to dock spacecraft that are currently under development by the Commercial Crew program, and the hardware and supplies needed to support science and research investigations on ISS Expeditions 48 and 49.

SpaceX-9 ended with splashdown off the Baja California coast on August 29, 2016. Returning with the spacecraft were over 3000 pounds of cargo including samples collected and preserved by investigations conducted onboard the ISS, live mouse "subjects" to be studied further after their exposure to microgravity, and an array of items no longer needed onboard the ISS.

Research Highlights include:
 
Plant RNA Regulation (PRR)
This investigation studied the plant model organism Arabidopsis thaliana, a member of the mustard family that has been widely used for basic research in genetics and molecular biology. Investigators studied these seedlings to better understand the biological mechanisms involved in plant responses to spaceflight. The investigation focuses on the roles that certain types of molecules known as ribonucleic acids (RNAs) play in these responses during the early development of plant roots and shoots. The long-term goals of this research are to better understand the biological mechanisms that plants use to sense and adapt to changes in their environment. This knowledge can be applied towards designing plants that are better able to withstand adverse environmental conditions and also will help researchers to determine the ability of vegetation to provide a complete, sustainable, dependable and economical means for human life support in space.

Mouse Habitat Unit-1 (MHU-1)
This joint investigation by NASA and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) used mice to study the effects of microgravity on visual acuity in order to better understand the visual impairment that astronauts experience on long-duration spaceflights. Visual disturbances have been hypothesized to be related to increases in intracranial pressure and intraocular pressure. The study will examine conditions of the vasculature in the eye and the potential role of microgravity in altering the blood-retinal barrier, which maintains a similar function in the eye for regulating intraocular pressure as the blood-brain barrier does in the cranium. The research results may provide new and important information regarding the effects of spaceflight on oxidative stress in the eye and its potential deleterious effects on the blood-retinal barrier. The experiment was conducted in the Cell Biology Experiment Facility onboard Kibo.
