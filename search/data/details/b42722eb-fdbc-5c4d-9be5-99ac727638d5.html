Personal Dosimetry: Measurement of the Astronauts' Ionizing Radiation Exposure

Record ID: D2RDPERDO
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Since the beginning of manned space flight, the problem of radiation protection for all living organisms on board the spacecraft has been a topic of experimental biomedical research. This experiment investigated the basic tasks of personal radiation protection by measuring the exposure of the astronauts to the different types of radiation present inside the Space Shuttle.

For measurements, the radiation field was separated into its biologically relevant parts: the densely ionizing and the sparsely ionizing components. The densely ionizing component consists of the heavy ions of the cosmic radiation and the nuclear disintegrated stars, while the sparsely ionizing radiation consists of gamma rays, protons and electrons. Radiation in space mostly consists of sparsely ionizing radiation, but the densely ionizing radiation is a greater biological hazard.

APPROACH:
First, the exposure level for each component had to be established for the entire mission. Secondly, the biological responses to the joint impact of the components had to be evaluated. Of special interest were those radiobiological effects considered to be the most serious and which limit the acceptable exposure of humans.

The astronauts were each equipped with a set of four small personal dosimeters. Three dosimeters were permanently attached to the neck (to estimate the radiation dosage for the brain), to the back at waist level (for the spinal cord), and to the wrist or ankle. The wrist and ankle dosimeters were least affected by self-shielding of the body (radiation particles can be stopped by the human body), to estimate the whole body radiation exposure. Each dosimeter contained a 1 mm thick plastic detector and a 1 mm thick lithium fluoride thermoluminescence dosimeter, sealed in polyethylene bags.

The fourth dosimeter, worn on the lower body region, measured thermal neutrons back-scattered from the astronauts body as they occurred when fast neutrons hit the body (fast neutrons have too much energy to be stopped by the body, they lose energy and leave the body as thermal neutrons). This dosimeter was composed of an Albedo dosimeter, a 1 mm plastic detector and a 1 mm lithium fluoride thermoluminescence dosimeter.

After the space flight, the dosimeters were disassembled and the dosage of the different radiation types was estimated. The impact rate for each radiation type was multiplied by an individual weighting or quality factor - more biohazardous radiation had a higher factor. This factor took into account that radiation effects depend not only on the absorbed dose, but also on the type and energy of the radiation causing that dose.

RESULTS:
As expected, the mean radiation doses were lower for measurements taken at the astronaut's waist as compared to the neck and wrist positions, due to the higher body shielding. The overall radiation dose, measured at the wrist of the subjects, was higher than the dosage received by dosimeters inside of the Spacelab (this data was collected by the D-2 investigation "Measurement of the Radiation Environment Inside Spacelab at Locations Which Differ in Shieliding Against Cosmic Radiation"). Therefore, it was concluded that the astronauts stayed in locations where the shielding was lower than in the Spacelab module for considerable time periods.

Overall, the astronauts of the D-2 mission received a very low dosage of radiation during the 10-day space flight. Densely ionizing radiation was minor in appearance. However, it contributed to more than 50% of the entire dosage received by the astronauts after the values were multiplied with the correction factor.
