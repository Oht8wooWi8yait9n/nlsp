Preflight Adaptation Training (PAT)

Record ID: DSO 468
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This study assisted in developing a standardized Motion Perception Vocabulary and Reporting System that provided for quantitative descriptions of perceptual responses that occur during each phase of space flight. Such descriptions provided insight into neurosensory adaptation and readaptation processes associated with varying mission durations, and importantly, toward the evaluation of neurosensory countermeasures. These data are being directly applied to the development of preflight adaptation trainers and training procedures. Astronaut participation is essential for the development of accurate sensory stimulus conditions effective for pre-adapting crewmembers to microgravity and return to Earth's gravity.

APPROACH:
Preflight, crewmembers practiced using a Quantitative Motion Perception Reporting Vocabulary to describe their self/surround motion during brief exposures to several sensory stimulus rearrangement conditions produced by the PAT Tilt-Translation Device (TTD) in both pitch and roll orientations. Inflight (early and late), during entry, and at wheels stop, crewmembers reported perceived self/surround motion elicited by slow, low amplitude head movements in the pitch, roll, and yaw planes with eyes open and with eyes closed. Postflight, crewmembers were re-exposed to the PAT-TTD conditions and made comparisons between self/surround motion perceptions associated with head movements on-orbit, during entry, and immediately postlanding with those elicited by the various PAT-TTD conditions.

RESULTS:
Significant findings include: 1) PAT-TTD stimulus conditions can elicit perceptual responses consistent with the hypothesis that during exposure to microgravity all otolith signals are interpreted as linear translation (Otolith Tilt-Translation Reinterpretation - OTTR). 2) Exposure to PAT-TTD stimulus conditions has elicited sensations and symptoms very similar to those experienced immediately postflight for up to 7-10 days. 3) Exposure to PAT-TTD motion profiles postflight has elicited sensations of near weightlessness. 4) Linear vection can be elicited postflight where none was perceived preflight and 5) Data indicated that a 180 degree or 270 degree phase angle between tilt motion and surround translation elicit motion perception sensations more similar to those associated with pitch head movements made immediately following landing than the other phase relations presented.

Conclusions:
Data from eight crewmembers 1) support the OTTR model of neurosensory adaptation to microgravity; 2) indicate that the PAT-TTD can reproduce some of the sensory stimulus characteristics present on-orbit; and 3) support the idea that the PAT-TTD may be effective in helping to pre-adapt crewmembers. Preflight Adaptation Training is expected to speed neurosensory adaptation on-orbit, thus reducing spatial orientation disturbances and space motion sickness. Preflight preparations for on-orbit experiences will lead to a dual-adapted state which will also help speed readaptation upon return to Earth.
