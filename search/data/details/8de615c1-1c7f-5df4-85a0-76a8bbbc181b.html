High LET Radiation Induced Carcinogenesis: Epigenetic Mechanisms in Mouse Models of Human Cancers

Record ID: NNX13AK69G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This was a collaborative project with other NASA investigators who were characterizing mouse models of human lung and colon cancers. Data from both the investigator’s laboratory and the literature suggest that epigenetics play a significant role in carcinogenic processes. By describing the changes in DNA methylation and miR regulation of gene expression for these mice, investigators aimed to provide NASA with mechanistic data regarding carcinogenic initiation and progression. By using normal, cancer, and metastatic cancer cell lines they obtained information regarding epigenetic changes that occur during carcinogenesis based on stage. For example, loss of control of gene expression for extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling genes may foster metastatic behavior in colon cancer. For this reason they included ECM gene promoter DNA methylation and gene expression among the loci evaluated in this study to the hypothesis that dis-regulation of epigenetic marks and gene expression changes with disease progression. Similarly, the work of other NASA investigators suggests epigenetically regulated differences among cell lines in expression of inflammatory response pathway gene regulation among lung cell lines. These experiments provide information regarding radiation responses of these mouse tissues and human cell lines based on the linear energy transfer (LET) of the radiation used. Abnormal epigenetic silencing of genes can occur at a variety of times during tumor progression.

This study had the following specific aims:

  1. Evaluate normal and cancer cell lines from lung and colon for responses to low dose high LET radiation exposure.
  2. Establish epigenetic profiles for in vivo radiation induced cancers in mouse models of human lung and colon cancers.

APPROACH:
For the first aim, nvestigators used normal, cancer, and metastatic cancer cell lines to test the hypotheses that functional differences in profiles of DNA methylation and miRNA (miR) mediated gene regulation are a) associated with tumor aggressiveness, b) altered following radiation exposure to affect epigenetic carcinogenic progression, and c) differ based on the (LET) of the radiation used. Aim two tested three hypotheses in mouse models of human lung and colon cancer. These hypotheses were a) miR expression affects carcinogenic initiation and progression by altering target gene expression, b) aberrations in gene promoter DNA methylation affect changes in gene expression contributing to the initiation/perpetuation of radiation carcinogenesis, and c) hypomethylation of repeat elements allows reactivation of the elements that may increase the likelihood of retrotransposition and genomic instability in radiation induced carcinogenesis.

Because work from a separate NASA project demonstrated a lack of radiation responsiveness for two long interspersed nucleotide elements (LINEs) in high LET irradiated mice, investigators developed an additional panel of pyrosequencing assays for intracisternal A particles (IAP) that have shown changes in DNA methylation following exposure to chemicals. These IAP are a different class of mouse retrotransposable elements than the LINE. They are members of an endogenous retrovirus family that are also kept inactivated via high levels of DNA methylation, though.

RESULTS:
Because funding arrived late in the year, the first NSRL run was the NSRL14A campaign. During that run, investigators exposed lung cell lines to 600 MeV/n 56Fe ions and 350 MeV/n 28Si ions at doses of 10 and 50 cGy. Samples of these cell lines were snap frozen at BNL for early postirradiation time points. For later time points, the cells are now being grown up from frozen cell stocks. Shortly, they will begin the comparisons of DNA methylation changes based on cellular genotype and radiation dose and quality. They are also isolating the RNAs in preparation for qRT-PCR for gene expression and analysis of miR mediated changes in gene expression.

Investigators have demonstrated a correlation between epigenetic aberrations and adverse effects of space relevant irradiation on cognition. In cognitively impaired irradiated mice they observed increased 5-methylcytosine and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine levels in the hippocampus that coincided with increased levels of the DNA methylating enzymes DNMT3a, TET1 and TET3. By inhibiting methylation using 5-iodotubercidin, investigators demonstrated amelioration of the epigenetic effects of irradiation. In addition to protecting against those molecular effects of irradiation, 5-iodotubercidin restored behavioral performance to that of unirradiated animals. The findings of this study establish the possibility that neuroepigenetic mechanisms significantly contribute to the functional and structural changes that affect the irradiated brain and cognition.

There are no datasets available. A final report was archived.
