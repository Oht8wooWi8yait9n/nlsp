Hydrazine Monitoring

Record ID: DTO 640
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
To monitor hydrazine concentrations accurately and safely, hydrazine is converted into a stable derivative that was monitored and correlated to the actual hydrazine concentration. The hydrazine’s reactivity was harnessed to produce a chemical reaction that formed a stable gas-phase derivative which would not react or decompose before it reached the detector.

Hydrazine, monomethylhydrazine, and unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine belong to a class of compounds known as hypergolic fuels. These fuels self-ignite upon mixing with hypergolic oxidizer (dinitrogen tetroxide), without need of a spark or other ignition source. The resulting reaction produces thrust with exceptionally high energy, making these compounds particularly useful as rocket propellants.

Hydrazines are also highly toxic and corrosive. The combined properties of reactivity, corrosivity, and toxicity present the potential for a leak, a disastrous situation in a hypergol-loaded system. Consequently, leak detection is of the utmost importance in protecting equipment and personnel. The Hydrazine Monitor was tested to ensure effective real-time operations so that it could be manifested as operational hardware on future (EVA) missions.

APPROACH:
After activation, the Hydrazine Monitor and Data Logger were sequentially placed in the airlock, near the galley and near the Water Collection System (WCS), where real-time measurements of hydrazines were taken. Instrument response was displayed to the crewmember via a bar display, and spectra were stored by the Data Logger for post¬flight analyses. Measurements were taken at each of the three locations prior to the pre-breathe period, during the pre-breathe period, and after the EVA.

RESULTS:
No hydrazines were detected in any of the measurements taken on orbit. Post-mission inspection of the stored spectra indicated a very low (not toxicologically significant) level of ammonia present in the cabin air. An instrument lock-up condition (display locked up in a full bar condition) was obtained when liquid water droplets were inadvertently sucked into the instrument inlet by the internal pump. Pre-flight versus postflight calibration status of the Hydrazine Monitor indicates that the instrument’s response to hydrazines is unaffected by the rigors of space flight.
