Renal Stone Risk During Space Flight: Assessment and Countermeasure Validation

Record ID: 96-E057
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The purpose of this study was to assess the renal stone-forming potential of humans as a function of mission duration and to determine how long after space flight the increased risk exists. In addition to the analyses of mineral components, this study was designed to determine levels of the naturally occurring urinary protein inhibitors of calcium crystallization. This investigation provides a direct understanding of and overcoming human limitations in space that may affect crew health and potentially mission success. If a crewmember were to develop a kidney stone, only supportive action is possible at this time. Medical support would involve pain relief through on-board medication with the hope that the crewmember would pass the stone over time. The afflicted crewmember may not be able to perform his/her duties or, more significant, cause the mission to be aborted and return to Earth.

Previous data have demonstrated that human exposure to the microgravity environment of space increases the risk of renal stone development during and immediately after space flight. In-flight assessment has shown a greater risk of calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate and sodium urate stones. Postflight changes to the urinary chemical composition increase the risk of uric acid and calcium oxalate stone formation.

Astronauts in the U.S. program have experienced multiple postflight stone events (2 crewmembers had 2 postflight stones). The length of time for appearance of symptomatic stone formation ranged from 9-120 months. Six of these postflight stones have occurred since 1994 and have taken place concurrently with the beginning of the extended shuttle missions of greater than 12 days in space. Discussions with Russian medical science investigators have noted multiple stone events among cosmonauts, with one in-flight renal stone episode nearly causing the crewmember to abort the mission. These epidemiologic data may suggest that that risk of stone formation may extend beyond the testing period implemented during this investigation in some crewmembers.

APPROACH:
Potassium citrate (K-cit), a proven Earth-based therapy to minimize calcium-containing renal stone development, was tested during space flight as a countermeasure to reduce the risk of renal stone formation. Preflight baseline data collection sessions were performed at approximately L-180, L-60 and L-45 days. Two 10 mEq potassium citrate tablets (total dose 20 mEq) or placebo were ingested daily with the evening meal beginning three days before launch, all days during flight, and following landing through R+14. Urine was collected before, during, and after flight. Food, fluid, exercise, and medications were monitored before and during the urine collection period in order to assess environmental influences other than microgravity. During ISS operations, 24 hour urine collections and 48 hour dietary monitoring were performed early (< FD 30), mid-mission (FD30-FD100), and late (within 30 days of landing). Postflight urine and dietary monitoring were performed at approximately R+0-2, R+7 and R+14 days.

Subjects were active astronauts and cosmonauts who were certified for space flight. There were no gender specific requirements, therefore both male and female crewmembers participated in this study. This investigation was designed as a double blind study with neither the investigators nor the subjects knowing whether the subjects were ingesting potassium citrate or placebo.

RESULTS:
This investigation was successfully completed by 19 crewmembers. The majority of renal stones are composed of calcium containing salts. The risk of calcium oxalate stone formation as determined by the urinary supersaturation of calcium oxalate was observed to be lower in the potassium citrate treated group. The decreased risk was evident during the in-flight and immediate postflight assessment sessions. The risk of calcium phosphate (brushite) risk followed the same trend observed with the risk of calcium oxalate stone formation.

This study also evaluated the effect of fluid intake and urinary output on the supersaturation of the urine and the potential risk of stone formation. Regarding the correlation between 24-hr urinary volume and the risk of calcium oxalate stone development, data indicate an inverse relationship between urine volume and risk of calcium oxalate stone formation.

Approval for elimination of the double blind requirement was received from NASA in December, 2005. As a result, the participating crewmembers ingested only potassium citrate as a countermeasure to the risk of renal stone formation. Data from all the ISS missions were combined for a detailed analysis involving statistical analysis and evaluation of the pharmacologic countermeasure to reduce the risk of stone formation.

Potassium citrate-treated crew members had decreased urinary calcium excretion and maintained the calcium oxalate supersaturation risk at preflight levels compared to that in controls. Increased urinary pH in the treatment group decreased the risk of uric acid stones. Results from this investigation suggest that supplementation with potassium citrate may decrease the risk of renal stone formation during and immediately after spaceflight.
