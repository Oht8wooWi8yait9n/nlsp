Haptics Feedback Control in Microgravity

Record ID: Haptics_1
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
It is expected that the future of human space exploration will rely heavily on advanced telerobotic systems to support human presence and activity in harsh environments. So far, all testing of the technology has been performed on the ground, and never with a microgravity adapted crewmember. Ground testing has shown that proprioceptive feedback – the awareness of the position of the body’s limbs and the applied forces when performing a task – is important for the control and the performance of remote operated telerobotic systems. It is known that the kinesthetic feedback for humans in space is altered, and little is known about haptic (i.e. force) proprioception alterations when subjected to a microgravity environment. Therefore, a series of tasks will be performed by microgravity-adapted crewmembers using a telerobotic system with proprioceptive feedback that utilizes haptic display technology. Haptic displays are mechatronic devices that provide force stimuli to crew during control to stimulate force proprioception, similar to a force-feedback joystick. Such force stimuli are then either simulated (as is the case for this experiment) or can also directly result from measured forces from a telerobotic system that interacts with its environment.

It is the objective of the Haptics-1 experiment to perform an in-orbit engineering parameter calibration and identification for one joint of a future exoskeleton haptic device and to perform a first extensive set of force feedback control performance measurements with crew operating force reflective controllers in microgravity.

In particular, the following results are expected from the experiment:
(1) The first, complete set of mechatronic engineering parameters identified for enabling safe interaction in microgravity with high performance haptic devices;
(2) The first detailed evaluation of haptic feedback perceptional changes under micro-gravity during position and force control tasks;
(3) The first extensive human factors data set identified for haptic interaction after long exposure to microgravity environments (more than 3 months)
(4) Validation of a new Graphical User Interface (GUI) guided procedures approach for robotics control software (used for real-time experiment control & monitoring from a touch-screen tablet); and
(5) Validation of mechatronic safety concept for haptic controllers in close human contact for safety relevant applications.

APPROACH:
The purpose of this experiment is to gather information about haptic feedback in microgravity, in order to allow more efficient preparation of technologies necessary for such future applications. For this purpose, a single degree of freedom haptic force control joystick will be provided, together with a touchscreen tablet, for performing a suite of parameter identification and control experiments with human-in-the-loop characteristic.

The Haptics-1 experiment consists of two sessions made up of seven independent protocols each that will be carried out by each crew participating in the experiment. The two sessions will be distinguished by two mounting configurations of the force control joystick, i.e. wall-mounted and body-mounted.

Two inflight data collection sessions are planned for each crew; one as a wall-mounted configuration and the other as a body-mounted configuration. Each session consists of seven individual small protocols that can be performed in random order. The Tablet PC GUI keeps track of status of execution per crew. The inflight portion of the experiment should be performed no earlier than three months after arrival in orbit to ensure that the crew is fully adapted to the microgravity environment.

Data collection will be done by the payload hardware itself. Prior to the sessions, the crew will set-up the equipment from its storage location in either the wall-mounted or body-mounted configuration. Then, the crew will follow the guided procedures on the Tablet PC screen. They can select each of the seven protocols and perform them until the minimum number of trials has been performed by that crew. The software logs the crew ID and the number of trials executed per protocol.

The seven protocols that make up one session are:
(1) System self-test
(2) Human admittance identification (human-in-the-loop)
(3) Human position control bandwidth identification (human-in-the-loop)
(4) Human force control bandwidth identification (human-in-the-loop)
(5) Torque discrimination task (human-in-the-loop)
(6) Stiffness discrimination task (human-in-the-loop)
(7) Crisp contact perception choice task (human-in-the-loop)

RESULTS:
Results for this investigation are unavailable. This is an international experiment. NASA does not currently have an agreement with international space partners to archive their data in the LSDA.
