The Influence of Rotational Cues on Human Tilt and Translation Responses

Record ID: NNJ04HF79G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
To investigate the influence of rotational cues on the neural processing of tilt and translation in humans, the investigators proposed to develop a countermeasure to adapt and/or pre-adapt astronaut canal-otolith interactions to any gravitational environment (Earth, Mars, etc.) by providing correlated sensory cues between the canals and the otoliths. The countermeasure they developed often referred to as Artificial Gravity, consisted of centrifugation combined with intermittent motion. They measured behavioral (eye movements such as vestibulo-ocular reflexes) as well as psychophysical responses (orientation estimation) during tilt alone, translation alone or combined tilt-translation motion in either a 1-G or hypergravic environment.

This study addressed the following specific aims:
1) Using psychophysics and eye movement measurements, investigate how humans utilize semicircular canal cues to help estimate the relative orientation of gravity (monitored through perceptual tilt responses) as well as the estimate of linear acceleration (monitored through translational VOR responses). Investigators proposed a ground study to measure human responses (perceptual tilt and eye movements) during combined canal-otolith stimulation on Earth, in a 1-G environment. Their goal was to provide a perceptual measure of tilt with simultaneous eye movement recording to comprehensively investigate how the human Central Nervous System distinguishes tilt from translation. Subjects were tested using protocols in which they passively a) tilt, b) translate, or c) tilt and translate simultaneously.
2) Using psychophysics and eye movement measurements, investigate how humans utilize semicircular canal cues to help estimate the relative orientation of gravity (monitored through perceptual tilt responses) as well as the estimate of linear acceleration (monitored through translational VOR responses) by measuring adaptive changes induced by exposure to a hyper-G environment. Investigators proposed to measure human perception of orientation and eye movement responses during exposure to artificial gravity. To induce adaptation, they proposed to utilize intermittent motion during centrifugation: passive roll tilt in a supine orientation will dynamically stimulate both semicircular canals and otolith organs, such that both sensory cues are correlated.

APPROACH:
Using a variable-radius roll swing motion paradigm, the investigators examined the influence of inter-aural (y-axis) and dorso-ventral, (z-axis) force modulation on perceived tilt and translation by measuring perception of horizontal translation, roll tilt, and distance from center of rotation (radius) at 0.45 and 0.8 Hz using standard magnitude estimation techniques (primarily verbal reports) in darkness.

RESULTS:
Results show that motion perception was significantly influenced by both y- and z-axis forces. During constant radius trials, subjects’ perceptions of tilt and translation were generally almost veridical. By selectively pairing radius (1.22 m and 0.38 m) and frequency (0.45 and 0.8 Hz, respectively), the y-axis acceleration could be tailored in opposition to gravity so that the combined y-axis gravitoinertial force (GIF) variation at the subject’s ears was reduced to about 0.035 m/s2 – in effect, the y-axis GIF was “nulled” below putative perceptual threshold levels. With y-axis force nulling, subjects overestimated their tilt angle and underestimated their horizontal translation and radius. For some trials a radial linear acceleration at twice the tilt frequency (0.25 m/s2 at 0.9 Hz, 0.13 m/s2 at 1.6 Hz) was simultaneously applied to reduce the z-axis force variations caused by centripetal acceleration and by changes in the z-axis component of gravity during tilt. For other trials, the phase of this radial linear acceleration was altered in order to double the magnitude of the z-axis force variations. Z-axis force nulling further increased the perceived tilt angle and further decreased perceived horizontal translation and radius relative to the y-axis nulling trials, while z-axis force doubling had the opposite effect. Subject reports were remarkably geometrically consistent; an observer model-based analysis suggests that perception was influenced by their knowledge of swing geometry.
