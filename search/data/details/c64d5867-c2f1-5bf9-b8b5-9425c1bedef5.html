Integrated Experimental and Computational Study of Radiation Induced Matrix Remodeling in a Human Skin Equivalent

Record ID: NNX10AB06G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Current risk estimates for manned space flight are based predominantly on human epidemiology data following low linear energy (LET) radiation exposures. These data are scaled to the radiation types and fluences commonly encountered in space through the application of radiation quality factors and dose, dose rate modification factors. NASA and the scientific community acknowledge the limitations of this approach, however appropriate data on the radiation quality dependence for high energy, high atomic number ions (HZE) such as those found in space are currently unavailable making for large uncertainties in risk estimates. This application proposed an integrated experimental and computational strategy to develop a predictive understanding of the link between cell signaling, gene expression, and proximal tissue responses to radiations commonly found in space.

APPROACH:
Investigators aimed to achieve this by quantitatively measuring the appropriate variables that represent these processes and using the experimental data to construct a mathematical model of the relationships between the measured variables. The constructed model provided them with a mechanistic understanding of the effect of radiation quality on tissue responses. Experiments are specifically designed to obtain information on how track structure modifies the radiation induced signaling pathways important to maintaining tissue homeostasis. Using a 3D skin tissue model, investigators determined changes in gene expression and protein activation levels for targeted matrix signaling components as a function of radiation quality for two constant beam fluences. Ion beams used were: 600 MeV/u iron, 400 MeV/u silicon and 250 Mev/u oxygen. Investigators used immunohistochemistry to monitor overall matrix integrity as well as cell proliferation and apoptosis rates. They used the quantitative data collected to determine the parameters of a coarse-grained model involving the key modulators of matrix remodeling.

RESULTS:
The goal of this project was to use a combined experimental and computational approach to develop a predictive understanding of the link between cell signaling, gene expression, and proximal tissue responses to radiations commonly encountered in space. Investigators hypothesized that radiation quality differentially affects the tissue response by altering signaling pathways and cellular responses. They characterized molecular, cellular, and tissue level changes induced by radiation – an essential step to construct an integrated understanding of the response. This enabled them to identify the critical biological endpoints for further investigation. Investigators observed radiation quality-dependent effects on endpoints related to the cancer development processes including proliferation, differentiation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. They also constructed a predictive agent based model to understand regulation of proliferation and differentiation following high LET exposures.

All experiments were performed with a commercially available three dimensional (3D) skin equivalent composed of normal human epidermal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts. This is a robust experimental model for mechanistic studies of radiation effects on epithelial tissues. To study radiation quality dependent effects, investigators exposed the skin model to low linear energy transfer (LET) radiation X-rays, and a range of high atomic number ions (HZE). All ion exposures were conducted with constant ion fluences (3.6 x 10(4) and 1.1 x 10(5) particles/cm2). By following this irradiation protocol, they can focus on the delta ray contribution to the induced tissue responses.

Investigators initially studied the global temporal response of the skin model to low LET X-rays using microarray-based transcriptional profiling. This data provided a baseline for comparing high LET responses, and revealed the features and biological processes relevant for radiation responses. They identified cell-type specific responses occurring in the epidermal and dermal layers. The data suggested that ionizing radiation affects cell proliferation and the differentiation profile in the tissue, and affects ECM remodeling.

The data demonstrates that HZE radiation induces LET, and fluence dependent changes in the fraction of proliferating cells, marked changes in the thickness of the epidermal layer, and altered cell proliferation and/or differentiation in the tissue. The largest effect on epidermal thickness was for Neon ion exposures. Investigators analyzed the results using a mathematical model that links cell behavior changes to the experimentally measured tissue-level quantities. Analysis with the agent-based model suggested that radiation-induced changes in both cell proliferation and differentiation are required to match the experimental observations. They have subsequently validated these findings by measuring mRNA expression levels for proliferation and differentiation markers. To correlate observed changes in differentiation with skin function, they measured barrier function following X-ray and Fe ion exposures. Neither radiation disrupted the skin barrier in the first 72 hours following exposure. All high LET data endpoints were compared to low and medium doses of low LET X-ray radiation (3 to 200 cGy).

Data on mRNA expression, signaling and metalloprotease activities viewed in the context of the ECM remodeling pathway indicated that connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) and plasmin were important drivers of ECM changes in the system. Following HZE ion exposures, investigators observed differential effects on CTGF intracellular localization in the epidermal and dermal layers as a function of LET. They used a qRT-PCR array containing 84 genes in the ECM remodeling pathway to perform targeted studies of the radiation quality dependence of these biological endpoints, and found strong LET-dependence in the response. While, the ECM pathway was found to be affected both at high and low LET, there was little overlap in the specific transcriptional targets. Interestingly, high LET exposures exhibited a very different cell type specific response than X-rays where dermal responses predominated. HZE exposures resulted in almost a complete absence of transcriptional changes in the dermis. This is likely due to the inhomogeneity of the energy deposition pattern for high LET radiation, and the low density of cells (fibroblasts) in the dermal layer (in the skin model the cell density is ~85% lower in the dermis compared to the epidermis).
