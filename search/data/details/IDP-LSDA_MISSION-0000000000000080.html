Summary / Description

The flight of Friendship 7, or Mercury 6 (MA-6), marked a place in history as being the first American manned orbital flight. MA-6 launched on February 20, 1962, carrying astronaut John Glenn into orbit for nearly five hours before descending to Earth. The launch vehicle was the first manned flight to utilize the Atlas D booster rocket, which placed pilot John Glenn into the orbital insertion window where he completed three orbits of Earth.

The flight provided a significant addition to the body of information regarding human responses to this new situation. The life sciences objectives of the flight included the study of the effects of microgravity, launch and reentry accelerations, and gravity transition periods.

The much shorter Redstone ballistic flights permitted little time in the weightless phase for physiological adjustment mechanisms to stabilize. The MA-6 mission provided a period of near-weightlessness of sufficient duration so that the pilot's physiological responses attained a relatively steady state. In addition to the biosensor data, the pilot's subjective evaluation of general body sensations provided a very important source of information. His comments regarding body function, such as, spatial orientation, eating, urination, and task performance, were regarded as most significant. Additional information on the operation of the spacecraft environmental control system and the bioinstrumentation system was also obtained.

Specifically, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature and, for the first time, blood pressure were measured during the flight of MA-6. Preflight and postflight calibrations of these measurements were performed. The total biosensor monitoring time, from astronaut insertion into space until just prior to landing, was 8 hours and 33 minutes.

During the early part of orbit number one, Glenn tested the astronaut's potential as a spatial navigator by visually tracking the spent rocket booster nearby, making depth and distance determinations. This and other experiments were interrupted just prior to orbit number two when the jets controlling the yaw (side-to-side) movements began misfiring, pushing the capsule out of proper orientation. The value of having an astronaut on board a spacecraft to overcome mechanical difficulties was illustrated on Mercury 6, as Glenn resorted to using the semiautomatic and manual controls to maintain the capsule's attitude (direction).

Mercury 6 reentered the atmosphere safely, despite problems with the protective heat shield. The mission provided a period of extended microgravity during which the astronaut's physiological responses apparently stabilized. The values attained were within ranges compatible with normal function. No subjective abnormalities were reported by the pilot.
