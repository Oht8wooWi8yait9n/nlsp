In-flight Assessment of Renal Stone Risk

Record ID: DSO 610
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Physiological changes that occur in microgravity include changes in urine volume and composition that could contribute to the formation of kidney stones. An earlier study showed that spaceflight increases urinary concentrations of calcium and tends to increase concentrations of phosphate, probably as a result of bone demineralization. Also, earlier studies showed decreases on landing day in the concentration of citrate, which is an inhibitor of the formation of stones that contain calcium, and in urinary pH. A decrease in pH decreases the solubility of uric acid, which can form crystals that act as a nucleus for the formation of other stones. The objectives of this study were to make a preliminary assessment of spaceflight-induced changes in a profile of risk factors for kidney stones and of the influence of diet on these risk factors.

APPROACH:
Six male astronauts were studied before, during and after Shuttle missions lasting 11 to 16 days. Urine was collected during six 24-hour periods that occurred more than 10 days before launch, within 10 days of launch, on flight days 3 and 4, within 4 days of landing, on landing day, and 7 to 10 days after landing. In-flight samples were stored at room temperature in tubes containing an antiseptic. Samples collected before and after a flight were refrigerated, but the same antiseptics were added to certain aliquots to serve as controls for the in-flight samples. The urine samples were analyzed for pH and for the concentrations of potential stone-forming solutes. The degree of supersaturation was calculated for various salts, and the risk of stone formation was assessed relative to the risk in a normal population. Before and after the flights, the astronauts kept handwritten logs of food and fluid consumption for 48-hour periods beginning 24 hours before the urine collections began. During flights, intake of food and fluid was tracked with a barcode scanning system.

RESULTS:
Urine volume was less for the in-flight collections than for the preflight collections (the mean volume fell from 1.7 liters before the flight to 0.8 liters early in the flight), although the difference was not statistically significant. Urine pH dropped significantly on landing day. Urinary calcium levels increased late in the flight and were significantly greater on landing day than before the flight. Urinary potassium levels were significantly lower during the flight than before the flight, and citrate levels were also lower, although the change was not statistically significant. Relative supersaturation ratios indicated a high risk for formation of calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, struvite (ammonio-magnesium phosphate), sodium urate and uric acid stones early in the flight. The risk of calcium oxalate stones remained high throughout the flight. Dietary intakes of fluid, energy, protein, potassium, phosphorus and magnesium were all significantly reduced during the flight. The results of this preliminary study showed that astronauts (at least male astronauts) are at risk of forming kidney stones because of reductions in urine output and changes in chemical composition of the urine during spaceflight. These changes may include decreases in pH and in the concentration of citrate, as well as an increase in the concentration of calcium. Learning how to minimize the risk of forming renal stones before or after spaceflights will require additional studies of larger numbers of astronauts. A preliminary recommendation was made that astronauts drink 2.5 liters of water daily to dilute the urinary concentration of stone-forming salts.
