Experiments

Inflight Radiation Measurements

Record ID: 5.2.1
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Continuous exposure to radiation is one of the major health risks associated with long-term human space flight. When the International Space Station (ISS) receives its first crew in 2001 scientists would like to be able to accurately measure the amount of biologically important radiation the crew is receiving so that they know how long they can stay in orbit without endangering the crews health. Current models of determining radiation exposure don't take into account the fact that the primary radiation dose is much different than the dose received by human tissue after the radiation has passed through the spacecraft/human body. This modified dose is known as the biologically important dose equivalent and can only be obtained by accurately measuring the full linear energy transfer (LET) spectrum. Measurements of genetic risk can then be made using the total absorbed dose, dose rate, and the LET. The following objectives were outlined for this experiment.

1. To correlate independent simultaneous space radiation exposure measurements and independent radiation exposure calculations made by NASA/Johnson Space Center (JSC) with those made by Russian Space Alliance (RSA)/Institution of Biomedical Problems (IBMP).
2. Compare the absorbed dose measured throughout the Mir core module with calculated values and determine empirical correction factors for the currently used free space environment models.
3. Compare the measured LET spectra for the galactic cosmic radiation (GCR) and trapped particles with the calculated spectra using the most current radiation environment and transport models.

APPROACH:
To cover all aspects of this investigation, four different radiation measurement devices were flown:
1. Passive radiation dosimeters (PRD) were mounted at six preselected locations inside the Mir core module and at six locations inside the shuttle, during STS-74. The PRD's were lithium fluoride passive thermoluminescent detectors (TLD) and measured the total absorbed dose from charged particles.
2. Each of the Mir astronauts wore two crew passive dosimeters (CPDs), one dosimeter designed by the U.S., the other designed by the Russian Space Agency (RSA). The US dosimeter contained both LiF TLDs and CR-39 plastic nuclear track detectors (PNTDs), whereas the Russian dosimeters contained only TLDs.
3. A tissue equivalent proportional counter (TEPC), simulating a 2 micrometer diameter tissue was also flown on board the Mir Space Station (Mir core module). The TEPC measured the LET spectrum at one minute intervals from which the absorbed dose rates, and dose equivalent rates could be calculated. In addition, just the absorbed dose rate measured at 2 second intervals was recorded.
4. An area passive dosimeter (APD) that contained both LiF TLDs and PNTDs was also flown. The APD was mounted in the Mir core module next to the TEPC.

Although not part of this planned investigation, data from the Russian operation dosimeter, called R-16 and the French-Russian collaboration instrument, Nausicca, were also available for data comparison.

RESULTS:
Although this experiment was scheduled for both Mir 18 and 19 missions a technical problem with the Mir interface payloads system (MIPS) resulted in the loss of all TEPC data from the Mir 19 mission. Passive radiation dosimetry results show that the Russian measurements were 16% lower on Mir-18 and 8% higher on Mir-19 than the U.S results. Dose rates across the Mir core module vary by a factor of nearly two, due to the differences in shielding and spacecraft orientation. The dose rates measured by the US and Russian APDs that were mounted at the same location are in remarkable agreement. However, these rates are lower than the TEPC measured dose rate at the same location. The TEPC data is separated into two components, galactic cosmic radiation and trapped particles for calculation purposes.

Trapped Particles
The trapped particle dose rate was separated from the galactic cosmic radiation component using the one-minute TEPC dose rate data. The trapped dose rate varied by a factor of 1.7 with an average trapped absorbed dose rate of 153.4 µ Gy/day. AP8 MIN model calculations were within a reasonable limit for measurements of LET > 4 keV/µ m, but were increasingly inaccurate below 4 keV/µ m, the area where the majority of trapped particles are located.

Galactic Cosmic Radiation (GCR)
The GCR rate is modulated by changes in solar activity. The standard diffusion-convection theory describes the modulation of primary GCR differential energy spectra very well in terms of the deceleration potential. The dose rate is a monotonic non-linear function of the deceleration potential that depends on the orbit inclination. The calculated dose rate was 135 µ Gy/day with a quality factor of 3.25. Although the model provides good agreement between the dose and dose equivalent rates, there is room for considerable improvement in the model calculation of the LET spectrum.

The combined GCR plus trapped proton absorbed dose rate was 299.3 µ Gy/day, dose equivalent rate was 781 µ Sv/day, and average quality factor of 2.61. TLDs mounted next to the TEPC measured total dose rate of 241 µ Gy/day. Because TLDs are inefficient at detecting particles with LET > 10 keV/µ m PNTD data is used to estimate the additional dose. The adjusted absorbed dose rate is 272 µ Gy/day, which is only 9% lower than the TEPC measured dose rate. If the crew exposures measured with the TLDs were adjusted for this deficiency the U.S. crew skin exposure would be 1130 µ Sv/day.

The results of this experiment show: (1) our understanding of the GCR environment is fairly good in predicting the absorbed dose and dose equivalent. However, additional work is needed in the area of radiation transport models to better predict the LET spectrum; (2) the AP8 MIN model does not predict the absorbed dose, dose equivalent, or LET spectrum of trapped particles very well; (3) the average radiation quality factor, 2.6, is higher than model calculations. The major cause of this difference is due to errors in the AP8 MIN model. The effect of secondary neutrons that the TEPC measures but are not included in model calculations is also a contributing factor; (4) if the only radiation received by a crewmember is natural (GCR plus trapped particles) inside the spacecraft, crewmembers can stay in orbit for up to three years without exceeding their career limits (1000-4000 mSv); (5) there is a clear need to improve the trapped proton belt models. These static models are inadequate for predicting the exposure for long term stay in low earth orbit.
