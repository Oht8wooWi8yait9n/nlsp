Vascular Health in Space: MicroRNAs in Microgravity

Record ID: NNX15AB55G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Astronauts are faced with several health risks during both short- and long-duration spaceflight due to the space environment. Microgravity itself seems to affect cardiovascular parameters. For example, studies on animals (rats and rhesus monkeys) have found consistent decreases in heart rate during spaceflight. Similarly, data from 12 astronauts over 6 missions showed that heart rates of astronauts were reduced in space. These early studies demonstrate an obvious change in an easy to measure cardiovascular parameter. Although cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in the United States, to our knowledge, cardiovascular physiology in space has not been studied, yet. More in-depth studies are now required to shed light on how microgravity exerts changes on individual cells and tissues. We believe that subcellular effects of microgravity can be documented and we hypothesize that microRNAs (miRNAs), because of their regulatory function, will be involved. miRNAs are now understood to be powerful modulators of biological pathways and have generated considerable excitement as next-generation therapeutic targets for a variety of diseases, including cardiovascular diseases. In this proposal, we will assess the gravitational impact on the miRNA response to vascular injury and will evaluate the efficiency of vascular repair in space. This will shed light on how microgravity alters vascular remodeling and healing and could reveal currently unknown targets for potential therapeutic modulation. We will evaluate the role of miRNAs in vascular disease (Objective 1), vascular health (Objective 2), vascular smooth muscle cell function (Objective 3), and angiogenesis (Objective 4). Previously, we investigated miRNA regulation in diseased human vessels with myointimal lesions and demonstrated the important regulatory role of miRNAs in vessel injury. Our studies demonstrated that various vascular miRNAs were downregulated after vessel injury and only a few were upregulated. Recent spaceflight studies showed that the expression of miRNAs in both men and mice were sensitive to gravity. The limited data available demonstrated down-regulation of multiple miRNAs in microgravity. We therefore hypothesize that microgravity could aggravate pathologic vessel remodeling via miRNA alterations. Because inflammation is a known stimulus for myointimal hyperplasia, we will pay special attention to the inflammatory responses to vessel injury.

APPROACH:
In Objective 1, we will investigate how vascular miRNAs are differentially regulated at 1xg and µg in response to vascular balloon injury. In Objective 2, we will investigate the effect of microgravity on miRNAs in healthy vessels. If the gravitational effect on vascular miRNAs is profound, microgravity might even induce the development of vascular lesions in healthy vessels. In Objective 3, we will assess the effect of microgravity on SMC function in both healthy and diseased vessels. Under normal physiologic conditions, SMCs rarely proliferate. However, in the presence of injury or a growth stimulus, SMCs begin to proliferate and migrate. Microgravity could be another so far unknown growth stimulus. Uncontrolled SMC proliferation can result in pathologic vasoconstriction or in myointimal hyperplasia with narrowing of the lumen. Microgravity might thus affect SMC plasticity. In Objective 4, we will assess and compare angiogenesis at 1xg and µg and define the role of angiogenetic miRNAs. Since angiogenesis plays an important role in vascular health, we anticipate that effects of microgravity on angiogenesis-associated miRNAs will impact vascular health and repair processes.

RESULTS:
This experiment is currently in progress. Results will be available at the conclusion of the study.
