Vestibulo-Spinal Reflex Mechanisms

Record ID: 1NS104
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Recent research studies of the vestibular response to space flight found that exposure to prolonged microgravity results in rearranged relationships between signals from visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive receptors. Studies have also found that motor performance and spatial orientation perception are altered as a consequence of adaptation to this rearrangement. In addition, space motion sickness often accompanies the adaptation process.

The sensory rearrangement produced by space flight results in reinterpretation of otolith signals. Otolith reinterpretation is revealed by changes in postural responses, eye movement reflexes, and orientation perception. This study focused on the Hoffman reflex (H-reflex) in conjunction with linear acceleration to assess otolith-induced changes in the soleus (calf) postural muscles.

The hypothesis for this study focused on the idea that exposure to prolonged microgravity would reduce the need for postural reflexes in the major postural muscles, and that postural adaptations would reflect a change in a central location such as the brain stem as opposed to the peripheral vestibular organs. Based on this hypothesis, the purpose of this experiment was to:

  • investigate vestibulo-spinal responses as a function of prolonged space flight
  • employ the H-reflex as a means of determining the excitability of the lumbo-sacral motor neuron pool
  • investigate the concept that vestibular adaptation (otolith) is central rather than peripheral
  • relate space motion sickness susceptibility to the results of the vestibulo-spinal responses.

    APPROACH:
    Four of the six crewmembers assigned to the Spacelab 1 mission served as subjects for this investigation. Preflight data were collected 151, 121, 65, 44, and 10 days prior to the flight (F-151,... etc.), and inflight data were collected on day 2 and day 7 of the mission (Mission Elapsed Time (MET)-01 and MET-06). Postflight tests were conducted on the day of landing (R+0), R+1, R+2, R+4, and R+6. One crewmember participated in data collection at R+120. All four crewmembers participated in the preflight and postflight testing. However, only two crewmembers were tested on R+0. H-reflex and linear acceleration data was obtained inflight from one crewmember on MET-01 and from two crewmembers on MET-06.

    Hoffman Reflex Recording Set-up: The monosynaptic H-reflex was activated by electrical stimulation of the popliteal nerve and recorded from the soleus muscle. A needle electrode, which served as the cathode, was inserted in the popliteal fossa at a predetermined location on the right leg. The anode, a plate electrode, was placed just above the knee. Different levels of 1 millisecond (ms) constant current pulses limited to a maximum of 20 milliamps (ma) were delivered through an isolation unit under computer control. A differential amplifier and a bipolar surface electrode configuration were used to record the reflex from the soleus muscle. The reflex was a two-part response: a direct muscle response (M-wave) with a latency of 5 to 10 ms that was followed 15 to 20 ms later by the monosynaptic H-reflex. The M-wave represented the direct muscle response and was therefore used as a drop control response to assure constant stimulation. The H-reflex amplitude reflected the sensitivity of the lower spinal motor neuron pool as set by the descending postural control signals.

    Vestibular Stimulation - Preflight and Postflight Testing: Otolith stimulation during preflight and postflight testing was provided by dropping the subject in a special harness, designed to leave the arms and legs free, from a quick release hook. Prior to each drop, the subject grasped a T-shaped handle with both hands which allowed him to hang with his feet approximately 15 cm from the floor. For each drop the subjects were shocked three times. The shock sequence consisted of a conditioning shock and a control shock, applied before the drop, and a test shock applied during the drop. The conditioning shock, used to condition the neural tissue, was followed three seconds later by the control shock which elicited a control response. The test shock, which was applied 3 to 5 seconds later, was delivered at predetermined time delays from the onset of the drop. During the fall, tests shocks occurred coincident with the drop (0 ms delay) or at 10 ms intervals up to 80 ms following initiation of the drop. Landing times were recorded with ribbon switches placed under a rubber mat. Head acceleration was recorded with a linear Z-axis accelerometer attached to a biteboard which was held in the subject's mouth during the drop. An experimental session was composed of four drops at each of nine randomized delay times for a total of thirty-six drops. The averaged response to the test shock was normalized with respect to the averaged control shocks and presented as a percent change in H-wave (or M-wave) amplitude.

    Vestibular Stimulation - Inflight Testing: The inflight testing protocol utilized the "hop and drop" station to complete the drop tests. The station was composed of a special torso harness to hold the subject, a drop apparatus similar to the ground based device, and calibrated bungee cords adjusted to pull the subject to the floor of the Spacelab with a transition from free fall to -1 g. The delay times (from the onset of the drop to the test shock) ranged from 0 to 70 ms in 10 ms increments. On MET-01, four responses at each of the eight delays times were recorded. On MET-06, only two responses at each of the delay times were obtained. The inflight computer was used to control the experiment, release the drop mechanism, time the electric shock and collect the data. The inflight head accelerometer was held on the back of the head with a velcro strap. Floor contact was detected with ribbon switches attached to the bottom of the subject's right foot. Data on body position during each drop was obtained by filming the pre-, in-, and postflight test sessions. Every 10th film frame was analyzed to determine the angle of the head, trunk, hips, knees, and ankles. Pre-drop body angles were compared to body angles during the drop to determine the percent change in a selected body angle.

    Gastrocnemius Electromyographic (EMG) Responses: The relationship between the direct vestibulo-spinal reflex and the H-reflex was investigated by recording the EMG activity of the left leg. The electrodes were placed on the middle section of the gastrocnemius muscle. The EMG activity was recorded as a function of the sudden drop and then amplified and band pass filtered between 1 Hz and 1 kHz. The amplitude of the EMG activity was then determined over a 100 ms section that was between 50 and 150 ms of the total time of the sudden fall.

    Space Motion Sickness and Self-Motion Reports: Space motion sickness symptoms experienced by the four crewmembers were monitored as part of the protocol. Data was collected with interviews and questionnaires which were designed according to how the crewmembers responded to preflight terrestrial provocative motion sickness testing. A ranking procedure was applied to the inflight motion sickness responses which related them to the peak H-reflex preflight average. Self-motion responses were obtained pre-, in-, and postflight. The crewmembers were asked to describe the sensation experienced during the sudden falls and to note any unusual effects or illusions.

    RESULTS:
    Motor Neuron Pool Excitability: Twenty H-reflex response measurements were taken for each subject at nine different delay times. Preflight results indicated that there were considerable differences in the motor neuron pool excitability between the four crewmen tested. The maximum H-reflex response for subject D was a 300% change in amplitude over control values, while subject A showed a 1100% increase. Results from the two way analysis of variance indicated that differences in the means for the subject and the delay time factors were significantly different (p < 0.001). The significant differences were noted across subjects only and not between subjects. However, the results of a Dunnett a posteriori multiple comparison test did indicate that the means for each subject's delay times did differ significantly from each other (p < 0.01).

    Pre-, In-, and Postflight H-reflex Summary: Results for subject A indicated that the percent change of the H-reflex obtained after 24 hours (h) in orbit was similar to the preflight response. By the seventh day in flight, the H-reflex no longer increased as a function of the time delays as it did before the flight and early in flight. The R+0 data (2.5 h after landing) showed a three-fold increase of the H-reflex over preflight averages. The results of the F-test on the percent change of H-wave peak responses for subject A compared with the peak control responses showed a significant difference (p < 0.01) for test day, shock delay time, and test day by delay time. The Dunnett multiple comparison tests on the data for subject A showed that the means of the percent change of the H-reflex responses of all inflight and postflight test days, except MET-01 were significantly different (p < 0.01) from the preflight data.

    Inflight data was not collected on subject B on MET-02 due to space sickness problems. On MET-06, the H-reflex data from subject B showed very little potentiation as a function of the shock time delay. Data collected on R+0 showed a large increase in peak H-reflex responses from a preflight average of 900% to 1900%. On R+1 the maximum percent change dropped to 1700% and then rebounded on R+4 and R+6 to 1900%. By R+6, subject B was showing a tendency to return to baseline values. Subject B was also tested on R+120 and showed responses that were lower than preflight values. The F-test results indicated a significant difference (p < 0.01) for percent changes of H-reflex peak responses when compared to the control responses for test day, shock delay times, and test day by delay time. The Dunnett multiple comparison tests showed that the means of the percent changes of the preflight test days combined, and the means of the inflight and postflight test days were significantly different.

    The H-reflex amplitude for subject C showed an 800% increase over control values for R+1 and R+2. That translated into a 300% increase over the preflight value. The data for subject C showed no significant differences when analyzed with the F-test; therefore the data was not subjected to the Dunnett test. The preflight data for subject D indicated a peak percent change from control values of 260%. On R+2, a peak H-reflex response of 400% occurred which was statistically significant.

    Pre-, In-, and Postflight M-wave summary: The M-wave percent change measured from the average control responses for each subject did not show a significant difference (p > 0.05) when tested with the F-test. These data indicate that the percent changes observed with the H-reflex reflect the excitability of the motor neuron pool, and not local effects in the reflex arc or artifact.

    M- and H-reflex recruitment curves: The recruitment curves generated for subjects A, B, and C when tested with a two-way analysis of variance did not show a significant change during preflight and postflight testing. Subject D did show a significant change between between test days (p < 0.05) but not for tests within a specific day (p > 0.05). Inflight, the recruitment curves for subjects A and B were similar to those obtained preflight.

    Gastrocnemius EMG Activity: Preflight latency and amplitude measurements both within and across all four subjects indicated a stable baseline. No significant differences were noted in the preflight data when subjected to a two-way analysis of variance. Latency of the EMG response, which represented the first detectable activity following release, averaged 93.97 ms with a variance of ± 5.24 SEM. The preflight mean RMS amplitude calculated over the 50 to 150 ms interval for each drop across all four subjects was 251 mv and had an SEM of ± 57.2 average. Postflight EMG latency and RMS amplitude for each subject did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) when compared to the average preflight baseline. Postflight latency for all subjects averaged 93.63 ms and RMS amplitude averaged 243.5 mv.

    Perceived Motion and Sensations: When asked about their sensation to the sudden drop inflight, subjects A and B reported that on MET-06 they did not feel as though they were falling when compared to their experience on the ground. The sensation was described as being very abrupt and unpleasant. The postflight drops on R+0 and R+1 were reported by the crewmen tested inflight to be similar to the inflight drops on MET-06. Postflight, all crewmen reported that the sensation did not feel like a fall, but that the floor came up to meet their feet.

    Space Motion Sickness and the H-reflex: The results indicated that three of the four subjects vomited more than once during the first three days of the mission. The fourth crewmember was free of symptoms. The subjects were ranked according to severity of symptoms during the flight. Subject A and B were ranked higher than subject C. This rank order was then compared with the peak H-reflex shock delay time preflight average for each crewmember and with the postflight H-reflex curves. The results show that subjects A and B both had the greater H-reflex potentiation preflight and postflight. Subject C had less preflight H-reflex potentiation and postflight changes quickly returned to baseline. Subject D had a very low H-reflex potentiation preflight when compared with subjects A, B, and C. Antisickness medication was taken by the crew and may have affected the results. The drug was a combination of scopolamine (0.4 mg) and dexedrine (2.5 mg). The drug was used by subjects A, B, and D on different schedules during the first three days of the mission. Subject C used a combination of promethazine (25 mg) and ephedrine (25 mg) and took only one dose 28 h into the flight.
