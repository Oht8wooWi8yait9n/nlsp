The Effectiveness of Manual Control During Simulation of Flight Tasks (PILOT)

Record ID: 6.2.2
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Russian flight investigations have found evidence of functional disorders affecting psycho-physiological capacity and navigational skills during initial periods of weightlessness. Deterioration of these skills has potential impact on mission safety, as crewmembers are required to perform complex and dynamic activities throughout a mission.

Previous studies of performance have been limited to the first week in weightlessness. The PILOT experiment was designed to evaluate how crewmembers perform work tasks during a long-duration mission. Specific goals of the experiments were to: 1) evaluate how operator performance is affected by prolonged exposure to microgravity; 2) determine the stresses involved in performing work tasks during various phases of long-duration flight; 3) assess how well crewmembers retained their skills at performing work tasks during and after a long-duration flight; and 4) compare ground training performance and crewmembers' physical and mental sate with actual in flight performance and state.

This study tested the hypotheses that exposure to space flight results in temporary performance impairment and decreased mental acuity and that long-term exposure to microgravity can result in partial loss of complex manual control skills. In particular, task completion was expected to require more time in space than on the ground. Researchers also expected that manual control style would degrade, task performance would be accomplished by heightened emotional responses and operator stress and the number of response errors would increase in space. This experiment explored the long-term effects of long duration exposure to microgravity on the crewmembers' physical and mental coordination. Results of this research will contribute to further progress in optimizing crewmembers' performance and be used to develop countermeasures for increasing crewmember efficiency on future space missions.

APPROACH:
Two crewmembers, subject 1 and subject 2, were scheduled to participate in the experiment. This experiment used a ground-based Russian spacecraft control simulator and a Mir-based workstation system to simulate the re-docking of a Soyuz module to the Mir station and collect performance data. Both the simulator and workstation contained a television receiver, simulation control devices and physiological sensors. The two systems were telemetrically connected in a closed loop system, with the ground-based unit transmitting simulated vehicle states to the operator at the Mir workstation. The workstation, in turn, transmitted operator actions and physiological information to the ground-based simulator. Audio and video systems uploaded and displayed the ground-based simulator version of the orbital station Mir to the test subjects.

Crewmembers were instructed not to take psychotropic medication, consume alcohol or perform strenuous physical exercise within 24 hours of data collection.

Preflight data collection was scheduled four times preflight for both subjects during training exercises on the simulator at the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Russia. During each data collection session, the subjects completed three cycles of experimental tasks in approximately 45-50 minutes. Medical monitoring sensors called "Gamma-1" were applied to the body to detect heart rate, cranial blood flow and blood pressure. The data collection sessions established a baseline for individual levels on manual control performance (work performance) and functional state (skill retention).

The same work tasks were to be performed during flight while the subject was positioned at the Mir workstation. The experiment was scheduled to be performed on flight days 5 (FD5), FD20, FD40, and FD82 by subject 1 and FD24, FD57 and FD86 by subject 2. Heart rate, cranial blood flow and blood pressure were to be measured using the same medical equipment as preflight. At the completion of each data collection session, the subject was to complete a self-report describing his functional state and manual control performance. All data was to be transferred from the Mir to the Shuttle (STS-71) for return to earth.

Postflight, both subjects were scheduled to be debriefed the third day after landing (R+3) during a 30-minute verbal question-and-answer session using a structured questionnaire developed by Russian investigators. A self-evaluation of the difficulty and quality of task performance was to be collected during this debriefing. Simulator sessions were to be performed on R+7 and R+30 using the same equipment as the preflight data collection sessions.

RESULTS:
Data was collected preflight for both subjects but the results were not reported to the US investigator. In-flight data was collected on FD5 and FD20 for subject 1, but the data was incomplete for those sessions. Data was not collected on subject 1 as planned on FD40 and FD82 and no postflight data was collected for this subject. Sessions scheduled on FD24, FD57, and FD86 for subject 2 were canceled and no postflight data were collected for this subject.

The PILOT experiment was not performed successfully in flight due to hardware and logistical problems. Experiment hardware used during ground-based training was considerably different from that used in flight and the control strategies for using the flight hardware differed from those of the ground hardware. Also, the procedures used in conducting the experiment preflight differed from in flight procedures.

Given the incompleteness of the data collected and the anomalies with ground and flight hardware and the preflight and in flight procedures used, researchers were unable to perform data analysis. No valid conclusions or findings were possible.
