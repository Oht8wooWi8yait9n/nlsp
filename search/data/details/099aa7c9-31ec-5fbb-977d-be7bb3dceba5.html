Novel Microstructures for Vascular Tissue Engineering

Record ID: NNJ04HC73G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The major goal of this research project was to tissue-engineer small diameter blood vessel substitutes. Such tissue-engineered blood vessels can also serve as tissue models for studying the effects of microgravity environmental changes on tissue adaptation and to assess potential therapeutic strategies. Requirements for a blood vessel substitute include sufficient burst pressure, viscoelastic mechanical properties, vasoactivity, and nonthrombogenicity. Native blood vessels have evolved a sophisticated structure and hierarchical organization of cell types and matrix molecules in order to satisfy these requirements.

The central hypothesis is that the physiological spatial arrangement of cells and extracellular matrix in an engineered blood vessel will affect the viscoelastic mechanical properties, which in turn, will affect vascular smooth muscle and endothelial cell response. This hypothesis will be tested by answering questions directly posed in Specific Aims 1 and 2 in which the effects of two main biomaterial scaffold properties on tissue function will be explored: (1) microstructure and (2) mechanics.

APPROACH:
The strategy of fabricating novel scaffolds with defined microstructure and mechanical properties will help to reveal the effects of specific ultrastructural and mechanical factors on cell response and tissue development. Investigators used this approach to develop a new generation of biomaterials for vascular tissue engineering that will provide an in vitro model tissue in which parameters can be varied systematically to investigate cell mechanics, tissue development, cell-substrate and cell-cell interactions in controlled microgravity environments in order to attain a better understanding of effects of microgravity on human physiology. Furthermore, the investigators recognize that these tissues may also be used as potential blood vessel replacements that are in high demand and are an integral part of more complex organs and tissues.

RESULTS:
Investigators have made significant progress in investigating the role of microstructure on vascular smooth muscle cells, specifically studying the role of substrate microtopography combined with mechanical cyclic strain on cell orientation. They also developed novel methodologies to self-assemble biomacromolecules such as collagen that are critical components of blood vessels. They also showed that they could stack several layers of microtopographically patterned thin scaffold films and keep the cells viable in this configuration, a critical step in forming the blood vessels.
