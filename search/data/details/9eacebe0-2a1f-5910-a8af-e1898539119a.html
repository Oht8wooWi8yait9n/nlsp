Printed Electrochemical Sensor Strip for Quantifying Bone Density Loss in Microgravity

Record ID: Bone_Sensor
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Without the load-bearing forces of gravity experienced on earth, astronauts have an increased risk of bone-related injuries resulting from changes in bone mineral density. Microgravity disrupts the balance between bone formation and bone resorption which can lead to the development of space osteopenia/osteoporosis after extended exposure to microgravity, which can manifest in a reduction of bone density by 1 to 1.6%. Development of a sensor that monitors reliable biomarkers of bone loss is a critically important step to help mitigate this specific risk. This experiment aimed to develop a flight suitable sensor that evaluated the change in bone mass indirectly through an excreted biomarker of bone remodeling, amino-terminal collagen crosslinks (NTX). NTX, a degradation by-product of Type I collagen, is widely measured in clinical bone resorption studies and is considered a biomarker of interest when evaluating bone health.

Bone density loss is currently evaluated through the measurement of bone mineral density by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) of flight crew before and after spaceflight. However, DXA only provides partial information regarding bone strength and cannot detect the early shift of bone metabolism. Several bone remodeling biomarkers have been identified and used to provide an early assessment of bone loss, when DXA data is insufficient to make a diagnosis. Moreover, the equipment necessary for DXA is bulky, heavy, and energy inefficient, making it incompatible for flight. The proposed printed bone health sensor would be more suitable for spaceflight due to its versatility, size, and ease of use. Not only is the necessary hardware more compact than DXA equipment, but the sensor is also conducive to potential in-flight manufacturing using commercially available materials for inkjet printing.

This study had the following specific aims:
  1. Develop conductive, telopeptide selective and dielectric inks for printed sensor.
  2. Print and electrochemically characterize 3-electrode device for NTX detection.
  3. Integrate sensor with urine collection device and handheld potentiostat hardware.

APPROACH:
Aim 1: Develop conductive, telopeptide selective and dielectric inks for printed sensor.
a) Develop conductive and dielectric inks
Both commercial and home-made inks were evaluated for this study. JS-B40G silver nanoparticle (AgNP) ink, XP PriElex SU-8 1.0, and 7500 Series carbon nanotube (CNT) were used without modification as inkjet conductive and dielectric inks. BQ242 carbon, FE3124 silver, and 5880 silver|silver chloride (Ag|AgCl) were used without modification. Home-made carboxylic acid functionalized multiwalled carbon nanotube (MWCNT-COOH) ink was modified in house by refluxing commercial MWCNT-COOH in 56% nitric acid for 8 hours, sonicating by probe sonication for 3 hours, and finally resuspending in water. Home-made gold nanoparticle (AuNP) ink was synthesized using the Tukevich method and then resuspending in water. Homemade inks were evaluated by dynamic light scattering and zeta potential (ζ-potential) analysis to measure particle size and surface charge.

b) Develop telopeptide selective ink and conjugation reactant
Telopeptide selective inks of AuNP functionalized with NTx were prepared. A solution of 10 nM citrate capped AuNP (AuNP@citrate), 0.028% SDS in water and 0.015 mg/mL of HS-PEG-COOH was incubated overnight at room temperature. The solution was washed with DI water, centrifuged at 14000xg for 30 minutes at 4°C, and resuspended in 25 mM MES pH 5.5 to remove excess PEG, yielding pegylated AuNP (AuNP@PEG-COOH). To 20 mL of 1 nM AuNP@PEG-COOH 250 μL of EDC (0.2M) and 500 μL of NHS (0.2M) were added. The solution was allowed to incubate for 30 minutes at room temperature, centrifuged at 14000xg for 30 minutes at 4°C, and suspended in 10 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.4, yielding EDC/NHS activated AuNP@PEG-COOH. 100 μL of NTx (1 mg/mL) was added to the EDC/NHS activated AuNP@PEG-COOH solution and incubated overnight at room temperature. The resulting AuNP@PEG-NTx solution was centrifuged twice at 14000 xg for 30 minutes at 4°C and finally suspended in DI water as the telopeptide selective ink. The telopeptide ink was evaluated at each step of preparation, AuNP@citrate, AuNP@PEG-COOH, and AuNP@PEG-NTx, by UV-Vis, dynamic light scattering and zeta potential (ζ-potential) analysis (ZetaSizer Nano, Malvern PANanalytical, Westborough, MA) to measure particle size and surface charge. The AuNP@PEG-NTx will be referred to as AuNPNTx for simplicity.

c) Develop Anti-NTx conjugate
Conjugate antibody was prepared for the sensor’s competitive assay. Methylene blue-NHS was covalently bound to Anti-NTx using EDC/NHS coupling chemistry to form Anti-NTx-MB. 100 μl of antibody (0.4 mg/ml) were incubated with 10 μL of methylene blue (1 mM) for 1 hour. The labeling reaction was dialyzed using a 10 kDa molecular weight cut off dialysis membrane against PBS. The PBS was substituted with fresh PBS twice (every 2 hours) and left to dialyze overnight.

Aim 2: Print and electrochemically characterize 3-electrode device for NTx detection.
a) Print sensor
3-electrode electrochemical sensors, containing a working electrode (WE), counter electrode (CE) and reference electrode (RE), have been prepared by the following two methods: 1) precision microdispense printing by an nScrypt 3Dn-Series printer and 2) dropcast method. All sensors were print onto Kapton 500HN as the substrate material. To generate the base architecture of the electrochemical sensors, precision microdispense printing was performed. A nScrypt 3Dn-Series printer was used to laser cut 3 holes through a Kapton substrate. Next, FE3124 silver ink was microdispense printed inside the 3 holes to create a via structure and around the holes on one side of the Kapton to form the contact pads. Finally, the substrate was flipped over to expose the front side of the device. 5880 Ag|AgCl ink was microdispensed to form the RE and BQ242 ink was microdispensed to form the WE and CE. All prints were performed at the NextFlex Research Institute in San Jose, CA and delivered to the NASA ARC team for evaluation, electrochemical characterization, and further modification with 7500 Series CNT and AuNP-NTx inks.

To generate the telopeptide sensing surface, modification of the WE were performed first by a drop cast method and finally by inkjet printing. For the drop cast method, 0.5 μL of 7500 Series CNT ink was pipet on the WE surface and annealed at 125°C on a hotplate for 15 min. Finally, 1 μL of AuNP-NTx (37 nM) ink was pipet on the CNT modified WE surface and dried at 42°C for 10 min. The resulting electrochemical sensors were characterized immediately or stored in a petri dish at 4°C. For inkjet printing, the 45 layers of the 7500 Series CNT ink were deposited on the surface of the WE. The printing bed was heated to 60 °C and the print head was heated to 42°C. The CNTs were then dried at 125 °C in a convection oven at 125 °C for 15 min. The electrodes sheet was then loaded and aligned in the inkjet printer. 10 layers of the AuNP-NTx selective ink were then deposited on top of the CNT modified WE. For this deposition the print bed temperature was lowered to 40 °C and the printhead was kept at 42 °C. The resulting electrochemical sensors were characterized immediately or stored in a petri dish at 4°C.

b) Develop fluidics
Fluid delivery was accomplished using a lateral flow assay assembly. A lateral flow assay’s fluid handling components consist of various layered wicking materials that are assembled in a plastic cartridge. A sample pad consisting of bound glass fiber was placed under the sample inlet port of the top of the cartridge. A conjugation pad consisting of glass fiber and containing the dried anti-NTx conjugate was placed overlapping and underneath the sample pad. A membrane of nitrocellulose served as the fluid delivery layer and contacted the printed sensor, the conjugate pad and a waste pad consisting of high purity cotton fibers. All the layered wicking materials and the printed sensor were arranged in a clamshell configured plastic cartridge with top and bottom components that snap closed for a tight fit. The lateral flow’s fluid delivery was evaluated by assembling and running an optical assay through the assembled fluid handling layers. The conjugate pad was cut to size and saturated with 1× PBS pH 7.4, 1% BSA, 2% sucrose, 0.25% Tween 20 and dried in a convection oven at 37 °C for 2 hours. 2 μl of 70 μg/mL Anti-NTx-HRP were diluted in 18 μL of PBS and these 20 μL were pipet onto the conjugate pad and dried in a convection oven at 37 °C for 1 hour. The conjugate pads were used immediately or stored in a sealed microcentrifuge tube at room temperature. For the lateral flow fluid delivery experiment, 100 μL PBS was introduced and let flow for 10 min. It was then dried at 42 °C for 1 hour. Next, 100 μL of 3,3’5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) solution was placed in the sample inlet and allowed to flow for 15 minutes. Finally, the cassette was disassembled and the TMB colorimetric product was observed on the waste pad.

c) Validate sensor performance
The sensor was designed as a competitive assay. Briefly, AuNP-NTx on the WE surface served as a recognition element. If NTx is present from the specimen, it binds to the Anti-NTx-MB from the conjugate pad and thus eliminates the Anti-NTx-MB ability to bind to the NTx on the AuNP-NTx on the WE. To optimize the assay and demonstrate electrochemical readout, incubation steps were first performed without the nitrocellulose fluidics. 20 μL of Anti-NTx-MB (128 μg/mL) with various amounts of NTx specimen (0 to 100 nM) and allowed to incubate 30 minutes. The resulting mixture was incubated for 30 min on the AuNP-NTx WE surface. The sensor was washed with PBS and electrochemical measurements were performed in K4Fe(CN)6/K3Fe(CN)6 in PBS. Electrochemical measurements of cyclic voltammetry, differential pulse voltammetry and electrochemical impedance were acquired using an EmSTAT Pico Development Kit. Cyclic voltammograms were performed sweeping potentials of -0.2 to 0.6 V at 50 mV/sec. Cathodic and Anodic peak heights and peak-to-peak separation were recorded. Differential pulse voltammetry was performed from -0.2 to 0.6 V with step size of 0.005 V. Electrochemical impedance spectra were acquired from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. Resulting Nyquist plots were analyzed by NOVA software and charge transfer resistance (RCT) were recorded.

Aim 3: Integrate sensor with urine collection device and handheld potentiostat hardware.
d) Package the sensor
The printed sensor was packaged in a lateral flow cassette with lateral flow materials. The lateral flow cassette was cut to allow the electrical contact pads of the electrochemical sensor to extend outside of the lateral flow device. An EmSTAT Pico Development Kit was used as the potentiostat to drive and read the sensor. The electrical contact pads of the packaged sensor were interfaced with the EmSTAT Pico by a screen printed electrode (SPE) adapter/connector for simple and reproducible measurement setup. Lastly, the lateral flow design of the packaged sensor was chosen to easily interface with the urine collection vessel. The urine collection vessel has a spigot at the bottom, used to evacuate the vessel. The lateral flow sensor can be placed in direct contact with the spigot of the urine collection vessel to deliver 1 drop of urine to the inlet port of the lateral flow sensor.


RESULTS:
The NTx printed sensor strip was successfully prototyped using an In-Space Manufacturing approach. A combination of CNT and AuNP inks were used to improve the sensors electrochemical response and provide a simple method of manufacturing the telopeptide selective ink. Sensor manufacturing proved to be very reproducible with sensor devices achieving a suitable variance for real testing applications. The lateral flow fluid delivery provided a simple method of delivering liquid specimens to the sensor surface without the need for more complex microfluidics and/or crew provided laboratory operations. The packaged sensor design provided a reliable interface between the urine collection device and handheld potentiostat reader. Lastly, detection of NTx was accomplished in low concentrations, down to 0.1 nM NTx, for a sensitive measurement.
