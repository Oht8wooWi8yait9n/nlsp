Countermeasures to Reduce Sensorimotor Impairment and Space Motion Sickness Resulting from Altered Gravity Levels

Record ID: NCC958SA03401
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The effect of altered gravity on astronauts' perceptions and motor skills is significant as it threatens the health, well-being, and performance of crews. Astronauts experience gravitational transitions during launch from Earth's gravitational level to microgravity in space, then to partial gravity if landing on the Moon, Mars, or Martian moons, followed by a return to microgravity, and finally re-entry back to Earth. In addition, the use of Artificial Gravity (AG) from an on-board centrifuge also presents an altered gravity challenge, in particular during transitions between gravity levels. During each of these g-transitions astronauts must adapt their sensorimotor programs to coordinate perceptual and motor capabilities and function successfully and safely. The ability to identify and predict individual differences in this adaptability is essential to the development of pharmacological and training interventions for future crewmembers. This study took a new approach which could lead to an effective, practical, and acceptable protocol for pre-adapting astronauts to space flight. By using the gravito-inertial alterations possible with centrifugation in different body orientations investigators quantified an individual's sensory adaptation capability using measures of sensorimotor impairment and motion sickness under altered gravity.

This study had the following specific aims:

  1. Demonstrate that individual differences exist in the ability to adapt to gravitational transitions, and can be measured quantitatively by measures of subjective orientation, closed loop manual control, and subjective motion sickness reports.
  2. Test whether pre-training by adapting to one altered gravity environment can improve sensorimotor adaptation in another altered gravity environment.
  3. Test whether the leading pharmacological agent, promethazine, affects either basic vestibular perceptual function or the adaptation rate to an altered gravity environment and the associated motion sickness symptoms.
  4. Develop and test a combined pre-adaptation training and pharmacological intervention protocol that can both improve sensorimotor adaptation and reduce the associate motion sickness.

APPROACH:
During phase 1 of the experiment investigators tested whether an individual's adaptation rate in one altered gravity environment could be predicted by an individual's adaptation in a different gravity environment. For this purpose they tested if an individual's adaptation rate in -1.5 Gz (headward centrifugal force) would predict their adaptation rate in +1.5 Gz (footward centrifugal force). Since they expect adaptation to one environment to temporarily influence an individual's ability to adapt to other environments, they separated these two adaptation conditions by at least six months.

During phase 2 investigators tested the hypothesis that pre-training by adapting to one altered gravity (-1.5 Gz) will temporarily enhance an individual's ability to adapt to another altered gravity environment (+1.5 Gz). For this purpose they separated the pre-training and testing sessions by one week.

During phase 3 investigators tested the impact that promethazine has on basic vestibular function using perceptual thresholds, tilt perception, and manual control measures.

During phase 4, investigators tested whether promethazine influenced adaptation to altered gravity. Specifically, they studied adaptation to a +1.5 Gz environment with and without promethazine application. They hypothesized that motion sickness would be reduced with promethazine, but that adaptation rate will be unaffected.

Finally in phase 5, investigators combined promethazine use with the pre-training countermeasure. They hypothesized that the combined intervention will result in reduced motion sickness and improved sensorimotor adaptation during adaptation to an altered gravity environment (+1.5 Gz).

RESULTS:
The investigators completed analysis of a double-blind, within-subject study to compare vestibular perceptual thresholds with the administration of promethazine and a placebo. Roll tilt thresholds were found to be 31% higher after ingestion of promethazine. They believe that these findings are an important step in understanding implications of motion sickness drug administration during critical and demanding mission phases.

Using a short radius centrifuge, investigators created a land-based hypo-gravity analog test paradigm. They developed the test protocol, conducted pilot testing, and tested 10 subjects in their altered-gravity perception test protocol. Analysis revealed that subjects underestimate their roll tilts when put into hypo-gravity compared to their baseline 1 G perception. After approximately 45 minutes in the hypo-gravity environment, subjects' motion perception returned to their 1 G baseline showing that subjects were able to adapt to the altered-gravity environment.

Investigators also developed, pilot tested, and conducted a full manual control experiment using a short radius centrifuge and a human in the loop feedback control system. They found that both the root-mean-square deviation (RMSE) and variability in the nulled chair position increased when subjects transitioned into the hypo-gravity environment, representing a worsening in the ability to perceive and null out passive roll tilt motions.

Finally, investigators collected additional motion threshold data from subjects who were involved in their centrifugation studies. With this additional data, they were able to make statistical comparisons between basic vestibular function as estimated by motion thresholds and functional control in altered-gravity environments, as described by various metrics of manual control ability. Investigators found a positive, linear correlation between manual control variability and vestibular thresholds in the 1.0 GZ baseline condition. This suggests that sensory precision is a limiting factor in manual control performance. Additionally, manual control performance was 12.7% lower in 1.33 GZ and 37.5% higher in 0.5 GZ, as compared to 1 GZ.
