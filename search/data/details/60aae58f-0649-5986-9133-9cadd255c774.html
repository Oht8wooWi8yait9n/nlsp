Dosimetric Mapping

Record ID: 96-E094
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Radiation constitutes one of the most important hazards for humans in long-duration space missions. Leaving the earth's surface, humans are exposed to a wide spectrum of particles and energies of galactic and solar origin. The intensity, energy, and elemental composition of the radiation field changes considerably with orbital parameters, shielding thickness and solar activity. Results from numerous space probes demonstrate heightened radiation levels compared to the earth's surface and a change in the nature of the radiation field, particularly the heavy ion component.

Although considerable data has been accumulated from several spaceflights, it is not yet possible to provide a quantitative description of the radiation field, especially at the edge of the radiation belt, because of its highly complex composition regarding the particles present and their energy spectra. Data comparison is hampered by uncertain angular distribution of the single radiation components, by missing knowledge on the angular response of instruments and missing information about shielding distributions at the instrument locations. Current models either under- or overestimate the contribution of different radiation components.

Due to the variety of particles and energies present in Low Earth Orbit (LEO), no single detector system is capable of providing sufficient information. Therefore, the DOSMAP (Dosimetric Mapping) dosimetry package consisted of several types of detectors to allow for measurement of different particle types and energies. The DOSMAP dosimetry package was further supplemented with instruments contributed by NASA (Phantom Torso) and NASDA (Bonner Ball). The different systems allowed for the first time inflight cross-calibration of measurements at the same shielding configurations. This is absolute necessary when recognizing that the comparison of currently available data is often not possible due to not well known angular response of the detector systems combined with unknown angular incidence of radiations and only approximate knowledge of shielding distributions.

APPROACH:
Several components of the DOSMAP dosimetry package (dosimetry telescopes [DOSTELS], computer interface unit [CIU], and the thermoluminescent dosimeter [TLD] reader and bulbs) were brought to the International Space Station (ISS) aboard A Shuttle flight. These components were unstowed and set-up in the Retention Net of the Human Research Facility (HRF). Another shuttle brought the remaining DOSMAP hardware (mobile dosimetry units [MDUs] and nuclear track detector packages [NTDP] to the ISS. The MDUs were charged, and an initial read-out was performed for the MDUs and the TLD bulbs prior to their installation throughout the U.S. Lab and Node 1. The NTDPs were also placed at the required locations. All placements were photo documented.

Data collected from the TLD bulbs by the TLD Reader were downloaded to the HRF PC every 7-10 days, and data collected from the MDUs were downloaded through the CIU to the HRF PC every 5 days. During the same time intervals the MDUs were recharged. The DOSTELs were activated and data downloads to the HRF PC were performed every 10 days. All data of the active instruments were downlinked during the mission. All of the dosimetry hardware was returned to Earth on A shuttle flight.

RESULTS:
All data received by the investigators was of good quality. Several hardware issues resulted in small amounts of data loss. Due to the retirement of Prof. W. Heinrich the evaluation of the CR39 NTDP detectors by the group of the University of Siegen could not be completed. The science return may be roughly quoted at about 95%.

TLD Results:

Based on the characterization of the different detector systems, the following can be concluded. The data for all the different applied TLD systems agree very well taking into account the different Linear Energy Transfer (LET) efficiency of the detector types used as well as the different neutron sensitivity of 6Li doped and 7Li doped TLD materials. The higher dose values for TLD600 and TLD700 dosimeters are due to the higher LET efficiency of these dosimeter materials compared to Cu, P doped LiF crystals.

Neutron Dose Results:

The determination of neutron dose equivalent using chiefly recoil protons can give too high of a dose value depending on the amount of primary protons contributing to the signal. For this reason, two methods of neutron dose equivalent determination were used. However, both methods still have their limitations and uncertainties. On the other hand, the neutron dose values determined by the two different methods are almost comparable within the standard deviations determined by uncertainty propagation, which indicates that the assumptions made are not too wrong.

Measurements with converters with even higher sensitivity to thermal neutrons may improve the standard deviation and better spectral knowledge of the neutron spectra in space may improve the uncertainty of the calibration factor for Albedo dosimeters.

Passive Dosimetry System (PDS) Results:

The use of the Pille reader in automatic mode permitted dose to be measured as a function of orbit number by TLD A0101. Orbits that passed through the SAA received doses of 30 to >50 µGy/orbit. During these particular orbits, the spacecraft received a substantially larger exposure to trapped protons than during orbits that did not pass through the SAA. For orbits that did not traverse the SAA, the absorbed dose was ~10 µGy/orbit. All orbits that passed through the SAA do not receive the same dose because each orbit passed through only a fraction of the SAA and the size of this fraction changed as the ISS orbit precessed around the Earth. Generally, the ISS passed through the SAA twice during each twenty-four hour period.

Following exposure and return to the laboratory, each PDS detector was disassembled and the individual CR-39 PNTD layers were selected for chemical processing (etching). The combined LET spectrum for the x-, y- and z-oriented short-etch CR-39 PNTDs was generated following readout of the 3 detectors. Similarly, the combined LET spectrum was generated for the x-, y- and z-oriented long-etch detectors. There is fairly close agreement between the three mutually orthogonal spectra measured in the long-etch detectors. Similarly, for LET <100 keV/µm, there is fairly close agreement between the 3 mutually orthogonal detectors measured in the short-etch detectors. Only above 100 keV/µm is there a difference in the 3 spectra, where the curve measured in the short-etch x-oriented detector lies significantly above those measured in the short-etch y and z detectors. This is likely due to greater shielding in the x direction relative to the other two directions.

The relative contribution to the LET spectrum from neutron- and proton-induced target fragments tend to increase with increasing LET. The reduced efficiency with which tracks are detected in the short-etch detectors below ~30 keV/µm is apparent, as is the increased signal due to target fragmentation in the LET region above 100 keV/µm in the short-etch detectors. There is fairly close agreement between the detectors for the 2 etch regimes in the LET interval between 30 and 100 keV/µm. The crossover point of the short-etch and long-etch spectra, 35 keV/µm for PDS A0103, was located in the plot differential LET fluence spectra and used in combining the short-etch and long-etch curves into one total LET spectrum.

During the DOSMAP experiment, mean total dose rate measured in the PDS dosimeters ranged from 6.88 ± 0.42 µGy/hr in Node 1, above the forward hatch leading to the FGB module, to 10.36 ± 0.54 µGy/hr above the aft hatch of Node 1. The mean total dose equivalent rate varied from 14.06 ± 0.93 µSv/hr to 18.81 ± 1.11 µSv/hr at these same respective locations. The average quality factor varied from 1.82 ± 0.13 above the aft hatch of Node 1 to 2.11 ± 0.16 on the starboard side of the US Lab near the aft hatch.

DOSTEL Results:

By chance a significant solar particle event occurred during the active phase of DOSTEL on April 15, 2001. Thanks to the action of the crew, DOSTEL 1 was switched into a special mode while DOSTEL 2 remained in the mode for article and dose rate measurement. In the special mode, the 2 detector signals of DOSTEL 1 were stored for each individual particle. Stopping protons impinging from the top and bottom of the telescope were clearly seen, along with some stopping He-particles that were also identified. A plot of dose rates during the mission clearly shows the dependence of dose rates from the altitude, which ranged from 404 km to 386 km. Based on the mean quality factor derived from the LET spectra by applying the LET dependence of the quality factor as defined in ICRP Report No. 60 equivalent doses can be calculated.

Mobile Dosimetry Unit (MDU) Results:

Approximately 4.5 MB of rough compressed information was received by each MDU. After decompression, the volume of data increases by an approximate factor of 30. The values of the mean doses per day was 180.7, 189.9, 166.1, 126.3 µGy/day in MDU#1 to MDU#4, respectively. When graphed these doses lie on the AP8MAX prediction line.

Comparison of fluxes by 4 different MDUs shows that large differences are produced by the differences in the position of MDU and shielding distributions for them. In the time span 27 June-4 July 2001 four MDUs were placed at fixed locations: one unit (MDU#4) in the ISS "Unity" Node-1 and three (MDU #1-3) units were located at different locations in the US Laboratory module.

MDU #2 results showed that the spectra inside the ISS has a maximum energy of 300 MeV and that the place of the maximum of the spectra moves from about 100 MeV at low L values toward 50-60 MeV at higher L values. The agreement of the data received by active and passive detectors is in the range of ±10%. Comparisons of the passive devices yield even a smaller range, when corrected by the efficiency function of the different materials. Dose equivalents were calculated from the measurements using the quality factor (Q) as defined in ICRP Report No. 60.

Conclusion:

In comparison to other spaceflight missions, the lower dose equivalents seen for ISS are due to less solar activity during the measurement period and due to higher shielding of the ISS. The dose equivalents received by the combination of nuclear track detectors with TLDs show values close to the DOSTEL dose equivalents. The error in the determination of the dose equivalent from passive detectors is about 6%. The combined CR39 NTDP/TLD dose equivalent appears too low compared to the DOSTEL dose equivalents. Since the low let measurements of the TLDs are in good agreement with DOSTEL and the CR39 NTDP LET spectra as well, further investigations are needed to close this gap of knowledge. Adding the neutron dose from the personal dosimeter would increase the dose equivalent measurements by about 33 µSv/day.

The DOSMAP experiment was the first effort to map dose and dose equivalent as function of the location in the US lab and in Node 1 of the ISS. Having a fully developed 3-D ray tracing model the obtained data can be used to validate the Model. A quite impressive set of data has been produced by the "Dosimetric Mapping" experiment from which only a part could be presented in this summary. Since the radiation field is both complex and dynamic, measurements across the ISS need to be continued in future missions to study the radiation field and its modification by mass shielding, leading to attenuation, build up and/or activation. There is a continuous need for information on absorbed dose, particle and linear energy transfer spectra in real time.
