Signaling at the Vestibular Calyx Synapse (Postdoctoral Fellowship)

Record ID: NCC958PF03004
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The vestibular system is responsible for our sense of balance. Precisely how mechanical signals generated by head movements are detected and transformed into electrical signals by hair cells and their afferent nerve fibers is poorly understood. Signals are altered during space flight, changing the perception of body orientation and movement. Astronauts experience dizziness and disorientation during the first few days in microgravity and again on return to Earth. Temporary disorientation on leaving and on returning suggests that there is plasticity in the system. Investigators aimed to better understand the transfer of information between hair cells and afferents as well as the mechanisms underlying plasticity. This is important in understanding alteration of vestibular perception in space. Mammalian vestibular epithelia can be divided into central and peripheral zones which differ in hair cell morphology, ribbon synapse number, afferent innervation, and firing pattern. Calyx-only afferents contact type I hair cells in central regions, whereas dimorphic afferents form bouton and calyx endings and supply peripheral and central hair cells. Action potential firing is irregular in calyx-only afferents and varies from regular to irregular in dimorphic afferents. The investigators were interested in how mechanisms on either side of the type I hair cell/calyx synapse shape transmission in central and peripheral zones. The first aim was to use whole cell patch clamp to record postsynaptic responses from semicircular canal calyx afferents while their second aim was to investigate efferent modulation of calyx responses.

APPROACH:
To elucidate the roles of ion channels in the processing of signals in the vestibular periphery investigators developed slice preparations of rodent crista and utricle which allowed electrophysiological comparisons between central zones (CZ) and peripheral zones (PZ).

RESULTS:
To study the hypothesis that zonal variations in calyceal ion channels play a role in shaping postsynaptic events, the investigators developed a slice preparation of the gerbil crista to record spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents, action potentials, and voltage-dependent conductances from CZ and PZ calyx terminals. Spontaneous firing was regular or irregular and frequency ranged from 1.3 to 63 Hz. Firing persisted when transduction channels in hair cell stereocilia were blocked by streptomycin and when postsynaptic receptors were blocked by NBQX, suggesting that calyceal ion channels play a role in generating action potentials. The investigators further hypothesized that zonal variations in ion channel expression underlie the variation in inactivation of potassium currents previously seen in isolated calyces. They discovered that inactivation, expressed as percent reduction of peak current, had a median value of 9.4% in PZ calyces, which was significantly different from the value of 4.2% in CZ calyces. The greater inactivation of potassium currents in PZ calyces could contribute to tonic activity of regular afferents, which may be better suited to signaling long duration stimuli.

The investigator team studied the contribution of diverse potassium channels to whole cell outward current. XE991 dihydrochloride (20 µM), a KCNQ channel antagonist, reduced outward currents in both CZ and PZ calyces. XE991 also reduced the frequency of spontaneous action potentials. 4-aminopyridine (10-50 uM) blocked rapidly activating and moderately inactivating currents more prevalent in PZ calyces. Dendrotoxins, which block Kv1.1, 1.2, and 1.6 channels, reduced a non-inactivating current in CZ calyces and dampened excitability. Dendrotoxin-sensitive currents may allow detection of high-frequency signals and may therefore be important in generating rapid vestibular-driven reflexes. Margatoxin, a Kv1.3 and 1.6 channel antagonist, blocked a slowly inactivating current more prevalent in PZ calyces.
