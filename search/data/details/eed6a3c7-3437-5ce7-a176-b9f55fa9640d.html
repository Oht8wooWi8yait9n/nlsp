Optical Coherence Tomography Analysis of the Optic Nerve Head and Surrounding Structures in Long Duration ISS Crew Members

Record ID: SD_OCT
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The primary aim of this study is to compare morphological ocular parameters taken from a database of International Space Station (ISS) long-duration crewmembers versus a control population of normal healthy subjects. A secondary aim is to perform a pre- and postflight comparison of those parameters for the crewmember group. Retrospective data analysis of non-attributable OCT data from both populations will compare measurements of optic discometry (disc size, elevation, cup and volume of the nerve), retinal thickness, retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (total and at specific points), choroidal thickness (peripapillary and sub-macular) and retinal pigmented epithelium angle. When a pre- or postflight Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) score is available for the crewmember data, it will be used to look for any correlations that might exist between OCT data and a manifestation of signs and symptoms of the Visual Impairment/Intracranial Pressure (VIIP) syndrome. Over the last several years, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s (NASA) Space Medicine Division has documented astronauts presenting with varying degrees of disc edema (papilledema), globe flattening, choroidal folds, and hyperopic shifts after long-duration space flight. The similarities of the setting and clinical features of these patients suggests a common etiology inherent to the microgravity environment. These findings may represent manifestations of a pathologic process related to ocular, optic nerve (ON), or perhaps intracranial cephalad fluid shifts experienced during microgravity exposure. Since the first NASA Papilledema Summit and through all of the proceeding Visual Impairment/Increased Intracranial Pressure (VIIP) summits, many experts from around the world have come together to try and define this VIIP Syndrome. Spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) has become one of the most sensitive clinical procedures in assessing ISS crew determined to present with VIIP syndrome. With 5 microns of axial resolution, OCT allows the assessment of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), the neural retinal rim (NRR) and integrity of the retinal layers in the posterior 30 degrees of eye. However, little is known about the changes in the presentation of the structures around the optic nerve and the vascular supply layer (the choroid) of the eye in the affected ISS crew. By analyzing these parameters in healthy non-astronaut controls to post-flight long-duration US ISS crewmembers, biometric parameters can be developed to better understand VIIP syndrome. By analyzing these parameters pre- versus postflight within the astronaut group, and by coupling this information with the Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPG) scores when those scores are available, investigators can better understand the role that the space flight environment plays in the onset and development of the VIIP syndrome. The primary aim of this proposal is to compare morphological ocular outcomes measured with spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) collected post-flight from U.S. long-duration ISS astronauts with similar measures taken from a cohort of ground controls that are available to the research team in an existing database.

The secondary aim is to perform a pre- and postflight comparison of those parameters for the crewmember group. When a pre- or postflight Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) score is available for the crewmember data, it will be used to look for any correlations that might exist between OCT data and a manifestation of signs and symptoms of the Visual Impairment/Intracranial Pressure (VIIP) syndrome.

APPROACH:
A retrospective case-control design with n=40 healthy non-astronaut controls compared to approximately n=18 astronaut long-duration cases (pre-approved, non-attributable data from LSAHR). Data on 40 non-astronaut (healthy subject control) data was collected through the University of Houston - University Eye Institute. All data analysis will be conducted at the University of Houston. The U of H control data will be compared to non-attributable crewmember data (n=approximately 18) obtained retrospectively from LSAH-R.

RESULTS:
This study is currently in progress.
