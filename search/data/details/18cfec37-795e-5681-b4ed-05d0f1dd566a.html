Morphological, Histochemical and Ultrastructural Characteristics of Skeletal Muscle

Record ID: 4.1.2
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
When crewmembers make the transition from Earth's gravity to the weightlessness of space, they have to adapt to moving around in space and be able to determine their orientation as they move. Normal activities on Earth require the brain to interpret and integrate information coming from all the body's senses. This information is used to give feedback to the body on how the limbs are positioned, how the entire body is oriented, and which muscles need to be moved if reorientation or repositioning is desired.

Loss of muscle mass and strength in the lower extremities was noted as a consequence of exposure to microgravity in reports from Gemini, Apollo, and Skylab programs. Indirect evidence of increased muscle catabolism in humans during space flight has included elevated urinary amino acids, nitrogen, phosphorus and negative potassium balance. At the gross level, decreased circumferential size of the lower extremities has been recorded in American astronauts and Soviet cosmonauts.

Preflight and inflight countermeasures have not been completely effective in ameliorating the loss of muscle tissue and development of associated muscle weakness in all crewmembers. While it appears to date that the decrease in muscular function has not compromised the performance of astronauts except possibly during extravehicular activity, longer duration exposure to microgravity including extended (>14 day) Shuttle missions, Space Station habitation, and manned interplanetary flights are anticipated to induce significant muscle atrophy. This will require the development and implementation of appropriate and effective countermeasures to prevent or ameliorate muscle atrophy in order to assure crew health and safety.

Atrophy of skeletal muscle is a response that is considered common to conditions, which result in prolonged periods of markedly decreased muscular activity or physical unloading. Unweighting occurs during periods of chronic bed rest and prolonged exposure to the microgravity environment of space travel or habitation. The loss of skeletal muscle mass and the associated amelioration in function that occurs during space travel represents a multi-factorial conditional adaptation with contributions from peripheral proprioceptive and central nervous system pathways. When microgravity-associated adaptation of skeletal muscle results in functional deficits that are manifested upon return to a unit gravity environment, the muscle is considered to have undergone deconditioning or deadaptation with respect to that environment.

The unloading of skeletal muscles (normally utilized to resist the forces of terrestrial gravity) which accompanies space flight has been documented to result in varying degrees of muscle atrophy. This study examined the development of muscle atrophy in human space flight crewmembers that completed a 115-day space flight. The following measurements were made:

(1) Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessment of muscle volume in selected muscles and muscle groups
(2) serum markers of muscle damage (creatine phosphokinase and myoglobin)
(3) dry, lean body mass by hydrostatic densitometry

APPROACH:
MRI was performed on a Siemens 1.5 Tesla Magnetom either in Houston, Texas, at the Texas Medical Center/Methodist Hospital or in Moscow, Russia at the Cardiology Research Center. Computer software was used to produce preflight- and postflight-acquired 2-D images that were volume representations of the muscles and intervertebral discs. Phantoms were used judiciously to allow for corrections in pixel sizes between measurement sessions at both locations. In order to minimize fluid shifts caused by assumption of a recumbent position after being upright, the subject was required to lie down for 6-10 minutes before the start of data acquisition, and the order of measurements was consistent during each session. The feet were held static by strapping them to a Lean holder during image acquisition. The portion of the lower limb under analysis was suspended by placing supporting materials on either side of the imaged area. This allows the anatomical area of interest to assume a natural and reproducible shape rather than being distorted by its own weight. A radio frequency (RF) shield was positioned caudally to the imaged portions of the leg. For the thigh and calf muscles, thirty-two one-millimeter slice images were obtained using a Te=10 milliseconds, Tr=0.8 seconds and a 256 x 512 matrix. For the back muscles, a similar protocol was used, except twenty 0.5-centimeter image slices were obtained. For images of the cervical neck muscles, a Helmholtz neck coil was used in place of the spine coil.

Biochemical assessment of muscle dysfunction and damage were obtained before, during and after flight by venous blood samples taken by standard venipuncture technique or via indwelling venous catheters. Total creatine phosphokinase activity was determined on a Beckman CX5 clinical chemistry instrument using a commercially available assay based upon the conversion of NADP to NADPH. Myoglobin concentrations in venous serum samples were determined with a commercially available system (OPUS) utilizing a 2 site or "sandwich" fluorogenic enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Preflight and postflight comparison of dry lean mass was done using a 3-component model of body composition:

1) total body water (TBW)
2) lean body mass (hydrostatic densitometry)
3) fat mass (hydrostatic densitometry)

Hydrostatic weighing was performed in a water tank and weight measurements were made during maximal pulmonary exhalation; lung residual volume was determined by a standard oxygen dilution technique. TBW values determined by the doubly labeled water (DLW) technique were obtained from the Fluid and Electrolyte experiment on Mir 18 and details of the assay can be found in that report.

An exercise log recorded the type and amount of exercise performed 120 days before, during, and 30 days after flight, as well as exercise performance data, and any other countermeasures that were used (required for correlation with morphological and biochemical studies).

RESULTS:
Preflight measurements originally scheduled for the L-60 (60 days before launch) to L-45 timeframe were delayed until L-30. This actually improves the value of the science since the analyses were performed closer to the launch date. Postflight measurements were not performed until R+2 (2 days after landing) or R+3. Although measurements taken as close to R+0 are optimal, this should have little effect on the value of the science. It was not possible to schedule both R+0 and R+14 postflight hydrostatic weighing sessions because of intense postflight science and crew rehabilitation activities. It was elected to schedule one session between the two requested postflight days. Subject 1 declined to participate in the hydrostatic weighing session and the data collection from Subject 2 was suboptimal. Subject 3 did complete the session. Since Subject 1 declined to participate, this portion of the science was extremely compromised and it was elected not to conduct further sessions on other crewmembers. Only a partial data set for Subject 3 was obtained and that was analyzed for comparison to muscle losses demonstrated by MRI. Additionally, Subjects 1 and 2 both declined to participate in the recovery MRI studies at R+56. This was extremely unfortunate as little is known about the recovery rate of muscle loss after long-duration space flight.

With the exception of the above stated preflight and postflight anomalies, all data collection was completed. Inflight blood samples for creatine phosphokinase and myoglobin were originally withdrawn from the protocol because of concern about total inflight blood volumes. It was possible to perform these analyses on serum remaining after all other blood analyses were completed for other experiments. These data compliment the preflight and postflight blood data.

Averages of two preflight measurements were compared to postflight measurements made 2 to 3 days after landing. Significance of comparisons was determined with student's t-test, p<0.05. Summation data for the 3 crewmembers showed that the mean percent change from preflight values for skeletal muscles and muscle groups as calculated from raw data are as follows:

  • Anterolateral compartment -15+3

  • Gastrocnemius -18+1

  • Soleus -18+4

  • Quadriceps -11+3

  • Intrinsic lower back -18+4

  • Psoas -7+4 (not significant)

    Venous blood data did not support the presence of significant ongoing muscle damage in the inflight and immediate postflight period.

    With data from only three subjects, it is perilous to draw more than preliminary conclusions. However, the collective data reasonably supports loss of muscle mass during long-duration space flight even though participation in a highly structured inflight exercise protocol was a crew requirement. In this particular flight, the amount of exercise that was completed by each crewmember evidently was quite variable but the muscle losses in all three crewmembers were similar. Certainly, one of the most important conclusions that can be drawn is that the loss of muscle mass during microgravity exposure does not appear to be purely a linear response with respect to time. Short flights (5-11 days) have resulted in up to a 10% loss in some muscles, while the amount of loss nearly doubled that in this 115-day flight. The precise impact of the exercise program on countering muscle atrophy is not known as no studies of long-duration space missions have been performed on crewmembers that have not exercised during their period of microgravity exposure.

    Venous blood samples collected at various periods during flight and analyzed for creative phosphokinase activity and myoglobin do not support muscle damage during flight as a contributing factor to loss of muscle volume and function.

    Body composition data obtained in one crewmember showed an approximate 20% reduction in dry lean mass. This correlated well with the volume losses measured in the muscles of the back and legs which collectively contribute the bulk of muscle mass and thus dry, lean weight.

    It was suspected that measurements of neck muscle volume would reveal a loss of muscle volume, particularly in the posterior cervical extensors, since head bobbing has been observed in motion analysis studies of crewmembers upon return and weakness of neck muscles has been described and measured. It appears that since the neck muscles are small by comparison to the other measured muscles, the sensitivity of volume measurements for these muscles may not be sufficiently sensitive and the reproducibility of measuring such small volume (50-60 cc) muscles is poor. Thus, although it was not possible to show loss of cervical neck muscle volume, there is still good reason to suspect that atrophy of these muscle groups occurs. Alternative protocols and perhaps methods may be required to make measurements that are sufficiently precise to allow statistically significant comparisons.
