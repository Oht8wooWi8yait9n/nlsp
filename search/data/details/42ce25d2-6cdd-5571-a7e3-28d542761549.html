Microgravity May Detrimentally Influence Lipid Metabolism

Record ID: ARC00XX185
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

The spaceflight environment impacts many physiological systems, resulting in potentially serious consequences, particularly for longer duration space exploration. As use of the International Space Station (ISS) is increased, and with the rise of commercial spaceflight and tourism, the systemic effects of microgravity must be carefully investigated to protect human health. Although most research has focused on bone, muscle, brain and cardiovascular function, several studies have shown that exposure to the space environment alters both energy and lipid metabolism in humans and rodents. In astronauts, a diabetogenic phenotype was induced, the severity of which was linked with flight duration, suggesting the liver may be a target of spaceflight-induced deficits. Indeed, significant spaceflight-induced changes in hepatic genes linked to oxidative defense have been previously observed. The liver is a major metabolic organ and the most common chronic disease worldwide affecting the liver is nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). NAFLD describes a spectrum of liver abnormalities, ranging from hepatic steatosis, or fat droplet accumulation, to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), with inflammation and fibrosis causing irreversible tissue damage in many cases. The development of NAFLD is still poorly understood; however, several insults (“hits”) are thought to be jointly responsible for causing progressive liver injury. The “classical” hit typically involves excessive triglyceride accumulation in the hepatocytes and resultant dysfunction due to subsequent hits from oxidative stress or lipotoxicity inducing secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines that activate hepatic stellate cells. Our hypothesis is that exposure to the space environment affects hepatic lipid metabolism, leading to fat accumulation and initiation of pro-fibrotic signaling in livers of spaceflight mice. The goal of this study was to use global transcriptomics and untargeted metabolomics, as well as advanced Raman microscopy techniques, to more comprehensively characterize molecular and functional changes in the liver, some of which may represent early signs of NAFLD, which result from spaceflight. Our results may elucidate potential therapeutic targets for countermeasure development to protect astronaut health during long-duration space exploration.

APPROACH:

To test our hypothesis, we propose to carry out the following Specific Aims using liver tissue from 6 STS-135 placebo mice and 6 matched AEM controls fed the same diet. 1. Determine whether spaceflight mice have increased hepatic fat as compared with AEM controls. a. Used Oil Red O staining and Coherent anti-Stokes Raman spectroscopy. 2. Acquire lipid profiles from fat droplets in spaceflight and AEM livers and identify differences. Determine whether these differences correspond with a nascent pro-fibrotic phenotype. a. Performed untargeted mass spectrometry-based metabolomics and identified changes in lipid profiles. b. Performed Raman spectroscopy on individual lipid droplets and identified changes in spectra between groups. c. Performed immunohistochemistry, and analyzed fixed tissues histologically and using Second Harmonic Generation imaging to assess for the presence of collagen. d. Mined transcriptomics data to determine whether expression of pro-fibrotic genes correlated with mass spectrometry and microscopy results. 3. Using global transcriptomics and untargeted metabolomics, perform pathway analysis to elucidate signaling pathways that are altered by spaceflight and contribute to the observed phenotype. a. Performed data integration to generate a gene subset corresponding to upregulated metabolites and performed pathway analysis.

These studies will be repeated with RR1 tissues.

RESULTS:

We observed significantly more fat accumulation in livers of spaceflight mice as compared with ground controls. We also found that retinol was lost in the spaceflight mice lipid droplets. Transcriptomics data suggested that the retinol loss corresponded to early signs of hepatic stellate cell activation and transition to pro-fibrotic myofibroblasts. Integration of transcriptomics and metabolomics data showed that acyl coA-thioesterase activity was increased and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-alpha signaling was activated, likely in response to the increased fat accretion. A manuscript describing these observations has been published in PlosOne (PMID:27097220).
