Effects of Age and Activity Level on Tibia Mass and Distribution

Record ID: ARCbedrest202
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The purpose of this study is to gain a better understanding of the relationship between age, activity level (runners vs non-runners) and site specific bone mass and long bone structure.

APPROACH:
Three studies were performed because nonbone soft issue in the path of the x-ray beam bone is comprised of materials that have different attenuation properties than mineralized bone. The first accuracy study involved the construction of a large plexiglass tube with a bladder filled with saline to emulate soft tissues for the leg. An aluminum phantom was used to estimate in vivo setup accuracy at 3 different angles and two energies by two sets of three scans. The second study determined the in vivo precision of cross-sectional structural parameters in the long bones of 15 female human non-runners on the same day repeated measures for 60o and 90o. The third study compared the parameters in the legs of 14 25-35 year old women with the same in 12 women >age 60 and at two activity levels (recreational runners and non-runners). Scans were taken of the middle third of the lower right leg; structural parameters for the tibia and fibula were determined on each scan line, averaged over the section and adjusted by factors accounting for body size variations.

RESULTS:
Study one: Parameters for the eight rectangular sections of the aluminum phantom were averaged over four lines per section and errors computed as differences from expected values. Principal moments were estimated within 2.3% and principal angles were estimated within 2.3o. Study two: Low energy yielded slightly better precision than high with the area measurement the highest precision (CV <1%). Principal and polar moment CV were 2-6% and the precision of the principal angle was 3-6o. Study three: Low loaders had higher body weights than high loaders, emphasizing the need to account for body size in estimates of bone structure. All differences in the tibia were related to load level, not age. Imax was higher by 46%, Imin by 30%, J by 41% and A by 27% and along the midshaft, the principle angle for runners was consistently aligned closer to the AP direction than in nonrunners. Fibula results did not show the same pattern of results as in the tibia. There was no dependence on age or load group in any parameter for the fibula. Conclusions: A three-scan densitometric method for measuring cross-sectional structural parameters in long bones in vivo was validated; accuracy and precision measurements established confidence limits. Structural parameters related to bone stiffness and strength are increased in female recreational runners compared with nonrunners and these increases are maintained with age. These measures that incorporate bone mass distribution all had larger percent differences than area (A) and were therefore more sensitive measures in detecting differences between runners and nonrunners than simple bone mass measurements (A) alone or I in a single, nonprincipal plane. From the activity study results, it is postulated that higher loads associated with running lead to increased cross-sectional parameters to support axial loads, bending and torsion in the tibia.
