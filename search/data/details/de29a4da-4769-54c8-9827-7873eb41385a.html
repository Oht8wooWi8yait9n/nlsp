An Approach to Counteract Impairment of Musculoskeletal Function in Space

Record ID: E409
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Impaired musculoskeletal function has been reported in astronauts upon their return to Earth. This effect may not be caused only by muscle atrophy; other factors related to neuromuscular control or contractile force of individual muscle fibers may contribute to the decrease in muscle strength as well. Previous measurements of maximal voluntary muscle strength provided important information on overall muscle function, but did not reflect muscle endurance capacity. The aim of this experiment is to investigate changes in fatigue of muscles over time by testing the quadriceps femoris muscle.

It was hypothesized that isometric, concentric and eccentric quadriceps strengths decrease during maximal effort following 17 days of space flight, which will be reflected in a decrease in maximal electromyographic (EMG) activity. It was also hypothesized that space flight would increase muscle fatigue, which should be noted as a greater increase in EMG activity over time during a sustained submaximal isometric action for a given force.

The specific objectives of this investigation were:
1. To study the effects of space flight on musculoskeletal functions by measuring maximal force and power output during voluntary leg press exercise.
2. To determine changes in the cross-sectional area (CSA) of thigh muscles.
3. To determine changes in the muscle activation pattern of the quadriceps femoris.

APPROACH:
An ergometer was specially designed for measurement of muscle activity. The ergometer uses the inertia of a fly-wheel to provide resistance during both shortening (concentric) and lengthening (eccentric) muscle actions. The test protocol incorporated strength measurements of isometric, concentric and eccentric muscle actions, assessing strength and velocity of different types of muscle actions. In addition, EMG activity from the knee extensor group was measured during maximal isometric action. The EMG data will provide information on stimulation of the muscles by the nervous system.

In a collaborative effort with experiment E029, the CSA of the knee extensor muscle group was measured before and after space flight by using Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). All measurements were taken before and after the 17-day space flight.

RESULTS:
Lower-limb FWE exercise can produce forces and thus muscular stress comparable to what is typical of advanced resistance training using free weights. FWE also offers greater training stimuli during eccentric relative to concentric muscle actions, as evidenced by force and electromyographic (EMG) measurements. Muscle use of specific muscle groups, as assessed by the exercise-induced contrast shift of magnetic resonance images, is similar during lower-limb FWE and the barbell squat. Unlike free-weight exercise, FWE allows for maximal voluntary effort in each repetition of an exercise bout. Likewise, FWE exercise, not unassisted free-weight exercise, produces eccentric "overload." Collectively, the inherent features of this resistive exercise device and the results of the physiological suggest that resistance exercise using FWE could be used as an effective exercise counter-measure in space. The flywheel principle can be employed to any exercise configuration and designed into a compact device allowing for exercises stressing those muscles and bone structures, which are thought to be most affected by long-duration space flight.
