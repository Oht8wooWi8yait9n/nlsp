Autonomic Neurophysiology in Microgravity

Record ID: 9301095
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The Autonomic Neurophysiology in Microgravity experiment was one of four autonomic nervous system experiments performed during the 1998 Neurolab mission. This experiment focused on how microgravity affects the overall function of the autonomic nervous system.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the part of the nervous system that supplies stimulation to the involuntary muscles, like the smooth and cardiac muscles, and to the glands. The autonomic nervous system is controlled principally by parts of the brain stem and the hypothalamus. For the first time in space flight history, direct measurement of muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) was made via microneurography. Microneurography is a procedure in which a small needle (the size of an acupuncture needle) is placed in an accessible nerve just below the knee. With the needle, the nerve signals traveling from the brain to the blood vessels can be measured directly.

The investigators were particularly interested in the effect of microgravity on the sympathetic limb of the baroreflex through spillover of norepinephrine (NE). Norepinephrine is a hormone produced by the adrenal glands and also secreted from nerve endings in the sympathetic nervous system as a chemical transmitter of nerve impulses. Also known as noradrenaline, many of its general actions are similar to those of adrenaline, but it mainly functions to maintain normal body activity, rather than to prepare the body for emergencies. This hormone is of interest because one suggested reason for change in the blood pressure control system is that it is underactive in space. The ANS releases chemical messengers (e.g., norepinephrine) when it operates. There has been some evidence from previous space flights that NE levels are lowered in space, indicating a low level of activity in blood pressure control. However, looking at NE levels alone can lead to false conclusions, since the blood level of a hormone depends not only on how much is released, but also on how fast it is removed from circulation. For that reason, on Neurolab, norepinephrine was measured as spillover rate to clue into ANS changes.

APPROACH:
To provide clear insight into how ANS is functioning, a microneurography needle was placed above the peroneal nerve just below the knee; the nerve signals traveling from the brain to blood vessels were measured. Subjects infused radioactively-labeled NE into the blood stream and collected blood samples to show how fast NE is released into and removed from circulation, thereby allowing investigators to assess whether blood press control system is really underactive. To monitor physiological changes, serum and urine catecholamines were collected. After cooling to +4 degrees Celsius, the tubes were centrifuged into separate blood components. Both the blood tubes and urine samples were stored at -22 degrees Celsius until landing. To obtain baseline data, these procedures were also performed before and after flight.

RESULTS:
All three scheduled sessions were performed by four crewmembers, one session early (Flight Day 2), the second session mid (Flight Days 7 and 8), and the third session late (Flight Days 12 and 13) in flight. Additional sessions were performed on Flight Days 2 and 8 by one subject on the early mission session. Data appear to be of good quality based on the initial evaluation.

In this study, the hypothesis that space flight impairs cerebral autoregulation was studied in six astronauts approximately 72 and 23 days before, after 1 and 2 weeks in space (n = 4), on landing day, and 1 day after the 16-day Neurolab space shuttle mission.

Beat-by-beat changes of photoplethysmographic mean arterial pressure and transcranial Doppler middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity were measured during 5 minutes of spontaneous breathing, 30 mmHg lower body suction to simulate standing in space, and 10 minutes of 60 degree passive upright tilt on Earth. Dynamic cerebral autoregulation was quantified by analysis of the transfer function between spontaneous changes of mean arterial pressure and cerebral artery blood flow velocity, in the very low- (0.02-0.07 Hz), low- (0.07-0.20 Hz) and high-frequency (0.20-0.35 Hz) ranges. Resting middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity did not change significantly from preflight values during or after space flight. Reductions of cerebral blood flow velocity during lower body suction were significant before space flight (P < 0.05, repeated measures ANOVA), but not during or after space flight. Absolute and percentage reductions of mean (+/- S.E.M.) cerebral blood flow velocity after 10 minutes upright tilt were smaller after than before space flight (absolute, -4 +/- 3 cm per sec after versus -14 +/- 3 cm per sec before, P = 0.001; and percentage, -8.0 +/- 4.8% after versus -24.8 +/- 4.4% before, P < 0.05), consistent with improved rather than impaired cerebral blood flow regulation. Low-frequency gain decreased significantly (P < 0.05) by 26, 23 and 27% after 1 and 2 weeks in space and on landing day, respectively, compared with preflight values, which is also consistent with improved autoregulation. It can be concluded that human cerebral autoregulation is preserved, and possibly even improved, by short-duration space flight.

Results indicate that: (1) reductions of cerebral blood flow velocity during lower body suction are similar before, during and after spaceflight; (2) reductions of cerebral blood flow velocity during upright tilt are smaller after than before space travel; and (3) dynamic fluctuations of cerebral blood flow velocity in response to changes of arterial pressure, as reflected by transfer function gain, phase and coherence, are significantly less in the low-frequency range during and after than before spaceflight. These results reject the hypothesis that exposure to microgravity impairs cerebral autoregulation and suggest rather that microgravity improves it.
