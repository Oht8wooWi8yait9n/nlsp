Crew Population Characteristics: Perspectives for Spacesuit Fit

Record ID: Crew_Population
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objective:

Interactions between the spacesuit and the body are crucial for crew performance, mobility, and injury, particularly during demanding jobs or dynamic impact events. One of the main objectives of spacesuit design is to create suits whose size and shape correspond to the population of crew members. It is nevertheless recognized that some population subgroups are not as well accommodated as others, such as petite females or people with uncommon body ratios. Therefore, a structural evaluation of the crew population's characteristics is necessary to mitigate issues with suit fit. By identifying the geometric aspects that may be able to alleviate fit concerns, this evaluation can also aid in the development of recommendations for new suit designs. This work hypothesizes that spacesuit-to-body contact is closely correlated with the suit fit of the crew population, as the contact patterns can more sensitively and accurately measure the physical constraints and subjective perception of the wearer.
The investigative team will accomplish this objective using the following specific aims:
1. Conduct suit-to-body contact pattern assessments with a focus on the hard upper torso assembly (HUT). 2. Build a probability model to predict specific HUT sizes likely to be chosen by a given crewmember as a function of the suit-to-body contact pattern. 3. Build a generative model for suit design that can yield the targeted level of fit. 4. Evaluate a crew-like population to assess the proportion of the population that can be accommodated from the available suit sizes and identify segments of the population that require additional accommodations for adequate suit fit.

Approach:

The PI team will focus on 4 criteria as they approach the hypothesis in this study. Researchers will examine the Shuttle extravehicular mobility unit (EMU) suit, paying particular attention to the hard upper torso assembly (HUT), which is crucial for crew movement and fit. The resulting suit-to-body contact patterns for each crew member or test subject who took part in EVA training will be calculated by superimposing their 3D body scan with the suit computer-aided design (CAD). The researchers will then use the data of previously selected suit sizes to train the model and validate it on human participants wearing 3D-printed replica suits that are all uniquely made to mimic variances in contact patterns. Subjective opinions and mobility data from various designs will be collected. These will be used to compare discernibility and individual tolerance to off-optimal fit. The model will be projected onto the crew-like population consisting of approximately 4,000 body scans that have been screened for the EVA Suit System Requirements from the US Army Survey. These data will be used to determine what percentage of the population can fit into the suit sizes that are available, and which demographic groups (body sizes or shapes) were not accommodated (too small or large in uncommon body ratios). The researchers will then test "what-if" scenarios, such as how the Shuttle EMU's small size HUT should have been designed and how much better crew population accommodations could have been achieved despite it not being built for the Shuttle. Moreover, the researchers will describe a "perfect world" scenario in which there are an ideal number of suit sizes and shapes that can accommodate every member of the crew.

Results:

The results of this study will improve the characterization of the body size and ratios of the astronaut crew and ultimately improve the ability of the crew to perform during EVAs as suit-to-body interactions are better understood and issues relating to fit can be mitigated. The research team will produce a report and peer-reviewed publications, a summarized suit fit prediction model, a suit development and validation methodology, assessment results, and case studies.
