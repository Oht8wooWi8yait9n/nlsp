Effects of Low Dose Radiation and Radiation Countermeasures on Infection by Spaceflight Analogue Cultured Salmonella using 3-D Biomimetic Human Tissue Models

Record ID: 80NSSC21K1024
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objectives
Microgravity and radiation are major biological stressors associated with the spaceflight environment. Their cumulative impact on host-pathogen interactions and infectious disease risks are rarely considered. Since the cumulative effects of these stressors during spaceflight may result in unexpected negative impacts on crew health and performance that neither condition alone would predict, the ability to develop effective countermeasures is limited. Spaceflight and spaceflight analog culture have shown increased virulence and pathogenesis-related characteristics of the foodborne pathogen, Salmonella Typhimurium. S. Typhimurium is responsible for disqualification of food destined for the International Space Station and has been found aboard NASA spacecraft. Spaceflight-analogue cultures of S. Typhimurium have been shown to possess increased ability to infect 3-D biomimetic human intestinal tissue models.
S. Typhimurium is a major cause of foodborne illness causing intestinal inflammation and diarrhea. It was selected as the model microorganism for this study due to its response to culture in both spaceflight and Rotating Wall Vessel (RWV) spaceflight analog environments and due it’s potential for causing disease in flight, eliciting an intense inflammatory response during infection of human cells. The primary objective of this proposal is to evaluate the possibility that low dose radiation will exacerbate the already increased bacterial pathogenicity of S. Typhimurium observed following spaceflight analog culture. The impact of a radiation countermeasure to provide protection against both radiation and pathogen-induced tissue damage and inflammation will also be determined in this study. This study will address the following aims:
1. Characterize the impact of spaceflight- analogue culture on the ability of S. Typhimurium to infect 3-D biomimetic intestinal tissue models before and after exposure to low dose radiation.
2. Evaluate the ability of the radioprotective compound, EC-18, to protect 3-D intestinal models from low dose radiation, S. Typhimurium infection, and the cumulative impact of these stressors. Microbial responses to spaceflight and spaceflight analog culture have been profiled for decades and there is extensive evidence from this collective body of work to suggest that bacterial pathogens are ‘’hardwired’’ to respond to the presence (or absence) of environmental stimuli under these conditions.

Approach
This study will employ the use of the NASA Rotating Wall Vessel which is a rotating bioreactor in which cells are maintained in suspension in a gentle fluid orbit, creating a sustained low fluid shear and low turbulence environment for cell growth. The bioreactor will be used as a spaceflight analog culture system for S.Typhimurium and will also be used to establish 3-D biomimetic intestinal tissue models. All studies will be done in triplicate. Virulent S. Typhimurium strain will grown in Lennox Broth (LB) as shaking overnight cultures at 37°C and then inoculated into fresh LB media, which will be grown in RWV at 37°C until late log phase under Low Shear Modeled Microgravity (LSMMG) and control orientations. 3-D cultures will be harvested from the RWV for use at 15-16 days. For all the studies, 3D models will be seeded evenly into multi-well plates for treatment. EC-18 will be provided as its active ingredient in a lipid-based liquid. Adherence, invasion and intracellular survival assays will be performed using a standard gentamicin exclusion assay. Percent colonization will be calculated by normalizing Colony Forming Units at each time point to initial bacterial inoculum for LSMMG and control cultures. Samples will be processed and read on a Bio-Plex 200.

Results

The PI team observed little-to-no differences in viability of the 3D co-culture model following exposure to X-ray radiation up to 2 Gy. These findings differ from earlier observations using the same models with exposure to gamma irradiation, which showed signs of significant cell damage.
Infection of the models using S. Typhimurium cultured under LSMMG and control orientations in the absence of X-ray radiation were significantly different, matching the previous findings showing that LSMMG-cultured S. Typhimurium invaded better than the 1xg cultures. Infection of the models following low dose radiation (0.1, 0.5 Gy) did not alter this trend significantly. EC-18 treatments under these conditions also did not show significant differences.
Overall, based on prior findings using gamma irradiation, the PI team believes that if these studies were repeated with a different type of radiation, there would likely be more of an impact to the viability of colonization phenotypes.
