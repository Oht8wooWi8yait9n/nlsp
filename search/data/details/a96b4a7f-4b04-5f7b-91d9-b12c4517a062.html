Neuro-Vestibular Examination During and Following Spaceflight

Record ID: Vestibular_Health
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objective:

During spaceflight, astronauts experience a wide variety of well-documented sensorimotor issues directly attributable to the effects of microgravity on the vestibular system. Two-thirds of astronauts are afflicted in orbit from slowed motor behavior, awkwardness in moving about the spacecraft, errors and imprecision when reaching for objects, visual orientation illusions, reduced visual acuity, gaze instability, disrupted visual tracking, and motion sickness. Oscillopsia is a visual disturbance where surrounding objects appear to be moving, shaking, jumping, jiggling, or vibrating. The combination of oscillopsia with head movements and unsteadiness of gait seen in astronauts returning from space missions is typical of pathology of the vestibular system on both sides. However, vertigo could also result from the alteration of one labyrinth only, from cupulotithiasis (where otoconia become dislodged from the utricle and adhere to the cupula, rendering it gravity sensitive), or be caused by head concussion, intense exercise, decompression sickness, anxiety, or simply aging. The investigators will perform a systematic otoneurological examination of crewmembers before launch, in orbit at regular intervals, and immediately after landing.
The specific aims for the Vestibular Health investigation include: (1) Examine the head position relative to the body and, if tilted, look for the presence of an ocular tilt reaction. (2) Determine the subjective non-visual vertical using the test of the finger. A deviation of the subjective vertical occurs in more than 90% of all acute unilateral peripheral and central vestibular disorders. (3) Examine the subject for nystagmus when the gaze is straight-ahead or directed right, left, up, and down. (4) Perform positioning maneuver to determine if there is a positioning nystagmus or positioning vertigo, and to differentiate between a BPPV (Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo) and a central positional or positioning nystagmus. (5) Examine three different types of eye movements (smooth pursuit, saccades, gaze-holding function) and positioning (e.g., skew for ocular misalignment) for central eye movement disorders such as saccadic smooth pursuit, gaze-evoked nystagmus, disorder of saccades, otolith asymmetry as well as impaired visual fixation suppression of the VOR (Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex). If patients with acute vertigo have central ocular motor disorders, this suggests a central origin. (6) Perform whole body rotation and whole-body translation to evaluate whether the subjective perception of motion by the semicircular canals and otoliths is accurate. (7) Comparison of otolith measures with posture and locomotion measures in Human Research Program Standard Measures (HRPSM).

Approach:

The researchers will perform a systemic neurovestibular examination of crewmembers in orbit at regular intervals, as well as immediately after landing to diagnose the conditions of vertigo/dizziness in astronauts during and after spaceflight. The number of inflight data collection sessions will depend on the length of the mission. The resulting data will be examined for temporal changes and to identify adaptive trends in vestibular health and performance. Recordings of eye, head, and body movements, as well as subjective perception of motion and verbal reports will be used for evaluating the presence of abnormal posture, eye movements, dysmetria, motion sickness symptoms, and illusions of self-motion during head or body movements. Standard medical and clinical tests will be used to identify the following vertigo syndromes: labyrinthine disorders, central disorders, positional vertigo (cupulotithiasis), traumatic vertigo, visual vertigo, and psychogenic vertigo. Depending on the etiology of the vestibular syndrome revealed, countermeasures will be proposed based on vestibular rehabilitation therapy currently used in patients with vestibular disorders. Before and after the flight, the otoneurological examination will be completed by Computerized Dynamic Posturography (CDP) that allows measurement of body sway under different conditions, e.g., with eyes open or closed, while standing on stable ground or when the foot support accompanies body sway. These sensory organization tests are designed to examine how well the patient uses information from the vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive systems to control balance. Sit-to-stand, tandem walk, and recovery from fall functional tests will be implemented in the Sensorimotor Standard Measures that will be performed before and after the short-, medium, and long-duration missions. The information obtained from these functional tests will be used to further determine the implication of vestibular system dysfunction in altered functional performance associated with spaceflight. During the preflight Ocular Alignment segment, a tablet-computer display will be presented to subjects with red and blue lines. The subjects will also be wearing red and blue spectacles, so that each eye sees one of the lines. The subject is told to align the lines such that they show up parallel for torsional offsets or adjacent for vertical offsets. This offers a subjective assessment of torsional and vertical skew, a sign of otolith asymmetry. Investigators call the entire set of tools and processes Sensorimotor Assessment and Rehabilitation Apparatus (SARA), and these particular tests Vertical Alignment Nulling (VAN) and Torsional Alignment Nulling (TAN). Vertical and torsional skew will also be assessed in the above inflight and postflight eye tracking tests.

Results:

The goal of this study is to use well-established clinical and experimental procedures to assess astronaut’s vestibular health. Data obtained from this study will allow the construction of a full temporal profile describing changes in astronauts’ vestibular system responses, functional performance, and sensorimotor function. This includes characterization of temporal trends in central compensation for vestibular (otolith) asymmetry. The results from this study will be used to assess the risks associated with future long-duration deep space missions. It can help to identify any such association, by correlating investigators alignment tests with concurrent sensorimotor testing.
