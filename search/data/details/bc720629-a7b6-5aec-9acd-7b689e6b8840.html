Development and Use of Human 3-Dimensional Tissue Culture Models for the Study of Space Radiation Effects on the Central Nervous System

Record ID: NNX10AB38G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Human space exploration involves exposure to types of radiation not normally encountered on earth. The effect of these particles on the human central nervous system (CNS) is of particular concern since impairment of cognitive function could not only compromise missions but cause later effects on the quality of life. Studies in animals have shown that space radiation particles can have adverse effects on cognition and behavior. The effect on neurogenesis has also been documented and proposed to be a cause of cognitive loss. Information regarding the effects of space radiation on the CNS is, however, still in its infancy due to the difficulty of extrapolating data from animals and cell lines to the intact human being.

The primary objective of this investigation was to increase the understanding of the effects of space radiation on the human central nervous system with an emphasis on neurodegeneration. There were two primary goals:

  1. Develop 3-dimensional tissue models for neural tissue, achieving a more accurate representation of human neural tissue by incorporating human neuron, astrocytes, and endothelial cells into the model.
  2. Apply the 3-dimensional models to develop assays to support projected studies of the effects of space radiation on neurodegeneration.

APPROACH:
The study began with the development of the human brain tissue model. At least 3 human cell types were acquired for each of the components of the model: neurons, astrocytes and endothelial cells. This ensured the best possible model and provided back-up cells. Then, a multistep protocol was developed for the generation and differentiation of normal human neurons from stem cells. Expression of neuronal markers and degeneration markers in 3D monocultures was successfully detected. The strategy for constructing tissue models was to embed neurons and astrocytes into protein gel matrices after they had been differentiated into the respective cell type and then induce morphological or “end stage” differentiation while they were in the matrix.

Endothelial cells were added, given the right factors, and allowed to grow into the matrix and form blood vessels as in vivo during angiogenesis. Different media types and different pairings of cell types were tested. As a result, it was found that the requirement of endothelial cells was not entirely compatible with that of neurons and astrocytes. Endothelial cells, which line the vessels and are in contact with blood in vivo require serum for vessel formation. Neurons and astrocytes, normally behind the blood brain barrier (BBB), require serum-free conditions to differentiate in matrices. However, a prototype neurovascular unit using the above protocol was created by reducing the serum concentration and relying on trophic support from astrocytes for the maintenance of vessels. At this stage, it was determined that tissue models were developed enough to be incorporated into assays that assess tight junctions and provide an additional relevant endpoint.

Assay development was directed towards determination of the effects of space radiation on the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB).

Assays to determine the function of the endothelial barrier were developed. For the first assay, trans-endothelial resistance measurement was achieved by using equipment that measures electric cell-substrate impedance sensing (ECIS). Resistance monitoring of monolayers was easy and continuous. This assay is useful for determining parameters such as the time course of the effects of radiation. A second assay was developed using tissue inserts to determine the rate of labeled molecules permeation through the cell layer on the insert. This provides a direct measurement of the passage of molecules of varying sizes through the endothelial cell layer. This assay does not utilize the neurovascular unit but can still include a cell mixture of endothelial cells and astrocytes or pericytes. Endothelial cells are plated on one side of the membrane and the other cell types are plated on the other side of the membrane. Thus it represents the human blood-brain-barrier more closely than endothelial cells alone. 2 additional assays were developed for localization of the junction proteins that seal the BBB using immunolabeling of these molecules: the first examining the junction proteins in normal monolayers and the second, examining the junction proteins in the 3-D human tissue models. Further assays include endpoints more directly applied to human neuro-degenerative diseases. One such endpoint is the metabolization and localization of the protein involved in Alzheimer’s disease – the amyloid precursor protein (APP).

RESULTS:
The primary goal of this investigation has been achieved. Human 3-D tissue models have been developed sufficiently for use in novel assays to determine degenerative markers. Other assays were developed for use in future studies that address a pivotal part of the brain involved heavily in many neuro-degenerative disorders – the brain blood capillaries. Work going forward includes improving the longevity of these models and substituting neurons from stem cells for the neuroblastoma used in the prototype model.
