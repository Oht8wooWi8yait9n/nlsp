Summary / Description

The STS-81 mission was the fifth docking mission between the U.S. Space Shuttle and Russia's Mir Space Station and the second involving an exchange of U.S. astronauts. Space Shuttle Atlantis launched on January 12, 1997, carrying Commander Michael A. Baker, Pilot Brent W. Jett, and Mission Specialists John M. Grunsfeld, Marsha S. Ivins, Peter J. K. Wisoff, and Jerry M. Linenger. Linenger replaced John Blaha on board Mir staying as the fourth U.S. long-duration astronaut on Mir. Blaha took Linengers place on the Shuttle for the return trip to Earth. The exchange of these crewmembers ushered in the NASA 4 mission, marking the end of a successful NASA 3 mission. Linenger was returned to Earth on STS-84 after approximately four months aboard the Mir Space Station.

During the five days of joint operations, astronauts and cosmonauts transferred science samples and data collected by the Mir 22/NASA 3 crew from the Mir to the Shuttle. The data was returned to Earth for analysis by researchers. Crewmembers also transferred hardware and supplies to Mir to support the health and well being of the Mir 23/NASA 4 crew, as well as to support future investigations on the station. Many of the resupply science items directly supported Mir 23/NASA 4 investigations. A total of 5,975 pounds of logistics were transferred to and from the Mir, the largest transfer of items to date.

Atlantis carried the Spacehab double module, which provided additional space for secondary experiments. These included experiments in the fields of advanced technology, Earth sciences, fundamental biology, human life sciences, microgravity and space sciences. Data from these experiments will supply insight for the planning and development of the International Space Station, Earth-based sciences of human and biological processes, and the advancement of commercial technology.

Fundamental Biology investigators from NASA and the European Space Agency (ESA) worked on a series of experiments known as the Biorack experiments. The Biorack experiments studied the effects of microgravity and cosmic radiation, particularly the effects of high-energy particles on the development of plants, tissues, cells, bacteria and insects during spaceflight. Developed by the ESA, the Biorack is a complete experiment rack that fits into the Spacehab module. It consists of two incubators with 1-g reference centrifuges, a glovebox, and a refrigerator/freezer unit. Among the international set of Biorack investigations were a study of microgravity on bone loss; the effects of using centrifuges as 1-g references for the microgravity tests; cell response, repair, activation, and gene expression in microgravity; developmental processes; plant experiments; and a dosimetry experiment to document the radiation environment inside the Biorack facility and other locations inside the Spacehab module and middeck. The three U.S. investigations were as follows:

  • BIORACK: Microgravity Effects on Bone Cell Gene Expression (OSTEO)
  • BIORACK: Mechanisms of Gravity Sensing and Response in Hematopoietic Cells (TCELL)
  • BIORACK: Modification of Radiogenic Damage By Microgravity (ELEGANS)

    Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs) were also flown on the STS-81 mission. A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies required minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

    Electronic still cameras aboard the Space Shuttle Atlantis supported the second flight of KidSat, as a part of NASA's three-year pilot education program designed to bring space exploration to 15 U.S. middle school classrooms via the Internet. KidSat is a prototype of Earth viewing cameras and instruments that allowed students in grades Kindergarten to Grade 12 to see and direct the capture of pictures from space. Other payloads included the Shuttle Amateur Radio Experiment-II, Biorack, Cosmic Radiation Effects and Activation Monitor, Orbiter Space Vision System, and Midcourse Space Experiment.

    As the NASA-Mir program continued, unique data were collected from experiments that had never been performed during such long stays in space. With international cooperation, the Space Shuttle has lived up to its name, delivering and returning long-duration crewmembers, precious research supplies and living essentials to a one-of-a-kind research platform, the Mir Space Station. The STS-81 mission was one step closer to increasing the understanding of many scientific areas and how they relate to space exploration technology. This growing body of knowledge will undoubtedly serve as a foundation for maintaining a continuous human presence in space.
