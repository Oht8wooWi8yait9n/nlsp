Active Dosimetry of Charged Particles

Record ID: 9401681
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
With the advent of manned spaceflight, the field of radiation risk assessment entered new dimensions. The human body was exposed, for the first time, to the energetic heavy particle component of the space radiation field, a radiation component being absent on earth and hence without established risk estimates. The lack of adequate radiobiological data for risk assessments enforced the need for experimental research on the radiobiological effects of energetic heavy particles, both ground-based and in space.

Radiation measurements obtained from the spectra of charged particles and their Linear Energy Transfer (LET) can be used to assess radiation risk to crewmembers through calculations that take into account the specific radiobiological efficiency of particles of different charge number and energy deposition.

Previous radiation research has measured the physical parameters of heavy ions in space using integrating passive solid-state nuclear track detectors. By using time resolving active detectors, these measurements can be correlated to components of different origin. These components include, orbital parameters, solar activity, and mass shielding; all of which are subject to changes within a dedicated space mission as well as from mission to mission. This experiment measured particle spectra and spectra of their LET for mean quality factor calculations inside the Mir module with temporal and spatial resolution. These data helped contribute to radiation risk estimates and to improvements of radiation transport codes.

APPROACH:
The aim of this experiment was to gather radiation data of charged particles throughout the mission in order to obtain the variation of the charged particle spectra with orbital parameters as well as with temporal fluctuations. The evaluation of those data sets allowed discrimination of the origin of the radiation, the cosmic component and contributions from the radiation belt at the site of the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) as well as solar particles from unpredictable flare events.

A Particle Telescope Head (PTH), mounted in the Kristall module of the Mir Space Station, housed the radiation measuring devices used in this experiment. Two Passive Implanted Silicon (PIPS) detectors measured the time dose profile and the particle rate outside and inside the South-Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) and the LET spectra for each crossing of the radiation belts in the SAA and for each orbit. A telescopic set of Charge Coupled Devices (CCDs) was used to detect the time resolved angular distribution of heavy charged particles.

The data obtained from this experiment along with shielding data, calculated from three dimensional mass models of the Mir Space Station, was used to test transport code models for the radiation environment inside and outside the SAA.

RESULTS:
The data obtained from both detection systems used in this experiment were in good agreement with each other. The PIPS detectors measured the doses of Galactic Cosmic Rays (GCR), protons of the SAA and Solar Energetic Particle (SEP) event contributions during the initial phase of the November 6, 1997, solar flare event and determined the mean quality factors for the GCR and the SAA protons in the Krystall module of Mir Space Station.

The CCD telescope was shown to be capable of detecting spatially resolved single heavy particles. The CCD telescope was also able to clearly identify traversals of high LET particles with high energies (galactic particle component) from the analysis of 3-fold coincidences.

Aside from the measurements of LET spectra, the detection devices used in this experiment also measured single particles of the omni-directional radiation field with a local resolution of 10 micrometers. This would permit their use in radiobiological investigations of single particle effects in dedicated areas of individuals (as performed in ground experiments at accelerators) under space radiation and environmental conditions. The knowledge gained from this investigation will help to increase the reliability of radiation risk estimates in the future.
