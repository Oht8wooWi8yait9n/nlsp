Cellular Signal Perception and Signal Transduction

Record ID: 9307385
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

Cellular signal processing in all organisms is probably based on fundamentally similar mechanisms. The stimulus interacts with a primary receptor in order to initiate a response, mediated by signal transduction pathways. In the case of gravity as a stimulus, it has been shown that free- living single eukaryotic cells, like slime molds, often use this vector for their spatial orientation (gravitaxis) and, in addition, show distinct sensitivities to gravity. For this experiment, slime mold cells (Physarum polycephalum) were used to locate the gravireceptor and determine the interaction between signal perception and the cell's response (signal transduction and processing).

APPROACH:

To investigate the acceleration-stimulus signal transduction chain, a gravisensitive myxomycete, Physarum polycephalum (acellular slime mold) was used. Its ameboid locomotion represents one of the two major types of cellular motility (the other major type being microtubule-based locomotion). The plasmodia, giant cells, display a distinct gravitaxis and their intrinsic rhythmic contractions and cytoplasmic streaming are modulated by gravity. To determine the gravity influence on cell function, acceleration deprivation (i.e., near weightlessness, "0 G,") was used. Thirty-six petri plates (18 in each BRIC-60 canister) were flown on the Shuttle for 11 days. Petri plates were removed from the BRIC and frozen in the GN2 freezer at two different points in the flight, 9 petri plates after 2 days in space and another 9 after 3 days in space. The remaining 18 petri plates were returned to Earth and frozen after recovery. The flown samples were then analyzed and compared with the ground controls. Cellular second messenger levels (the cyclic nucleotides cAMP and cGMP) were analyzed.

RESULTS:

Previous flight studies with variable gravity found that plasmodia immersed in water (to eliminate indirect gravity effects) were still able to respond to acceleration changes. This proved that the gravity response in Physarum is based on direct gravity effects. This response is relayed via parts of the cell, such as the nuclei or mitochondria, that have a higher density than the rest of the system. This BRIC-06 study demonstrated that only the level of the second messenger cAMP drops significantly with time (spent in microgravity) in the force-generating part of the cell. So, contrary to short-term gravistimulations, adaptation to microgravity leads to permanently reduced cAMP levels. The results indicated that in Physarum polycephalum, the second messenger cAMP is involved in gravity signaling.
