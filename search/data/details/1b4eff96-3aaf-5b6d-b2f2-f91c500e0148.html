Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
In order to better understand the microbial ecology of the spacecraft environment investigators are developing technology that can be used to characterize and monitor important microbial populations in the spacecraft environment with emphasis on early detection of increased levels of undesirable organisms. It is envisioned that such organisms might include some that are not normally pathogenic or problematic but become so as a result of unique characteristics of the space environment. Investigators previously developed the theoretical underpinnings for approaches that could characterize the phylogenetic position of any bacterium that is present in a sample regardless of prior expectations of what might be found. In particular it was found that a large number of "signature sequences" whose presence corresponds with important nodes in a phylogenetic tree exist and can be identified. Investigators tested prototype assay systems that rely on array hybridization, mass spectrometry, or homogenous (1-step) fluorescence to detect the presence of specific signatures of interest. So as to directly test the effects of the spacecraft environmental conditions on microbial physiology at the level of gene expression they are using array hybridization to monitor the expression of each gene product in E. coli growing both aerobically and anaerobically in a rotating wall vessel (RWV) under low shear modeled microgravity (LSMMG) conditions.

The specific hypothesis tested was that there are specific genes that are responsive to environmental perturbations associated with the space flight environment, some which may play a role in mediating the pathogenicity of microorganisms. In support of these activities, investigators were also seeking to develop improved spacecraft-compatible methodologies for use in microbial detection. It is anticipated that samples might be taken from surfaces, water, or air. This aspect of the project included identifying chemistries for nucleic acid isolation and detection suitable for implementation in space-borne monitoring systems. Investigators sought to further characterize and exploit their discovery of immobilized-metal affinity adsorption for RNA isolation and the removal of primers and dyes from samples to facilitate further analysis.

RESULTS:
Investigators completed the development of a set of 16S rRNA targeted probes that will indicate the presence of major problem and indicator bacteria in flight samples. These probes can be used with similar efficiency under a standard set of operating conditions. The utility of these probes was demonstrated on several samples isolated from various systems including Mars and Moon soil simulants. Investigators also completed the development of a computational algorithm that allows identification of short 16S rRNA subsequences that are highly characteristic of various phylogenetic groupings. In principle, an array of appropriately designed probes based on these signature sequences could be used to determine the genetic affinity (nearest known relatives) in the absence of any prior knowledge of what the problematic organism might be. In practice, the signature sequences themselves tend to be short (15 nucleotides or less) and hence not ideal for use in arrays.

An effective set of probes may have utility in a large variety of formats. It is likely that actual implementation in space flight will be driven by mission instrumentation capabilities. Although there is considerable interested in development of array-based instruments other approaches may be preferable, especially for International Space Station applications. Therefore, investigators have focused their attention on several alternative formats as well. In this regard, several probes for organisms of primary interest have been successfully implemented in a molecular beacon format. Homogenous solution assays of this type would require minimal sample processing and could be readily conducted by astronauts in flight with results signaled by the presence or absence of color changes. It was found that "red-shifted" beacons have minimal contributions from sample autofluorescence. In addition, the investigators demonstrated the potential utility of fluorescent nucleotides such as 2-aminopurine in molecular beacon applications.

Regardless of the assay system ultimately chosen, rapid and simplified systems for sample processing in space will be required. Investigators provided further evidence that RNA/DNA purification using compaction agents eliminates the need for preprocessing steps and that the same agents can be used to enhance the adsorption capacity of anion exchangers. Investigators also discovered evidence that immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) which is widely used with proteins is also effective with nucleic acids. Most recently, they have begun to look at novel ways of obtaining rapid final purification of specific RNAs such as 16S rRNA.
