In vivo Induction of Chromosome Instability and Aberrant Patterns of DNA Methylation in Hematopoietic Stem-Progenitor Cells by Heavy Ions

Record ID: NNX11AK91G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Cancer of blood cells, in particular myeloid leukemia, is one of the major health concerns from exposure to space radiation. However, the predictions of risks for developing cancer of blood cells after exposure to space radiation are unsatisfactory. To increase the reliability of predicting risk for developing cancer of blood cells, a much improved understanding of space radiation-induced changes in the target cells (stem cells and their progenitors) in living organisms for the induction of this particular type of cancer is critically important.

This research was the first to use two in vivo endpoints relevant to cancer induction in the hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) that are the target of radiation-induced myeloid leukemia (ML) to evaluate the effects of space radiation. The data from this study contributed to reducing uncertainties in the assessment of cancer risk from exposure to radiation during space flights and also improved the understanding of the molecular basis for the influence of space radiation on genomic instability and aberrant DNA-methylation profiles.

APPROACH:
Since human experiments are not possible, animal studies are critically important. In this project, investigators used the CBA/CaJ mouse as an experimental model to investigate the biological effects of heavy ions. To do this, they exposed mice to two types of heavy ions found in space and relevant to NASA missions, i.e. silicon and titanium. They conducted experiments to determine the effects of these ions on the induction of damage at the genetic level by measuring the frequency and type of initial- and late-occurring chromosome instability (normally known as genomic instability). Analyses are done in HSPCs residing in the bone marrow (BM) of exposed mice collected at different times post-irradiation using a novel genome-wide multicolor fluorescence in situ hybridization (whole genome mFISH). The measurement of chromosome instability in HSPCs is coupled with the characterization of damage at the epigenetic level (i.e. the damage affecting gene function without a change in the sequence of DNA molecules) by determining the pattern of DNA-methylation. These two biological endpoints are highly relevant for assessing cancer risks; however, they have not been applied with in vivo exposure to space radiation with silicon or titanium ions until now.

There were six specific goals for this experiment:
1. Exposure of mice to different doses of 28Si or 48Ti ions.
2. Collection of BM cells from sham controls and exposed mice.
3. Determination of the number and types (according to morphology) of the BM-derived HSPCs colonies.
4. Collection of the BM-derived HSPCs obtained from sham controls and exposed mice for the analyses of chromosome aberrations (CAs) and DNA methylation induced in vivo.
5. Determination of the pattern of DNA methylation (both 5mC and 5hmC) in HSPCs obtained at various times post-irradiation from sham controls and mice exposed to 28Si or 48Ti ions.
6. Measurement of the frequencies of CAs in HSPCs obtained at various times post-irradiation from sham controls and mice exposed to 28Si or 48Ti ions.

RESULTS:
Investigators have accomplished goals 1 through 4. They have made substantial progress in determining the global (total) levels of 5mC and 5hmC and the frequencies of CAs in HSPCs (goals 5 and 6). The kinetics studies of 5mC and 5hmC after exposure to 28Si or 48Ti ions have been completed. The data suggest that the global level of 5-hydroxymethyl cytosine may be a better predictor of exposure to HZE particles found in space that, in turn, might be more useful for risk assessment than the global level of 5-methyl cytosine. Investigators found reduced expression levels of Ten-Eleven-Translocation 2 (TET2) enzyme (the enzyme known to convert 5mC to 5hmC) in plasma of exposed mice. Such a finding suggests an important role of TET2 in the production of 5hmC. Importantly, the data also suggest that targeting TET2 enzyme (by increasing its expression) may prevent or mitigate the acute or long-term effects of radiation on cells at risk for ML induced by radiation found in space.

For the cytogenetic analysis (by means of the mFISH method), the focus is on the delayed- or late-occurring CAs because it is well recognized that it is the best-established end point of radiation-induced genomic instability (a fundamental mechanism known to elevate cancer risk). Investigators have completed the analysis of CAs in HSPCs collected at six months after exposure of CBA/Ca mice to silicon or titanium ions. Several types of aberrations were observed in HSPCs collected from exposed CBA/CaJ mice. These include abnormal cells, chromatid breaks, chromosome breaks, and translocations (exchanges). Investigators found dose-dependent increases in the frequencies of abnormal cells, chromatid and chromosome breaks (p <0.05, respectively). Of note, the data also showed significant increases in the frequencies of exchanges in HSPCs collected from mice exposed to a medium (0.25 Gy) or a high (0.5 Gy) dose of silicon or titanium ions. Overall, delayed- or late-occurring chromosomal damage was clearly observed in HSPCs collected from mice exposed to silicon or titanium ions, suggesting the occurrence of genomic instability.
