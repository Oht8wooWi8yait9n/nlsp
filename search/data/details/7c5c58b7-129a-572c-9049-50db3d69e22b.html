Vestibular Experiments in Spacelab

Record ID: 178072
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The neurovestibular system faces a special challenge in weightlessness and is involved in a complex adaptive process, which may be associated with space motion sickness. Nearly half of all space travelers experience symptoms resembling those of motion sickness. Susceptibility to space sickness appears highest during the first several days in space, and is generally limited to the first week. Symptoms range from nausea and headaches to severe vomiting. This neurovestibular adaptation is studied for the following reasons: 1) to develop methods for prediction, prevention and treatment of space motion sickness; 2) to reduce risk in an emergency egress in the event of an accident; and 3) to address the issues of human adaptation to very long duration flights.

The neurovestibular investigations were based on the sensory reinterpretation hypothesis, which provides a framework for interpreting many of the results. The sensory re-interpretation hypothesis holds that the following changes occur during spaceflight: 1) nerve signals from the otolith organs are reinterpreted as indicating head translation rather than tilt, 2) sensitivity to footward acceleration is reduced, and 3) the nervous system relies more on visual and tactile cues in the perception of orientation and control of posture.

APPROACH:
To explore the full range of neurovestibular adaptation, a series of human experiments was performed, each with a different combination of stimuli and responses. Each sub-experiment will be briefly described.

Visually induced roll/visual-vestibular interaction (rotating dome, rod and frame) The rotating dome experiment was designed to explore the conflicting visual, vestibular, and tactile sensory cues that occur in weightlessness. Visually induced self-motion and compensatory eye and head movements, stimulated by looking into the open end of a rolling drum, were measured. On Earth, gravity acting on human otolith organs inhibits the development of visually induced self-motion. In weightlessness, the strength of visually induced self-motion should increase because confirming vestibular signals are absent. This experiment was carried out with the head held stationary by a biteboard to eliminate stimulation of the vestibular organs and thereby understand their role in sensory input. The rod and frame test measured a subject’s dependence on a static visual field. Subjects indicated tilt of a perceived vertical marker. The experiment was performed before and after flight to determine if the increased dependence on static visual cues for orientation in space affects readaptation once back on Earth.

Vestibulo-ocular reflex (rotating chair): The rotating chair experiment was performed to determine how the vestibulo-ocular reflex is affected by otolith input. The vestibulo-ocular reflex provides a stable retinal (eye) image during head rotation by generating appropriate compensatory eye movements. Nerve impulses are conveyed from the semicircular canals and the otolith to the oculomotor nerve. When the otolith is no longer stimulated, as in microgravity, the vestibulo-ocular reflex is hypothesized to change.

To assess how this reflex is affected when it no longer receives input from the otolith, a rotating chair was used to stimulate the semicircular canals and study the nystagmus “dumping” (shortening of the time constant of nystagmus) produced by tilting the head face down after rotation. Eye movements were recorded while the subject was slowly spun in the rotating chair and afterward.

Awareness of position: The “position awareness” experiment was designed to examine the influence of weightlessness on the way subjects orient objects in the absence of a “real” vertical and the accuracy of cues from certain nerve receptors in determining perceived limb position. Subjects were instructed to view the location of several different targets placed on nearby walls. They were then blindfolded and asked to describe the position of their limbs and point to the target position from memory.

Considerable evidence indicates that knowledge of limb and body position may be altered by exposure to weightlessness. In this experiment, investigators attempted to separate two factors that might contribute to the apparent alteration in knowledge of limb and body position: (1) degradation of proprioceptive function, and (2) an inaccurate external spatial map. Subjects' ability to point at a target with a light pointer was measured twice: once during the continuous absence of vision, and once by closing the eyes only while pointing (to maintain a fresh image of the outside world).

Responses to linear acceleration (sled): The sled is a linear acceleration device, which was used for stimulating eye movement as well as subjective motion during horizontal linear acceleration. The purpose of these tests was to study the response of the linear acceleration sensors. It is hypothesized that weightlessness leads to an inhibition of otolith-derived spatial information, with the central nervous system (CNS) eventually learning to reinterpret otolith information as providing information on transient linear acceleration. This experiment was performed after landing to find out if the otolith input for linear acceleration is affected by spaceflight.

Postflight postural instability (rails, posture EMG/ muscle fatigue measurement): The rails experiment was designed to measure the degree of postural instability after flight by recording the astronauts’ ability to stand and walk on narrow and wide rails. The pre- and post-flight results were compared to determine if degradation of postural stability occurred. The EMG experiment studied the otolith-spinal reflex, which normally prepares one for a landing from a fall. Sometimes called “hop and drop”, the experiment required the subjects to be suspended from bungee cords; they hopped in an upward direction, then dropped toward the floor to simulate a fall. Electromyography (EMG) measurements were made during the activity.

Space motion sickness recording (head movement and symptom monitoring, head movement comparison test): In this investigation, space sickness symptoms were clinically characterized and their relationship to head movements; visual, tactile and proprioceptive cues; and the shift of body fluids toward the head was studied.

The crew was trained to log motion sickness symptoms according to a well-defined scale. Also, in-flight head movement data were obtained from one individual. Motion sickness susceptibility was measured using the head movement comparison test on the KC-135 before and after the mission.

RESULTS:
Visually induced roll/visual-vestibular interaction (rotating dome, rod and frame): Results suggested an increased weighting of visual cues and reduced weighting of gravireceptor (otolith) signals in weightlessness. After flight, all subjects showed an increase in postural instability, and a strong tendency to sway when the visual field rotated.

Vestibulo-ocular reflex (rotating chair): The rotating chair results showed no consistent vestibulo-ocular (VOR) gain changes in orbit. Although the strength of the angular VOR is known to decrease acutely in parabolic flight, a longer time constant was measured in subjects after 4 to 5 days of adaptation in weightlessness, suggesting a return of angular velocity storage. In half the rotating chair tests, the head was pitched forward 90 degrees immediately after stopping the chair rotation. In microgravity, the pitch movement produced rapid decreases in the perception of subjective rotation, but not in the VOR. The observed changes in the VOR and the perceptual indications of rotation may represent a sensory processing rearrangement induced by weightlessness.

Awareness of position: During the awareness of position tests, in-flight pointing accuracy was very poor, with a bias toward pointing low. Performance was always better when the eyes were closed only while pointing, and was unchanged before, during and after flight under these conditions. After the flight, all subjects made greater errors than before the flight, but in a similar direction. Recovery to the preflight level of performance was nearly complete by 7 days after landing. These results provide evidence that the primary adaptation in spaceflight is loss of the external spatial map and that complete postflight recovery requires several days.

Responses to linear acceleration (sled): After flight, erect posture with eyes closed was disturbed for several days. Somewhat greater visual field dependence after flight was observed for two of the crew. Postflight testing using the sled revealed increased variance in the detection of acceleration. The ability of the returned crew to use nonvisual lateral acceleration cues for a manual control task appeared enhanced over their preflight ability for a few days after their return to Earth.

Postflight postural instability (rails, posture EMG/ muscle fatigue measurement): An early otolith-spinal reflex, measured by EMG from the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles during sudden footward acceleration, was inhibited immediately upon entering weightlessness and declined further during the flight, but when measured shortly after return to Earth, was unchanged from preflight measurements.

Space motion sickness recording (head movement and symptom monitoring, head movement comparison test): Six short-duration tests of motion sickness susceptibility, conducted before flight, failed to predict sickness intensity in weightlessness.
