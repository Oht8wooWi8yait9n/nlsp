Summary / Description

The STS-84 mission was the sixth docking mission between the U.S. Space Shuttle and Russia's Mir Space Station. The Space Shuttle Atlantis launched on May 15,1997, with seven crewmembers on board, including Commander Charles J. Precourt, Pilot Eileen M. Collins, and Mission Specialists Carlos I. Noriega, Jean-Francois Clervoy, and Michael Foale. Foale joined the Mir 23 crew, replacing Jerry Linenger as the fifth U.S. long-duration astronaut on Mir. Linenger took Foale's place on the Shuttle for the return trip to Earth.

The Space Shuttle Atlantis carried a double Spacehab module in the payload bay, which housed the logistics equipment to be transferred to Mir and experiments performed by the Shuttle's crew. During the 5 days of joint U.S.-Russian operations, astronauts and cosmonauts transferred the science samples and data collected by the Mir 22, 23 and NASA 4 crews from the Mir to the Shuttle, where they were returned for analysis on Earth. Crewmembers also transferred 7,314 pounds of water and logistics to and from the Mir. Logistics include vital equipment and supplies required by the astronauts to live and work in space for extended periods, such as laptop computers, computer cables, disk drives, flashlights, food packets, documentation and clothing.

Primary payloads carried in the Spacehab module were the European Space Agency's Biorack and the Morphological Transition and Model Substances (MOMO) experiment. Biorack is a large multi-purpose facility that provides temperature-controlled environments, centrifuges for simulating gravity, and a protected workspace for specimen handling, all integrated into a single rack. Biorack carried experiments from France, Germany and the United States investigating the effects of microgravity and radiation on various biological systems. MOMO investigated solidification, one of the most fundamental processes in the industrial production of materials.

Fundamental Biology investigators from NASA and the European Space Agency (ESA) worked on a series of experiments known as the Biorack experiments. The Biorack experiments studied the effects of microgravity and cosmic radiation, particularly the effects of high-energy particles on the development of plants, tissues, cells, bacteria and insects during spaceflight. Developed by the ESA, the Biorack is a complete experiment rack that fits into the Spacehab module. It consists of two incubators with 1-g reference centrifuges, a glovebox, and a refrigerator/freezer unit. Among the international set of Biorack investigations were a study of microgravity on bone loss; the effects of using centrifuges as 1-g references for the microgravity tests; cell response, repair, activation, and gene expression in microgravity; developmental processes; plant experiments; and a dosimetry experiment to document the radiation environment inside the Biorack facility and other locations inside the Spacehab module and middeck. The three U.S. investigations were as follows:

  • BIORACK: Microgravity Effects on Bone Cell Gene Expression (OSTEO)
  • BIORACK: Mechanisms of Gravity Sensing and Response in Hematopoietic Cells (TCELL)
  • BIORACK: Modification of Radiogenic Damage By Microgravity (ELEGANS)

    Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs) were also flown on the STS-84 mission. A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies required minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

    Other payloads on STS-84 included the Cosmic Radiation Effects and Activation Monitor and Risk Mitigation Experiments.

    Space Shuttle Atlantis landed on May 24, 1997, at Kennedy Space Center in Florida after a successful nine-day mission.
