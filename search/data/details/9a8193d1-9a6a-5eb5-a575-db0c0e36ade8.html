Personality and Biological Predictors of Resiliency to Chronic Stress among High-Achieving Adults

Record ID: NNX15AN96G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
In order to select candidate astronauts for high stress resilience, it is first necessary to identify traits that prognosticate resilience in high-achieving (astronaut-like) adults. Together, a combination of such traits would be compiled into a valid assessment tool to maximize accuracy. This study tested whether objective markers derived from innovative personality assessment and event-related potentials (ERPs; e.g., electroencephalography recorded during reaction time tasks) prognosticated stress resilience in high-achieving adults recruited from a naturally high-stress environment (e.g., Science, Technology, Engineering, and Medicine (STEM) graduate training programs).

This study had the following specific aims:

  1. Test personality facets as predictors of resilience after accounting for cognitive ability, over-claiming response bias, and Big 5 traits.
  2. Evaluate contributions of neurophysiological markers for predicting resilience.

APPROACH:
207 high-achieving adults were recruited from graduate and post-graduate training programs to participate in a longitudinal stress study. The baseline assessment was approximately two hours and included several domains. 26 personality facets were assessed using a modified version of the Facet Inventory of the Five Factor Model, an empirically-designed self-report instrument. EEG was recorded while participants completed two speeded reaction time tasks designed to yield event-related potential (ERP) indices of error monitoring. Stimuli were presented briefly in order to increase likelihood of behavioral mistakes. Response locked (for error and correct response trials, respectively) and stimulus locked (N200 and P300 amplitudes) ERPs were created by averaging trials in the usual way. Cognitive ability, a standard marker of resiliency, was assessed using a standardized computer battery. Response bias was assessed using the Over-claiming Questionnaire (OCQ), which was designed to capture the tendency to report oneself as especially competent and knowledgeable. The baseline assessment also included stress (perceived stress scale, a Work-Life inventory) and behavioral health outcomes (mood, anxiety, and psychosocial functioning).

After the baseline assessment, participants were invited to repeat online assessments of stress and behavioral health outcomes monthly for five months. This allowed investigators to carefully monitor fluctuations in stress and link them to corresponding fluctuations in behavioral health outcomes. In this way, stress-related declines in each outcome could be modeled as a linear slope and resilience could be defined by baseline predictors associated with less decline in behavioral health during periods of high stress.

RESULTS:
As expected, level of ten behavioral health outcomes over five months could be predicted by personality facets assessed at baseline. Also as expected, increases in stress were associated with worsening behavioral health across five months. Importantly, certain baseline personality facets prognosticated the rate of stress-induced decline, especially for three key behavioral health outcomes. This prognostic validity of facets largely held when controlling for cognitive ability, response bias, and standard superordinate Big 5 traits. Neural traits recorded during repeated trials of speeded reaction time tasks (e.g., ERPs) did not prognosticate level of behavioral health outcomes, but prognosticated worsening behavioral health outcomes at high levels of stress for some traits. In other words, some personality facets and some ERPs appear to represent promising prognostic tools to identify resilient individuals for high stress situations, at least for some relevant behavioral health outcomes. On the other hand, stress resilience for other relevant behavioral health outcomes were poorly prognosticated by the candidate predicators included in this study.
