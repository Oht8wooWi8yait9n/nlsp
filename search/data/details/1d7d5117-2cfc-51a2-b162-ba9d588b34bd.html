Crewmember and Crew-Ground Interactions During NASA-Mir

Record ID: 9401628
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
During future long duration missions, international crews will be engaged in complicated activities over long periods of time. A number of interpersonal issues likely to impact these missions must be addressed to ensure healthy crewmember interactions and optimal performance. The objectives of this study were to measure and characterize changes over time in a number of important interpersonal factors, such as tension, cohesion, leadership role, and the relationship between space crews and ground support personnel during the NASA/Mir missions.

The formal hypotheses addressed a number of aspects related to crew and ground control tension, cohesion, and leadership role. Over the course of long-term space missions, tension in the crew environment and crew cohesiveness were expected to change. Researchers expected an association between the level of tension in the crewmembers and their relationship with people on the ground, and between tension in mission control personnel and their relationship with management. Also, it was hypothesized that the perception of both the leadership role and outside monitoring support would change over time.

Although specific stressors that occur during the mission, there is evidence, can influence these issues, that time alone may be a factor. One approach, the biphasic model, proposes that significant psychological and interpersonal difficulties may occur after the halfway point of a mission. Hence, a sense of relief that half of the mission is over is outweighed by the realization that another half is yet to come, and this results in decrements in mood and interpersonal functioning of the crew. The triphasic model describes three phases of reaction for people working in isolating and confined conditions for long periods. The initial anxiety in the first few weeks are mainly due to the novelty of the situation; depression during the long middle period of the mission are mainly due to monotony and homesickness; and euphoria and adolescent behavior during the last few weeks due to anticipation of returning home.

When crews are composed of people from different countries or different ethnic backgrounds, language and cultural differences are important since they may lead to intra-crew tensions and disruptions in cohesion, and this in turn may negatively affect morale and interfere with the performance of mission tasks. Issues related to language and cultural differences have been reported in both simulation and space environments. Language and cultural issues also may adversely affect the interactions of mission control personnel on the ground, although these individuals have not been studied previously.

The relationship between the crewmembers and their mission control support personnel is important, since miscommunication or insensitivity between these two groups may lead to misaligned work schedules or inappropriate responses during times of danger. A phenomenon that may negatively influence crew ground communication is displacement, which occurs when tension and distressful dysphoric emotions (e.g., anger, depression) that arise within the crewmembers during the mission cannot be expressed openly and are instead transferred outside to ground personnel, who are perceived as unsupportive and negative. Interactions suggestive of displacement have been reported during space missions and during space simulation studies on Earth. In simulations, crewmember anger directed to outside monitors has been especially observed when overt anger and tension were not seen between the confined subjects, and vice versa.

Earlier work explored several of the above psychosocial issues in confined groups and included a questionnaire study of 54 astronauts and cosmonauts who had flown in space and a study of three male Russian subjects who were secluded in the Mir space station simulator in Moscow for 135 days. The study described in this paper involved a series of space missions in which two Russian cosmonauts and one American astronaut worked together on board the Mir space station for periods of four to seven months. The objectives of the study were to determine how tension, cohesion, and leader support affected the space crewmembers and mission control personnel on the ground and to determine how these factors changed over time.

Several of the hypotheses predicted decreases in crew behavior during the second half of the missions in accordance with the biphasic model. The value of the triphasic model was also explored and other time effects that might be present while the crewmembers were on orbit. As stated in the hypothesis, displacement of tension and dysphoria would occur from crewmembers to mission control personnel and from mission control personnel to management; differences in response between Russians and Americans and between crewmembers and mission control personnel, were explored. We made no predictions concerning these differences since little empirical evidence existed that would allow us to form hypotheses related to these socio-cultural factors.

APPROACH:
American astronauts, Russian cosmonauts, and American and Russian mission control personnel who were involved with the Shuttle/Mir space program were asked to participate in our study. The number of subjects who volunteered, gave informed consent, and participated in the study during the on orbit periods included 5 astronauts (in 5 U.S. missions), 8 cosmonauts (in 4 Russian missions), and 42 American and 16 Russian mission control personnel, who included flight surgeons, operations leads, engineers, mission scientists, spacecraft communicators, and psychological support staff.

To measure mood state and group interpersonal climate, standardized measures were used which have been shown to be valid and reliable in a number of studies on Earth. To assess emotions, we utilized the Profile of Mood States, or POMS, which consisted of 65 adjectives describing various affective states, such as "tense" and "cheerful", and were rated on a weekly basis by the subjects on 5 point Likert scales:

  • 0 = Not at all
  • 1 = A little
  • 2 = Moderately
  • 3 = Quite a bit
  • 4 = Extremely

    • To assess the interpersonal climate in the crew and mission control groups, subscales from two well-known instruments: the Group Environment Scale, or GES, and the Work Environment Scale, or WES. Both of these instruments measured dimensions of a group's interpersonal environment through subject endorsements of statements as being either "true" or "false" of their particular group during the previous week. An example of a question is: "There is a feeling of unity and cohesion in this group".

    Subscale scores were determined by summing the scores from related POMS, GES, or WES items. Examples of subscales were Tension-Anxiety (from the POMS), Cohesion (from the GES), and Supervisor Support (from the WES). Changes in subscale scores over time were regarded as alterations in the subjects' perception of mood and group climate. Descriptive analyses of the data suggested that the subjects considered the full range of responses when completing the measures. For example, on 63 of the 65 items from the Profile of Mood States, subjects gave all possible responses from 0-4 with exceptions for: "spiteful" and "forgetful", and on all 126 items from the GES and WES, subjects answered both true and false responses. For most items, however, the distributions were skewed in the direction of adaptive functioning (i.e., low values on negative subscales and high values on positive subscales).

    The above measures comprised our study questionnaire, which was available in both English and Russian computerized and hard copy formats. The questions took 15-20 minutes to complete. Both crew and ground subjects completed the questionnaire with reference to the preceding seven days Overall, 82% of the expected questionnaires were received from subjects participating during the on-orbit periods (70% from crewmembers and 85% from mission control personnel). This totaled 212 from crew subjects and 1,088 from ground subjects.

    Data analysis strategies

    One analyses examined the data for differences between U.S. and Russian subjects and between crewmembers and personnel in mission control personnel. Mean scores were calculated for each subject on each of the POMS, GES, and WES subscales. Subsequently, the number of observations that contributed to it weighted each mean, and a two-way repeated measure analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The model included main effects for country (American, Russian), group (crewmember, mission control personnel), and their interactions.

    A second set of previous analyses dealt with hypotheses related to the displacement of negative emotions for both crew and ground subjects. Displacement was operationalized as the negative association between measures of tension and dysphoria with WES Supervisor Support over the course of a mission. For the crewmembers, supervisors were defined as members of the outside monitoring staff in mission control, and for the mission control subjects, supervisors were defined as members of the American or Russian space agency management staff. A mixed model analysis was used to test the effects for each measure of tension or dysphoria. During weeks that there was high tension and dysphoria in the "in-group" (i.e., the crew or ground), we hypothesized that there would be a tendency to displace these feelings outwardly to the respective mission control or management "out-group" and to perceive these supervisors as not being supportive.

    A third set of analyses examined hypotheses related to the biphasic model, which predicted decrements in crewmember emotional state and interactions during the second half of the missions. Before analysis, the distribution of the raw data was examined for normality. Several subscales (dependent variables) had scores that were normally distributed. Others were successfully normalized using a logarithmic transformation. Still others had greatly skewed distributions and needed to be dichotomized. Cutoffs were made as close as possible to include 50% of the scores in the "high" category and 50% in the "low" category. These dichotomous variables were analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) fixed effect models with logit link functions and binomial variance. All other variables were analyzed using a mixed model approach that modeled random intercepts and slopes and assumed a decreasing correlation as the lag interval increased. Both the GEE and the mixed model approaches accounted for the effects of autocorrelation and were not handicapped by missing values in our longitudinal data caused by random events that prevented the subjects from completing the measures during a given week.

    The bi-phasic analysis used a piecewise linear regression approach. The weekly subscale scores for each subject for each mission were arrayed around the midpoint of the mission (counted as week = 0) because the launches and mission periods of Americans and Russians were staggered in calendar time and were of differing lengths. The missions varied from 16 weeks (an American astronaut) to 28 weeks (two Russian cosmonauts). From the actual weekly subscale scores, overall estimates of the average slope in the first half and in the second half of the missions were calculated, with these slopes intercepting at the midpoint.

    Another analyses also tested for the effects of time during the on-orbit period but modeled the effects of triphasic rather than biphasic changes. A regression approach with a quadratic function was selected to test for a pattern indicative of the presence of three phases in all crewmembers and in the Americans and Russians taken separately. For example, the literatures have suggested that crew tension levels would be high initially, and then drop during the middle phase of the mission. Tension would then rise again at the end as the termination approaches. Since quadratic functions describe curves with one bend, this analytic procedure was a reasonable way for us to test for U-shaped patterns among the variables in the data. However, since other curvilinear patterns may give a significant quadratic result as well, we had to visually inspect the data to see if a U-shape or some other curve best defined it.

    Since the regression models with a quadratic function included tests for linear functions in the analysis, a comparison of the quadratic pattern with a linear pattern was explored for the subscales throughout the course of the mission. If both of these tests for a given subscale are significant, then it is appropriate to present only the quadratic results. It is important to realize that the pattern of change in the actual data for a given subscale variable may not in itself be linear. For example, for variables that were dichotomized and subject to a GEE analysis, what was being evaluated was the presence of a linear pattern in the logit transformed values, not in the actual data. The most one could infer from such a case was that there was an overall tendency for a variable to increase or decrease versus showing no change over time (e.g., the tension level rose or fell during the course of the total mission versus staying pretty much the same). Therefore, one needs to visually examine the plot to deduce what kind of pattern is evident.

    A second set of new analyses was conducted to determine whether crewmember subjects differed from normative samples on Earth. T-tests were used to compare mission means with normative scores from other groups on Earth, both in terms of all subjects pooled and in terms of American and Russian subjects taken separately.

    A third set of new analyses tested for differences in the subscale variables during the missions compared to pre- and post-mission. Mean scores were calculated for each subject on each of the POMS, GES, and WES subscales for the mission itself and for the pre- and post-mission periods. Subsequently, each mean was weighted by the number of observations that contributed to it, and a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed that compared crewmember subscale mean scores during the missions with pre- and post-mission means. Again, we looked at all crewmembers pooled and at Americans and Russians taken separately.

    All of the previously conducted and new regression analyses were performed using a SAS macro based to execute the GEE analysis and the SAS MIXED procedure for the mixed models. The t-tests were conducted using the SAS t-test procedure, and the ANOVAs were conducted using the SAS GLM procedure. To control for a possible inflated Type I error rate associated with multiple significance tests, the false discovery rate procedure was utilized to determine an adjusted significance threshold. This procedure controls the expected proportion of falsely rejected hypotheses (i.e., the false discovery rate). For each effect being evaluated, the actual p-values from each of the total number of N subscales being tested were rank ordered from smallest to largest. Multiplying .05 by the rank number of each subscale result and then dividing by N calculated a cutoff significance threshold. The p-value from each subscale result then was compared against the threshold p-value until a comparison was found that did not exceed the threshold. This p-value became the significance level threshold for that particular effect. The results reported below have been interpreted with reference to the appropriate calculated Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted significance threshold.

    RESULTS:
    Interpersonal and cultural results

    To examine the potential impact of interpersonal and cultural factors, we used an analysis of variance to explore differences between Americans and Russians and between crew and ground subjects. Seven of the total of 21 subscale comparisons gave p-values lower than the adjusted significance threshold for the country main effect: Americans scored higher than Russians on POMS Vigor-Activity and WES Work Pressure, and lower on WES Managerial Control, GES Task Orientation, GES Self Discovery, WES Physical Comfort, and GES Leader Support. For the group main effect, four comparisons were significant, with mission control subjects scoring higher than crewmembers on four POMS measures of dysphoria (Tension-Anxiety, Fatigue-Inertia, Confusion-Bewilderment, and Total Mood Disturbance). There were significant country by group interactions for three GES subscales: Leader Support, Expressiveness, and Independence. American crewmembers scored lower than any other group on all three measures. Taken together, these findings suggested that Americans (especially U.S. crewmembers) experienced more emotional and work related problems than Russians, and that mission control personnel were more dysphoric than crewmembers.

    Displacement results

    Strong support was found in favor that displacement would occur for both crewmembers and mission control personnel using a mixed model analysis. Six subscales were picked to evaluate displacement (POMS: Tension-Anxiety, Depression-Dejection, Anger-Hostility, Total Mood Disturbance; GES Anger & Aggression; WES Work Pressure), and the phenomenon was indicated operationally when there was a significant negative association of each of these subscales with a measure of outside support (WES Supervisor Support). All six predicted associations were significant for the crewmembers, and five of the six were significant for the mission control subjects with the exception of WES Work Pressure. These results strongly suggested that crewmembers displaced tension and negative emotions to mission control personnel and that mission control personnel displaced tension and negative emotions to management.

    Biphasic model results

    According to the biphasic model, as hypothesized, there would be a significant rise in measures of crew tension, a drop in measures of crew cohesion and leader support, and a drop in measures of mission control leader support during the second half of the missions. The cohesion hypothesis was tested by examining six subscale variables: GES Cohesion, GES Task Orientation, GES Order and Organization, POMS Depression-Dejection, POMS Fatigue-Inertia, and POMS Vigor-Activity. We tested our tension hypothesis in terms of four variables: POMS Tension-Anxiety, POMS Anger-Hostility, GES Anger & Aggression, and WES Work Pressure. Finally, leader support was examined for both the crewmembers and the mission control personnel by exploring two variables: GES Leader Support and WES Supervisor Support. Using a piecewise linear regression approach with the appropriate mixed model or GEE analyses, only one of the, variables was significant when the Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted significance threshold was applied: there was a significant drop in GES Leader Support for crewmembers in the second half. Overall, the results did not indicate the presence of a moderate to strong biphasic time effect.

    The findings reported below deal with triphasic and linear time effects and with emotional and group climate responses reported by the crewmembers during their time on-orbit (the mission period). Unlike mission control personnel, the crewmembers were confined in space during the missions in an environment quite different from that on Earth. This isolation made them especially vulnerable to group and time pressures. Consequently, the results will have special relevance for the crew subjects.

    Quadratic (triphasic) model results

    The triphasic model was evaluated in terms of a regression approach with a quadratic function, since this gives a reasonable test of the U-shaped pattern one would expect to see in the subscales over the course of the missions if this pattern was in operation. However, since quadratic functions also describe non U-shaped curves, it is important to inspect the data visually to see just what kind of curve is depicted. A significant quadratic component in the model indicates that the quadratic function is a better representation of the data than a linear function.

    For the crewmembers, all of the 21 subscale variables from the POMS, GES, and WES were tested. After applying the Benjamini and Hochberg procedure, the results indicated that none of tests for the quadratic function in these subscales met the adjusted significance threshold (p = .0024). Additional evaluations were made of the quadratic function in the 21 subscales for Russian and American crewmembers taken separately. For the Russians, none of the tests resulted in significant findings. For the American crewmembers, three of the subscales were significant for quadratic functions, with the p-value being less than the Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted significance threshold of p =.0024. These were: GES Order & Organization (beta = .007, p = .000); GES Task Orientation (beta = -.007, p = .000); and GES Self Discovery (beta = -.099, p = .000). Since all three of these variables had a skewed distribution and were dichotomized, they ~ere analyzed using a GEE model.

    Linear model results

    For all the crewmembers and for the Russians taken separately, regression analyses for the linear model were made on all 21 subscales. In both cases, none of the subscales resulted in p-values that were less than the Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted significance threshold (p = .0024).

    For the American crewmembers taken separately, only one subscale variable (other than GES Self Discovery) had a p-value that was less than the Benjamini and Hochberg threshold of p = .0048: GES Cohesion. A GEE analysis had been performed on GES Cohesion since the data were skewed and needed to be dichotomized, and this resulted in findings of beta = -.144 and p = .003. The negative beta meant that the probability of achieving a high score decreased in some manner from the beginning to the end of the mission. Although the shape of the logit curve was better described by a linear than a quadratic function, this did not imply that the actual data necessarily would plot as a straight line.

    Crewmember results versus scores from normative samples on Earth

    For nearly every subscale, the crewmember means were in the positive half of the subscale range. That is, where a high score defined a negative outcome (e.g., POMS Tension-Anxiety), the crew mean was below the scale midpoint (18 for this subscale), whereas where a high score defined a positive outcome (e.g., GES Cohesion), the crew mean was above the scale midpoint (4.5 for this subscale). This suggested that the crewmembers generally endorsed positive adaptive responses, especially for the POMS emotion variables.

    The normative means and standard deviations were determined from several thousand people participating in non-patient work and task groups. The POMS sample consisted of 1,130 adult males who participated in a smoking cessation program; the GES sample consisted of individuals who participated in 101 task-oriented groups in health care, correctional, and educational settings [41]; and the WES sample consisted of 8,146 individuals who participated in 116 general and health care work groups [42]. Using t-tests and the Benjamini and Hochberg procedure, a total of 13 subscales resulted in p-values that were below the adjusted significance threshold of p = .033. For all six significant POMS variables, the crewmembers endorsed significantly less dysphoria than the POMS normative sample. They also scored lower on GES and WES measures of group expressiveness, independence, anger & aggression, and innovation; and higher on measures of cohesion, leader control, and managerial control.

    The subscale means and standard deviations for Russian and American crewmembers were taken separately, along with their respective comparisons with the normative samples; a given difference did not always reach significance (probably due to the smaller sample sizes). One notable new finding is GES Self Discovery, where the Russian mean is significantly greater than the norm, and the American mean is significantly smaller.

    Mission versus pre-and post-mission results

    Crewmember subjects were asked to complete the POMS, GES, and WES questions each week during the time they were on the Mir. They also were asked to complete these measures for four consecutive weeks pre-mission, ending at one month before launch (i.e., baseline data collection period), and for two consecutive weeks after they returned to Earth. However, it was discovered that during the post-mission period the American and Russian subjects were separated from each other, so there was no group environment to evaluate using the GES and WES items. Consequently, only POMS data were collected. To look for differences in subscale scores before, during, and after the missions, mean scores were calculated and compared using a repeated measures ANOVA (pre-mission versus on-orbit versus post-mission for the POMS subscales, and pre-mission versus on-orbit for the GES and WES subscales). After applying the Benjamini and Hochberg procedure, none of these comparisons were significant in terms of all crewmembers, Russians alone, or Americans alone.

    CONCLUSION

    There was no support for the triphasic or linear models for all crewmembers or for Russian cosmonauts alone when the Benjamini and Hochberg criteria were applied to contol for Type I errors. Although there were three significant quadratic findings for American astronauts, only one variable, GES Order & Organization, showed a U-shaped pattern consistent with the triphasic model. This finding suggested that Americans in space felt that their interpersonal environment was comparatively more organized at the beginning and end of the on-orbit period than during the middle. This makes some sense because the new astronauts were formally oriented to the Mir shortly after arriving by the departing astronauts and by the continuing cosmonauts, and because the preparation for the return to Earth required the astronauts to undergo a number of specific procedures during the end of their on-orbit period.

    The other two significant quadratic functions for the Americans, GES Task Orientation and GES Self Discovery, gave a different pattern, with the probability of a high score being greater during the beginning weeks of the missions, then declining as time progressed. This pattem also was evident for the significant linear function finding for GES Cohesion. Taken together, the results from these three subscales suggested that the American crewmembers might have been impacted by the novelty of the situation during the first few weeks of their missions. They perceived the crew environment to be one where orientation to tasks was high, where they could grow individually, and where there was a strong bond among the crewmembers. Over time, there was a significant decrease in these areas. Perhaps the novelty of the situation simply disappeared. Alternatively, the decreases may have reflected an emotional change in the Americans due to homesickness or asthenia, although one would have expected to find some POMS changes as well if this explanation was valid.

    The above results were found only for American astronauts. The Russian estimated probabilities for high scores were in the .4 to .8 ranges for all four of the subscales shown in the figures, and they did not vary significantly over time. Thus, the cosmonauts were much more likely to endorse higher scores throughout the mission than their American counterparts. This difference may have reflected actual cultural differences or differences based on interpretations of the English and Russian versions of our measures. But it also might have reflected the specific interpersonal characteristics of the Shuttle/Mir missions, where there usually were two Russians and one American on-board, where the leader was always a Russian, where the Mir and its operation was in Russian control, and where the predominate language being spoken was Russian. Thus, an American astronaut was psychosocially isolated during these missions, and the U.S./Russian differences may have been more a reflection of the crew composition and operational situation than of characteristic cultural differences.

    First, although the estimated curves were significant, an inspection of the figures suggests that the estimates are not idea fits of the actual proportion data. The smooth estimated probability curves contradict the variation that is occurring in the mean weekly responses. Only biphasic, quadratic, and linear functions were tested, and perhaps some other function would give a better fit to the data. Second, for GES Order & Organization, GES Task Orientation, and GES Cohesion, the high/low splits were at 9/8. This means that about half of the total responses were the highest possible value of nine, and about half were eight and below. Therefore, in many cases, the indicated drop in scores was from a high of nine to a low of eight or seven, still in the upper part of the subscale range. In contrast, the high/low split for GES Self Discovery was 5/4, which gave more room at the top and bottom of the subscale range for the subjects to score. Little support was found for the presence of moderate to strong time effects in the crewmembers during the Shuttle/Mir missions.

    In comparison with other work and task groups on Earth, the crewmembers were significantly less dysphoric and perceived more cohesion and direction from their leaders and managers. They also felt that their group environment allowed them less opportunity to express themselves and to be independent and innovative, and they reported lower levels of group anger and aggression. American and Russian subjects taken separately generally echoed these overall findings. In addition, unlike many work environments on Earth, the Shuttle/Mir missions were busy and highly structured, with many timelines, procedures, and directions from the ground that needed to be followed. One interesting difference between Russian and American crewmembers pertained to GES Self Discovery, with the former scoring significantly higher than the normative sample and the latter Scoring significantly lower. Although the Russian crewmembers perceived their interpersonal environment as conducive to such discussion (compared with the normative group on Earth), the American crewmembers had an opposite perspective. Perhaps language differences and a sense of psychosocial isolation handicapped the latter and prevented them from learning about themselves through such informal group interactions. No significant differences were establish in crew response before, during, or after the missions in terms of all crewmembers or Americans and Russians taken separately.

    Significant pre/post seclusion differences using the same measures were found in our 135-day Mir simulator study, and we expected to replicate some of the findings. For example, POMS Tension-Anxiety levels were significantly lower, and GES Leader Support, Expressiveness, and Self Discovery levels were significantly higher during the Mir simulation seclusion period than before. However, the psychological meaning of this simulation was much different from the Shuttle/Mir missions in terms of anxiety and perceived danger, and one must be cautious in extrapolating the findings from earth-bound psychosocial simulation research into space.

    Crewmembers may be more comfortable with their work environment in space than mission control personnel are with their work environment on Earth, and it is possible that for astronauts and cosmonauts, the excitement of being in space offsets any negatives from living and working on earth. Thus, crewmembers perceived their work environment differently form people on Earth, and they demonstrated equanimity in mood and group perceptions, both in space and on the ground.
