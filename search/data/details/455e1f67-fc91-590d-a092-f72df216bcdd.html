Flight-Induced Changes in Immune Defense

Record ID: 9601210
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objectives:
Microbiological surveillance is an integral element of health care for astronauts and for verification of the safety of the spacecraft environment. Changes in both crew and environmental microbiota have been observed after landing relative to before flight. Contamination of the cabin environment and the probable exchange of opportunistic pathogens among crewmembers also have been reported. The success of future long-duration missions will depend on the health and productivity of the crewmembers, and thus outbreaks of infectious diseases during space flight must be prevented, or at least minimized. Since microorganisms will always be associated with crewmembers and thus with the spacecraft interior, it is imperative to understand the interaction of the crewmembers and their microbiota during space missions.

Brief and long-term space missions affect several human immune responses. Missions lasting ten days or less have been shown to reduce the absolute numbers of lymphocytes, eosinophils, and natural killer (NK) cells, and elevate the total numbers of neutrophils in the peripheral blood. Other cell-mediated immune (CMI) changes include reduced lymphocyte mitogenic response, diminished delayed-type hypersensitivity (observed during flight), altered T4/T8 ratios, and reduced production of interleukin-2 (IL-2). Evidence also exists that T-cell activation is blunted during hypoxia. United States data on NK cells is sparse, but one Russian study reported that the cytotoxicity index of cosmonauts in orbit between two and twelve months was reduced after landing relative to preflight measurements.

The aim of this experiment was to characterize the effects of spaceflight on selected immune elements that are important in maintaining an effective defense against infectious agents. Two specific aims tested the hypothesis that essential functions of neutrophils, monocytes, and cytotoxic cells (lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) and NK cells) will be altered during space flight.

Specific Aims:

  • Conduct functional analysis of neutrophils and monocytes before and after Space Shuttle missions.
  • Assess NK cell and LAK cell cytotoxicity before and after Space Shuttle missions.

Approach:
Preflight activities included collection of blood samples (one 10 ml collection each scheduled day) at launch minus 51 days (L-51) and L-10. No inflight activities were conducted for this experiment. Postflight activities were to include collections on landing day or return plus 0 days (R+0), R+3, and R+7. The following methods were performed to achieve the specific aims and test the hypothesis.

Neutrophil and Monocyte Functions

  • Chemotaxis - The chemotactic ability, or a cell's ability to move to or away from a chemical stimulus, of neutrophils and monocytes was assessed by means of the microplate fluorescent-dye uptake method.
  • Adhesion - The adherence of neutrophils and monocytes to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was measured using a fluorescent method similar to the chemotaxis method.
  • Degranulation - Degranulation and the subsequent generation of superoxide (O2•) was tested as an indicator of neutrophil phagocytosis. An Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) colorimetrically measured the O2• generated from the reduction of ferricytochrome c.
  • Phagocytosis - This was performed according to the protocol of Dr. John Schrieber's lab of Case Western University, Ohio. The protocol used opsonized Pseudomonas aeruginosa and then flow cytometry analysis. The opsonization process made the bacteria more susceptible to phagocytosis, the process by which the bacteria were engulfed and digested by cells as a defense against infection.

NK Cell Activities

  • Cytotoxicity - Cytotoxicity of NK or LAK cells was measured by 51Cr release from radiolabeled target cells. The chromium release indicated that the NK or LAK cells had killed/lysed the target cell. The target cell line was from an NK-sensitive chronic myelogenous leukemia cell line (K562).

Results:
Due to the STS-107 tragedy, this experiment was not completed. However, the Detailed Supplemental Objective (DSO) 498 followed a similar procedure.
