Development and Evaluation of Remote Manipulator System Operator Proficiency and Training Effectiveness Metrics

Record ID: NAG91486
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Remote manipulator systems (RMS) exist aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and the Space Shuttle to assist in space operations such as material management, transportation, unberthing, retrieval and berthing of payloads, and construction tasks. Each RMS is remotely controlled by an astronaut using two hand controllers (one translational and one rotational) and an interface panel while monitoring robot arm movement on closed circuit television displays. Prior to launch, astronauts undergo several phases of training to acquire the skill set necessary for successful operation of the arms. The early phases of training involve the use of generic dynamic skills training simulators. Although the training program offers instructors objective criteria for evaluating trainee progress, there are currently no quantitative operator performance data generated or recorded to aid instructors in their evaluations.

The objective of the current research was to identify and validate relevant quantitative measures of RMS operator proficiency in a format that enabled instructors, trainees, and trained operators to appraise current skill levels and to determine candidate areas for additional training or practice. The research will likewise identify metrics that can serve as in-flight readiness-to-perform indicators prior to critical operational tasks.

APPROACH:
The research began by augmenting the current operator proficiency criteria with additional metrics used to assess teleoperator performance obtained from a review of literature, task analysis, and interviews with trainers and trainees. The research continued by recording operator activity data and robot arm movement data from a number of participants at various stages of the training process. Instructor ratings were used to confirm differing levels of expertise along the training progression of the participants. Statistical analyses were used to isolate the measures of operator activity and robot arm movement that provided the best discriminability between expert and non-expert operators. Finally, displays of the selected measures were developed to aid instructors and trainees in assessing training progress and skill proficiency. Following incorporation into the training program, the methodology was validated against the present system of instructor evaluation of trainees. Future extensions of the work would focus on system- and mission-specific implementations of the metrics and application in a readiness-to-perform setting in flight.
