Nutrient Energy Requirements Determination

Record ID: ASTP013
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Apollo nutrition scientists recognized that understanding human energy requirements in microgravity was essential to understanding the overall ability of an astronaut to live and work productively in space. Researchers investigating energy requirements in microgravity were focused on quantifying crewmembers' energy intake and expenditure during flight, and associating these factors with measures of muscle mass and body composition. Ground-based research provided evidence that the best estimates of 1-G energy requirements were made on the basis of lean body mass (LBM); i.e., muscle mass. Accurate determination of LBM was obtained from a total body count of gamma radiation emitted from the natural occurrence of potassium-40 in the body. This method of determining LBM is based on the assumption that the potassium content of LBM is nearly constant and that body fat is essentially free of potassium.

As a result of Apollo and Skylab experimentation, researchers acquired data that showed relationships between ground-based and inflight energy requirements. Thus, it was predicted that the average daily inflight energy intake of the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project (ASTP) astronauts would be less than the intake of the Skylab astronauts because the ASTP mission was much shorter in duration, and therefore did not allow enough time for metabolic stabilization. On the basis of Skylab energy consumption data and ASTP total body potassium measurements, researchers predicted the energy required to maintain LBM during the ASTP mission. The objective of this investigation was to design a flight diet for the astronauts, based on the predicted energy requirements and LBM measurements, that would theoretically maintain the astronauts' LBM during the mission.

APPROACH:
Lean body mass determination: Lean body mass was determined by measurement of total body potassium-40 in the low-background radiation counting facility at the NASA Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center. Calibration for the technique was accomplished using potassium-42 since it emits beta rays having a maximum energy of 3.52 million electron volts (MeV) and a gamma ray having an energy of 1.525 MeV. The gamma ray energy is close enough to that of potassium-40 (1.46 MeV) to enable direct comparison of the photo peak areas for calibration purposes.

For calibration, the same amount of potassium-42 ingested by the subject was placed in a 500 ml bottle, and the bottle was filled with water. A weighed quantity of potassium nitrate was then placed in the same size bottle and dissolved in water. The solution was then diluted to the same volume as the potassium-42 solution. Counting measurements were made with the whole-body counter on each of the bottles and the subject.

A 30-minute count was performed on each subject. Before and immediately after counting each subject, a 5-minute count of background radiation and a 5-minute count of a 2.3 kg bottle of crystalline potassium chloride (KCl) were performed. The periodic count of KCl was used to correct any variations in overall counter efficiency. Averages were obtained for all counts, corrected for background radiation, and then reduced to counts per second. Total potassium was calculated and LBM was determined by use of appropriate constants and formulas.

Inflight food: Flight menus were chosen from crew-selected preferred foods and were designed to adhere to specified nutrient levels and meet individual energy requirements under normal gravity conditions. To meet the specified daily nutrient levels based on the LBM measurements, some of the beverages were fortified with either calcium lactate or potassium gluconate. Calcium-fortified beverages were limited to two per crewmember per day, whereas only one potassium-fortified beverage was required for each 4-day menu cycle. The crew selected a 4-day menu cycle as used on previous Apollo missions rather than the 6-day cycle used during the Skylab missions. In addition to the scheduled meals, a pantry containing beverages and snack foods was supplied. These foods could be used to substitute or supplement the normal meal items.

RESULTS:
In general, the crew was satisfied with the quality and quantity of the flight food provided. No gastrointestinal problems were reported during the mission. Appetites during flight were reported to be the same as during the preflight period.

The results of the whole-body potassium-40 counts performed on the subjects showed that subject A, subject B, and subject C had LBM measurements of 61.78, 65.14, and 70.95 kg, respectively. Predicted energy consumption based on the LBM measurements was 2822, 3241, and 2975 kcal/day for subjects A, B, and C, respectively. Therefore, in order to theoretically maintain LBM during the mission, an average daily caloric intake of 2820 kcal was provided for subjects A and C, while 3165 kcal was provided for subject B. Estimates of actual inflight food consumption based on daily reports indicated that averages of 2900, 2867, and 3000 kcal/day were consumed by subjects A, B, and C, respectively. Inflight weight losses for subjects A, B, and C were 0.45, 2.9, and 2.52 kg, respectively.

Postflight comments from the crew indicated that the potable water was of good quality. No out-of-specification conditions were noted in the microbiological and chemical analyses conducted. Preflight chlorination was accomplished 19 hours before launch. The level of chlorine measured 2 hours later was sufficient for microbial control. Inflight chlorinations were accomplished approximately on schedule, and no inflight problems were experienced. As in previous flights, some gas was present, particularly in the hot water. Postflight analyses indicated a lack of residual chlorine in the potable water. This deficiency remains unexplained since the records indicate that the last in-flight chlorination was accomplished 17 hours before landing. Chemical analyses of postflight samples showed all levels within specification limits. Microbiological results were positive for Flavobacterium species at levels of 105 microorganisms/ml.
