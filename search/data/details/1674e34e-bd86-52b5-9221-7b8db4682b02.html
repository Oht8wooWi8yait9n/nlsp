Soft Contact Lens Application Test

Record ID: DSO 427
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Ocular counterrolling is a principal measurement of otolith function in humans, and is consequently of great interest in the study of space motion sickness and adaptation to weightlessness. There has been success with ocular torsion analysis of video and photographs of eyes to which soft contact lenses with visible markings have been attached. Contact lenses have been frequently used to measure, by optical or mechanical means, the motions of the eye relative to an external frame of reference. Prior to this investigation, only rigid (hard) contact lenses had been used for this purpose. Hard lenses are usually poorly tolerated by the subjects unless a topical anesthetic is used, and the investigator must also maintain a large trial kit of lenses to fit all subjects.

The objective of this experiment was to determine the effectiveness of a method of application and fixation of specially marked hydrogel (soft) contact lenses to the eyes during orbital flight. If distilled water is introduced into the eye, the lens becomes adherent to the corneal surface for a five to ten minute time interval. Investigators aimed to quantitatively document the dependency of lens adhesion on factors such as lens diameter, thickness, construction type, extent of subject blinking, tearing, etc. so that the lenses might be used in torsion measurement.

This was done in 1g by "dehydrating" the lens-eye interface with sterile water. In zero-g KC-135 flights, there had been difficulties in achieving fixation, therefore a simple inflight demonstration was requested.

APPROACH:
The placement method called for mounting the lens in the eye, application of distilled water droplets and wicking away the excess. Two methods of irrigating the lenses, verified in KC-135 flights, were used. Method 1 called for holding the squeeze bottle in front of the eye and squirting water on the lens; the water forms a blob that covers the lens and sometimes the upper lids. Method 2 involved producing a blob of water on the end of the squeeze bottle and then bringing the blob up to the eye; the water rapidly transfers to the eye once it contacts the eye. Once the distilled water was in the eye it needed to be wicked away so that the contact lens could adhere to the cornea. A lint free paper towel (e.g. Kim Wipe) was held to the corner of the eye and put in contact with the water bolus. The surface of the contact lens may also have been dabbed to assure all the water had left the eye. Investigators wanted to test the best methods of irrigation and removal, as there was not sufficient sustained time on the KC-135 to do so. Both irrigation then removal of water, as well as simultaneous irrigation and removal were tested.

Once the lens was in the eye, investigators needed to know how long and how well the lens stuck to the eyeball. After waiting about 15 seconds, the crewmember attempted to move the lens once again, first by vigorous eye movements, then by pressing on the lens with his finger, to determine if it shifts position. When the lens was properly adhered, pressing on it rotated the entire eye and contact lens together, and the effect could be felt and seen in the mirror. The crewmember was asked to do this every 45 seconds to determine whether the lens slips freely, sticks slightly, or adheres rigidly to the eye. The lens was not expected to adhere any longer than 10 minutes under the best of conditions. To repeat the test, the lens was bathed in physiological saline solution and the process could be repeated without requiring reinsertion of the lens on the eye. The crewmember was asked to repeat the test using different amounts of water and different times of wicking away the excess water to achieve the best lens adhesion to the eye. After all tests were complete, the eye was irrigated with saline to loosen the lens, which was then removed.

RESULTS:
Inflight, the installation was extremely simple, but in spite of a wide range of maneuvers lasting several hours, the lens would not adhere using the prescribed procedures. After extraordinary difficulty, the subject managed to get a lens in place, confirming the results from KC-135 flights. The same was experienced postflight. Because of this, the concept was redesigned for use on subsequent flights; it was only after procedures were modified that reliable adherence would occur. It was hoped that the use of multiple small applications of water would improve performance in future flights.
