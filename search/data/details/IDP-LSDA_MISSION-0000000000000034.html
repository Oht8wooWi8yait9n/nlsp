Summary / Description

The Life and Microgravity Spacelab (LMS) flew aboard the Space Shuttle flight STS-78, which launched on June 20, 1996, from Kennedy Space Center, Florida. STS-78 marked the 78th flight of the American Space Shuttle and the 20th flight of the Shuttle named Columbia.

The LMS mission involved scientists and engineers from 10 countries; the United States, Switzerland, Sweden, Austria, Germany, Belgium, The Netherlands, Canada, Italy and France. The teams developed 41 experiments that studied the effects of gravity on the human body, the development of plants and animals, the processing of protein crystals and metallic alloys and the behavior of fluids in microgravity.

The LMS crew consisted of Commander Terence T. Henricks, Pilot Kevin R. Kregel, Flight Engineer Susan J. Helms, Mission Specialists Richard M. Linnehan, Charles E. Brady, Jr., and Payload Specialists Jean-Jacques Favier, and Robert Brent Thirsk. Alternate Payload Specialists were Pedro Duque and Luca Urbani. The crew demonstrated the international nature of the mission, representing the United States, Canada and France; alternate crewmembers were from Spain (representing ESA) and Italy.

NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center was responsible for the overall LMS mission management and for the microgravity investigations; the life sciences investigations were managed at the Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas. Four other space agencies joined NASA to support this mission: the Canadian Space Agency (CSA), Italian Space Agency (ASI), French Space Agency (CNES), and European Space Agency (ESA). Each space agency contributed hardware and supported different experiments.

The reasons to study human physiology in microgravity range from the obvious - ensuring astronaut health, to the less obvious - improving health care on Earth. The human body is designed to operate in Earth's gravity field. Our skeletal structure, muscles, tendons and ligaments support our weight against the constant pull of gravity. Other systems regulate the distribution of fluids, organize sensory input to provide balance and coordination and provide a rhythm for the operations of the body. When the gravitational load is removed from the human body, as it is when a person travels in space, many complex changes take place: bones and muscles become weaker, fluids shift toward the upper body, the daily rhythms of the body may be disrupted and a person may suffer from Space Adaptation Syndrome (SAS) until the body gets accustomed to the new environment. All of these research areas were addressed during the LMS mission. In addition, biology experiments collected data on the adaptation of other living systems to the microgravity environment.

Thirteen experiments conducted during the Life and Microgravity Spacelab mission were sponsored by U.S. research institutes. This research included studies in the field of neurovestibular adjustment, pulmonary function, physiology of muscles and bones, energy metabolism during space flight in humans and the effects of space flight on human performance, sleep and biological (circadian) rhythms. The biology investigations included studies on the development of the neural system in fish eggs, alterations in the metabolism of bone in rats and changes in the cell structure of conifer seedlings.

STS-78 was the eighth flight of the BRIC small payloads program. BRIC payloads consist of passive experiments contained within a metal canister placed in a middeck locker with foam padding used to dampen vibrations. The BRIC-08 payload was specifically designed to investigate the somatic embryogenesis of daylily plant cells. BRIC experiment specimens were exposed to the temperatures, carbon dioxide, oxygen, atmospheric pressure and humidity conditions of the middeck.

Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs) were also performed on this mission. A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation that is performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies are designed to require minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

During the microgravity science experiments flown on LMS, many gravitational effects on the production and manipulation of certain materials were investigated. Scientists studied interacting fluid layers, stability of liquid bridges and the behavior and properties of bubbles and drops of particular materials while they were suspended in a test chamber. In addition, they investigated the melting and resolidification of solids to learn more about the effects of gravity on high-temperature processing methods. Protein crystal growth research, studied before on previous Shuttle missions, continued on LMS with the growth of approximately 90 protein samples. Also, the inflight microgravity environment was characterized by measuring accelerations and velocities caused by the firing of the Shuttle's thrusters or actions of the crew.

After a record breaking 16 days, 21 hours, and 48 minutes, the Space Shuttle Columbia landed on July 7, 1996, at Kennedy Space Center. The research performed during the LMS mission has the potential to significantly enhance the quality of life on Earth. If the structure of certain proteins can be determined with the help of the space-grown crystals, advances can be made to improve the treatment of many human diseases. Space investigations in physiological processes may lead to remedies for loss of bone or muscle mass suffered by astronauts and people on Earth with osteoporosis. Electronic materials research in space may help refine manufacturing processes on Earth, leading to improved products, like computers, lasers and other high-tech devices. Using the unique environment of space to conduct science investigations in biology and physics increases scientists' understanding of these fundamental processes.
