Effects of High Versus Low Autonomy on Space Crewmember Performance (Autonomy)

Record ID: NNJ05HG01A
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
In future space missions to an asteroid or Mars, crewmembers will be given more autonomy to plan and carry out their activities due to the long distances involved and the tasks that will be needed to explore a planetary surface. The impact of increased autonomy on crew performance has not been evaluated formally. In addition, while the evidence is largely anecdotal, strict adherence to timelines sent from mission control has been reported to be fatiguing and stressful for astronauts. Furthermore, such stressors can affect interpersonal interactions, resulting in increased team conflict and reduced morale and productivity.

The long-term goal of this experiment was to conduct a scientific hypotheses-testing study involving a series of on-orbit missions to the International Space Station (ISS) in which the investigators measured the effects on crewmember mood, team behavior, cohesion, task performance, and the crew-mission control interaction of “high” autonomy conditions (where crewmembers plan much of their work schedule and perhaps experience a “Mars-like” communication delay with family members and mission control personnel back on Earth). This was contrasted with “low” autonomy conditions (where mission control personnel communicate in real-time with the crew and develop the work schedule, much as happens now). The impact of high versus low crew autonomy on mission control personnel was also be studied.

Before engaging in the flight experiment, the investigators were asked by NASA/JSC management to test the feasibility and safety of their methods during pilot studies involving a series of ground-based space analog missions (e.g., NEEMO, Houghton-Mars Project, Mars-500 105-day Simulation), where key elements of the flight experiment can be evaluated. Thus, the short-term goal was to conduct such feasibility pilot studies involving such missions. This is the purpose of the current “Autonomy” pilot project, which deals with NEEMO and HMP simulations. Under separate funding from NASA and the NSBRI, the investigators also studied the Mars-500 105-day Simulation, but this will not be reported here.

APPROACH:
The investigators conducted a series of pilot studies in this project: NEEMO 12 and 13, and the Haughton-Mars Project in 2008 (HMP 2008). In the series of pilot studies reported below, crewmember and mission control subjects filled out a weekly Study Questionnaire, which included mood and interpersonal interactions questions from the Profile of Mood States, the Group Environment Scale, and the Work Environment Scale, as well as a critical incident log. Additionally, new measures were developed and tested in the Mars 500 105-day Simulation but not on any of the simulations described in this report. The investigators had planned to test these in the NEEMO 14 mission, but this mission was postponed due to operational reasons unrelated to the research done on this experiment.

The independent variable was low versus high autonomy, and the dependent variables were scores on the measures of crewmember mood, team behavior, cohesion, task performance, and the crew-mission control interaction. It was not expected that autonomy would adversely affect mood, group interactions, and performance. In fact, it was expected that mood, group interactions, and performance would be the same or better during high autonomy periods.

RESULTS:
NEEMO
One study involved the NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations (NEEMO) program. The purpose of this program is to study human survival in the Aquarium underwater laboratory in preparation for future space missions. The Aquarius is located near Key Largo, Florida. Missions typically last 10 to 14 days and involve research and other activities conducted by 4 or more “aquanauts”, who are usually astronauts or other NASA personnel. These activities are conducted within the habitat or during simulated EVAs in the ocean. “Mission control” personnel located topside monitor crewmember progress and timeline the schedule.

The investigators studied two NEEMO missions: 12 and 13. NEEMO 12 consisted of two weeks of low autonomy, where the crewmember work schedules were timelined by the topside mission control. Subjects included three NASA crewmembers and 6 topside personnel. NEEMO 13 consisted of one week of low autonomy and one week of high autonomy (where the crew had more flexibility to plan their own work schedule). Subjects included four NASA crewmembers and eight topside personnel. At the end of each week, subjects completed a measure of mood [Profile of Mood States (PMS)], items from two measures of social climate [Group Environment Scale (GES), Work Environment Scale (WES)], and a critical incident log.

Due to the small number of data points, investigators could only analyze the data descriptively by examining change scores of the subscales of their measures from the first to the second week, separately for the two missions. Preliminary analyses suggested that the autonomy condition was successfully employed in NEEMO 13 with no adverse results. There seemed to be clear differences between the low and high autonomy weeks by the results, the crewmembers reported more direction from the mission commander (reflected by an increase in “Leader Control”) as well as a decrease in fatigue from the low to the high autonomy weeks during NEEMO 13 as compared to NEEMO 12, which only had low autonomy conditions. For the topside personnel, work pressure was stable in NEEMO 13, whereas it rose in the second week during NEEMO 12. However, confusion increased during the high autonomy second week of NEEMO 13 as compared to the second week of NEEMO 12 (where it declined), suggesting that the topside personnel were not as clear in their roles when the crew took over more of the schedule.

HMP 2008
A second pilot study involved some of the summer 2008 crewmembers working on the Haughton-Mars Project (HMP) on Devon Island in Canada. The location was an old, large impact crater which had similarities to the Martian surface in appearance and geological characteristics. People participating in this program dress up in space suits, travel in a Mars rover, and conduct tasks similar to those anticipated during an actual expedition to Mars. There is no formal “mission control” and the participants have great freedom to plan their activities each day. Hence, there is great autonomy in the work schedule.

Investigators studied the last three weeks of the 2008 mission. Eight non-NASA scientists and graduate students served as subjects. As in their previous work, they completed weekly POMS, GES, WES and critical incident log measures. Since they had no contrasting low autonomy period with which to compare their results, investigators decided to compare the subscale scores of the HMP subjects with those from published adult norms in the POMS, GES, and WES manuals, and with subscale scores from mission control subjects who participated in their previous on-orbit studies.

The HMP group reported greater levels of in-group leader direction and innovation and less need for outside direction as compared with other work groups on Earth, which presumably had more managerial control. These findings suggest that they were working under fairly high autonomous conditions. In addition, they reported being more cohesive than as compared with other work groups as well. They also reported being more tired than mission control groups, but this might have been an artifact of their working in the Canadian north in the summertime, when there is constant daylight and the work if more physically taxing than sitting in front of a mission control console. Taken together, these results suggest that people working under relatively high autonomous conditions are not adversely affected and may even bond together more cohesively than other work groups.

The results of these pilot studies suggest that in isolated and confined conditions that are meant to simulate those encountered during space missions, high autonomy is safe, well-received by crewmembers, and offers some potential advantages over low autonomy conditions. The crews have reported that they enjoy the high autonomy condition, and there have been no untoward effects to date. There have been some suggestions that high autonomy improves mood and cohesion, with more innovation and reliance on the crew leader. Outside mission monitors reported less work pressure but some confusion concerning their role. Clearly, these results are suggestive based on limited pilot data, but they point to the safety and possible advantages of high autonomy on crew functioning during actual space missions.
