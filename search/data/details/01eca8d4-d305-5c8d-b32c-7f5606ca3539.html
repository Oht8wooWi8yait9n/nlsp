Physical Activity for Better Sleep and Psycho-physiological State during Isolation

Record ID: Sleep_Isolation
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Sleep is a very important factor for human performance, health, and well-being. From space and isolation studies it is known that sleep impairments are a frequent problem and are known to result in different psycho-physiological problems. Sleep deprivation can have serious consequences on mood, mental well-being, and physical and psychological performance. To date, sleep problems in space and isolation studies are mainly treated with medication causing side effects such as pharmacological interactions, sleepiness, and lassitude influencing mood and cognitive performance. Consequently, is desirable to find alternative methods to maintain and/or improve sleep, and to counteract mood and cognitive impairments leading to performance errors, as well as crew conflicts during space missions and other isolated environments. Previous sleep and exercise studies revealed that physical activity is a sufficient tool to improve sleep. This study aimed to investigate the effect of isolation on sleep, mood, cognition, neurotrophic factors, and brain cortical activity during 30 days of isolation in NASA’s Human Exploration Research Analog (HERA) facility.

This study had the following specific aims:

  1. Determine if people with low amount of physical activities suffer to a higher extent from sleep problems, mood, and cognitive impairment in isolation than people with moderate or high physical activity.
  2. Determine if the effect of physical exercise and its possible impact on sleep quality, mood, and cognitive performance go along with changes in brain activity.
  3. Determine if the effect of physical exercise and its possible impact on sleep quality, mood, and cognitive performance go along with changes of stress and sleep hormones as well as neurotrophic factors.

APPROACH:
Sixteen subjects were isolated inside the HERA habitat for 30 days. Seventeen ground-based control subjects without isolation were tested simultaneously at the German Sport University in Cologne. Both groups were asked to exercise on a daily basis during the 30 days of the interventions. Throughout the interventions participants wore wrist belt actigraphs to assess total light exposure during sleep, sleep efficiency, and wake time after sleep onset. Participants completed a sleep diary and the self-assessment questionnaire for Sleep and Awakening quality (SSA), the Positive Affect and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS-X), and cognitive tasks before, during, and after the missions.

RESULTS:
Investigators discovered that isolation, sleep, mood, and cognition were not impaired although high levels of cortisol suggest increased levels of stress during the 30 days of isolation. The maintenance of sleep quality through a daily exercise routine during isolation may positively affect central nervous system function and structural markers. Brain cortical activation and neurotropic factors were not impaired during isolation.
