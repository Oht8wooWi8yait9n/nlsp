Radioisotope to Enable X-Ray Based Inflight Space Radiology

Record ID: 80NSSC24K0949
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

Objective:
In-flight medical capabilities are required for long-term spaceflight missions, including lunar and Mars misson. This study investigated an approach to implement inflight x-ray imaging that overcome the power, expense, volume, and mass restraints. Common medical conditions on Earth, like thrombi, renal stones, and bone fractures, to name a few, can also happen during spaceflight missions. Having radiology capabilities available can lessen the risk of adverse outcomes from these health concerns. This study investigated an approach to implement inflight x-ray imaging in modern spaceflight vehicles. Due to limited space, mass limitations, and kilowatt (kW) power stipulations for x-ray imaging, this technology it is not available currently in space. This study adapted existing x-ray technology with modern intensifying screens and digital imaging technology. In the resource-limited environment of a spacecraft, the investigators considered radioisotope as an x-ray (XR) source to eliminate the kW power requirements for XR generation, enabling XR radiographs, and computed tomography (CT) scans.
The investigative team accomplished this objective using the following specific aims:
 
 
1.      Radioisotope source: Selected and obtained a suitable radioisotope source design that included encapsulation and protective shielding to migrate radiation exposure to crew members and other spacecraft systems.
2.      X-ray imaging system design: Designed a compact and lightweight x-ray imaging system that is compatible with the radioisotope source for use in the ground demonstration.
3. Testing and validation: Conducted ground-based testing to validate the safety, performance, and reliability of the radioisotope x-ray source and imaging system. 

Approach:
Investigators evaluated radioisotopes with intrinsic radiation suitable for medical imaging, including Co-57, Tm-170, and Cs-137. Tm-170 was the original focus of the project because it emits radiation at clinically relevant energies (e.g., 84 keV photopeak), and USNC(Ultra Safe Nuclear Corporation) has a license to use it. However, Tm-170 was not available in large activities. Co-57 was a convenient stand-in for Tm-170, and it was convenient to use because it can be possessed in activities of 100 uCi or less without a radioactive materials license. These sources were combined with several detection techniques, including a dark box containing a scintillation screen and camera, a lens tube attached to a DSLR camera with the source attached to Gadox phosphor (gadolinium oxysulfide), and direct coupling of the source to a dental x-ray sensor.

Results:
Images were successfully captured using Tm-170 and the dark box configuration, as well as Co-57 and Cs-137 and the lens tube. Cs-137 was also able to produce an image using the dental sensor, but the image was vastly improved by using a clinical x-ray source. This study was terminated due to PI institution going out of business, before final data collection report was completed.
