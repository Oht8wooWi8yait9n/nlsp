Crew Medical Restraint System

Record ID: CMRS
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Inflight postural changes follow predictable trends and are relevant to the design of inflight crew systems. The neutral body posture in weightlessness results in neck flexion. This position should be accommodated by crew restraints in work and sleep stations. Crewmembers testing a conventional crew medical restraint system (CMRS) on STS-40 noted significant discomfort with being restrained in an Earth-normal recumbent position.

APPROACH:
The redesigned CMRS hardware consisted of a patient restraint surface similar in design to a medical backboard. The CMRS measured 56 x 14 inches and weighed about 36 pounds. The patient restraint surface contained both patient and care provider restraint straps. Patient restraint methods included a V-harness, upper and lower torso straps, and a head restraint. The restraint system was constructed out of an extremely rigid plastic that would provide effective electrical isolation for the crew and equipment during defibrillation. The board had folding legs, seat tracks and track anchors. The tracks and anchors were used to attach the restraint system to a payload rack inside the Spacehab module. The CMRS was unpacked and secured to ISS seat tracks and anchors installed inside Spacehab to emulate a medical emergency inside the ISS. For stowage, the CMRS could be folded into a configuration that measured 28 x 14 x 5 inches.

Three different medical situations were simulated. In the first scenario, one care provider deployed the CMRS and secured a patient to the surface. The procedures were repeated in scenarios' two and three using two care providers. In addition, the CMRS was used to transport the patient to the Shuttle middeck, where crewmembers exchanged roles and transported the new patient back to the Spacehab. The session was videotaped.

RESULTS:
The CMRS was flown and operated during the STS-81 mission. The CMRS experiment was successfully flown and operated in the Spacehab module. Each of the three deployment scenarios were accomplished in less than one minute, well within the requirement specifications. This included the time needed for a medical care provider to secure the test subject to the restraint surface. Transportation of the patient attached to the CMRS from Spacehab to Shuttle middeck simulated the transfer from ISS to a Shuttle or the Assured Crew Return Vehicle (ACRV). It was determined that the CMRS seat track connectors should be attached to the ISS seat track before a CMRS deployment attempt; the crew found deployment to be considerably improved when connectors were already installed). An evaluation of the restraint effectiveness for both the patient and care provider resulted in several recommendations for future updates, such as the recommendation that arm straps be incorporated into the design to completely secure the patient for transfer (the crew had difficulty with this task on orbit). The CMRS is a safe and reliable restraint system to stabilize and treat ill or injured crewmembers in a microgravity environment. This experiment demonstrated the successful design and deployment of medical emergency hardware. The CMRS was certified and delivered for operational use on ISS beginning in June 1999.
