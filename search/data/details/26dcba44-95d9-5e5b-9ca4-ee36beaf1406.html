Measuring, Monitoring, and Regulating Teamwork for Long Duration Missions

Record ID: NNX13AM77G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:

One of the critical human performance challenges for long-duration space missions is the maintenance of good team interaction processes in isolated, confined, and extreme (ICE) environments. The purpose of this research was to understand the factors that facilitate and maintain good team “processes” (e.g., collaboration and cohesion) that support effectiveness in ICE settings. There is considerable research that supports the relationship between team processes and work effectiveness. However, most of the substantial research on this topic has adopted a cross-sectional research design where a set of team member characteristics (i.e., personality, demographics) are collected at a single time point along with a set of team processes such as team cohesion, collaboration, coordination, and team effectiveness ratings. Although such data are informative of team functioning, they are unable to capture human interaction dynamics. This experiment was focused on understanding the factors that influence these dynamic cycles of interaction and, ultimately, to learn how to help team members maintain good collaboration, cohesion, and effectiveness.


There were three specific aims of this research:

  1. Benchmark long-duration team functioning in ICE analog environments.
  2. Extend engineering development of an unobtrusive monitoring technology (i.e., a wearable wireless sensor package).
  3. Develop teamwork interaction metrics and regulation support systems. In particular, investigators will be testing the development of the monitoring technology using the benchmarking data they have collected in previous lab and field studies to guide how they capture and analyze interactions in the Human Exploration Research Analog (HERA) environment. They will be using various data collection methods to achieve these aims - daily diaries, event based sampling on interactions, and unobtrusive monitoring devices. Combined, these data will allow investigators to benchmark team cohesion and performance dynamics over the duration of the mission in an analog environment.


APPROACH:


This research was designed to collect data from team members in a confined environment. Investigators asked the participants to provide surveys before and after the mission regarding their demographics, expectations for the mission, previous experience, how they felt about the HERA mission experience, etc. They utilized event sampling methodology (interval contingent, event contingent, and signal contingent) in order to capture the experiences, affect, and team processes of individuals at various times of the day. Investigators also utilized monitoring technology to capture various physical and physiological indicators (e.g., heart rate, motion, sound intensity) to be used in understanding team processes. Finally, they had access to other behavioral data collected by the facility such as audio recordings and video data in order to compare their data to and to use for further analyses that were available through the generic HERA protocol.


RESULTS:

Across the benchmark observations, data patterns, and analyses, a pattern of evidence was seen demonstrating that team functioning is substantially and significantly challenged by the persistent stressors of ICE environments and that the breakdown in team functioning can be detected and tracked by the TIS IDA metric. First, the 3 teams in the HI-SEAS mission simulation (the longest duration ICE environment at 8 to 12 months) each suffered a desynchronization of social cohesion in the 5-to-7-month range, indicating that the challenges of long duration team functioning eventually begin to take a toll . Even in the limited volume of the HERA habitat, the TIS IDA metric demonstrated the capability to distinguish different patterns of intra- and inter-team interactions. Third, across a systematic series of analyses conducted on HI-SEAS team functioning, the IDA was (a) shown to relate to social cohesion, (b) the network metrics of density and distance derived from the IDA significantly mirrored the discontinuous fracture pattern evidenced in social cohesion ratings, and (c) density and distance significantly predicted cohesion separation patterns. This body of findings provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of the TIS as a means to unobtrusively track team functioning for long duration space missions.

Strong auto-regressive relations among constructs were found from the ESM ratings. This means that on average, over time, relationships were stable such that cohesion, conflict, and performance perceptions on a given day were predictive of cohesion, conflict, and performance perceptions on the next day. Cohesion and performance perceptions were positively related from one day to the next, whereas cohesion and performance with conflict was negatively related from one day to the next. In some instances, conflict management efforts on a given day mitigated conflict on the next day, but that finding was inconsistent across cases. Across ICE analogs, social cohesion was a key indicator of team functioning. One feature across analogs that was of particular research interest was mission duration. Longer missions would be expected to subject participants to more cumulative stressors with potentially negative influences on team functioning. Although there were some interesting dynamics across all the analog environments, no compelling patterns were found that challenged team functioning in the shorter duration missions. For the longer duration missions, a generalizable pattern of decline in team functioning across HI-SEAS missions exceeding 6 months in duration was observed.

Overall variance increased, and the trend of social cohesion a negative slope. This pattern was observed in Missions 3,4 and 5. It was noted that at the start of each of the missions, the teams evidenced low variability and high ratings of social cohesion. However, as destabilization manifested, social cohesion was no longer consensual, and the patterns indicate individual variance in social cohesion perceptions. Social cohesion was no longer team level, and although the factors that precipitated these patterns appeared to differ across teams, the patterns themselves are highly similar. This shows that long durations teams subjected to ICE conditions have high potential to manifest negative effects on team functioning. 

The project resulted in important developments and some critical findings. First, given the relative absence of well-designed longitudinal observational studies of long duration ICE team functioning, it examined a range of ICE team types (i.e., Antarctica and NASA mission simulations) across a range of durations (i.e., 1 week to 52 weeks). Although all types of teams and durations evidenced variation in functioning, the most noteworthy observation was the destabilization and decline of social cohesion for missions longer than 6 months in HI-SEAS. Second, focusing on the HERA and HI-SEAS environments, we provided a series of analyses using the TIS and IDA metric to demonstrate that the destabilization and declines in daily social cohesion ratings that were self-reported by participants were significantly captured by the IDA metric. This provides compelling field-based evidence that the unobtrusive data captured by the TIS can be used to monitor individual and team functioning for long duration missions. Third, the TIS technology was extended and transferred to the NASA WEAR Lab where a redesigned technology platform is under development. Finally, a system was created with architecture and notional dashboard designs so that TIS data may be used to help long duration astronaut team members self-manage their interactions and team functioning to maintain effectiveness.
