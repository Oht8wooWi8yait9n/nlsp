Effects of Skeletal Unloading and Vitamin E on Bone in Mature Rats

Record ID: NAG91159
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Bone loss is a critical concern for crewmembers in space. Free radicals have been shown to affect bone in vivo and in vitro by stimulating osteoclasts and increasing the rate of bone resorption. In space, increased exposure to radiation increases the possibility of oxidative damage to bone, and antioxidant nutrients may protect the skeletal tissue.

Rodent hindlimb unloading (HLU) is an alternative method to space flight for investigating microgravity-induced physiological changes. HLU produces a deficit in bone formation, lowers the number of osteoblasts, and has been used as a model for examining bone changes in simulated weightlessness.

This ground-based study evaluated the potential of vitamin E for preventing bone loss associated with weightlessness in the HLU rat model. The long-term goal was to identify an optimal dose of vitamin E to reduce the risk of bone loss and fractures under conditions of weightlessness. The hypothesis was that the dietary antioxidant vitamin E would reduce deterioration of bone in a dose-dependent manner when the bone loss was due to weightlessness and, possibly, oxidative stress. There were two specific aims. One was to investigate whether vitamin E prevents the loss of bone mineral density and deterioration of biomechanical properties in the HLU rat model in a dose-dependent manner. The other specific aim was to examine some of the underlying mechanisms by which vitamin E exerts bone-protective effects using histomorphometric, biomechanical and biochemical analyses.

APPROACH:
Eight and one-half-month-old rats were assigned to one of three diets containing 15, 75 or 500 International Units (IU) of dl-alpha tocopherol, a form of vitamin E. These amounts were low, recommended or high for this vitamin intake. The rats were assigned to either ambulatory (AMB) or HLU conditions. Diets began nine weeks prior to the start of the hindlimb unloading period and the rats were either HLU or AMB for four weeks. At eight days and at one day prior to necropsy, rats were injected with calcein for dynamic bone histomorphometric analysis. They were scanned using a small animal software package prior to unloading and at the end of the treatment period. Rats were anesthetized for scanning and blood was drained by cardiac puncture. Serum was then analyzed for various markers. Age at necropsy was approximately eleven months.

Mechanical strength of the femur was assessed with three-point bending. The femur was placed on two rods spaced 20 mm apart and force was delivered to the mid-shaft. The fourth lumbar vertebral body (L4) was prepared with parallel flat ends and placed on a fixed lower pressure plate while an upper plate moved downwards at a rate of three mm/min to cause compression in the front to rear direction in order to conduct compression analysis. Load displacement plots were analyzed to determine material properties. The average cross section for each vertebral body was used to calculate its ultimate stresses and Young's modulus, which is a number representing the ratio of stress divided by strain. Histomorphometric analysis of the femur produced multiple measurements.

Bone mineral area, bone mineral content, and bone mineral density (BMD) of the excised fifth lumbar vertebra (L5) and of the left femur were estimated. Femurs and the third lumbar vertebra were ashed, and the mineral residue was dissolved in concentrated nitric acid and diluted for analysis of calcium, magnesium, iron, copper and zinc.

RESULTS:
Results were divided into two parts. HLU and AMB conditions were compared and dose-dependent effects of vitamin E on bone parameters of interest under both conditions were evaluated. Significant effects of near weightlessness were seen in 11-month old rats. Both the L5 and femur BMD were significantly lowered in HLU animals compared to AMB controls. Lower BMDs in the HLU group were accompanied by reduced structural properties. The material properties of L4, ultimate stress, yield stress, and Young's modulus of elasticity were significantly decreased by HLU. Histomorphometric data indicated that HLU decreased the rate of bone formation and increased the rate of bone resorption. Serum markers indicated an increase in tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase activity, a marker of bone resorption, but no change in either marker of bone formation (alkaline phosphatase activity or osteocalcin). The findings confirmed that deleterious effects on bone of older animals due to HLU were similar to the deleterious effects on younger, growing animals. However, alterations in bone metabolism that result in bone loss of mature animals appeared to more closely mimic the metabolic bone changes occurring in crewmembers in space.

Dietary treatments were effective in creating differences in serum vitamin E concentrations even though diet was not begun until the rats were eight and one-half months old. The diet produced significant differences in ferric reducing ability of serum and strong trends for decreased malondialdehyde, a product of lipid peroxidation, in the liver. Both ferric reducing ability of serum and liver malondialdehyde are indicators of antioxidant status. In HLU animals, high intakes of vitamin E had a positive effect on material properties of vertebrae measured by increased yield stress and by Young's modulus of elasticity. The positive effects of the high vitamin E intake were not observed in AMB controls. Histomorphometry data indicated that higher doses of vitamin E produced positive effects on bone volume parameters in HLU rats. Contrary to expectations, the same doses of vitamin E had negative effects on these histomorphometric parameters in AMB controls. Serum alkaline phosphatase, a marker of bone formation, increased with increased vitamin E in the diet.

Data indicated that higher doses of vitamin E improved skeletal health. Higher bone volume and biomechanical properties in HLU animals were observed with the increased dietary vitamin E. Contrary to expectations, vitamin E at higher doses was found to be deleterious to bone in AMB control animals. Research previous to this study showed that higher doses of vitamin E were beneficial in aged mice but had no beneficial effects on the bone of younger mice. It is possible that the bone of older animals or of those in HLU conditions benefit from more vitamin E, particularly if the HLU resulted in greater oxidative damage.

Research of the mechanisms of vitamin E in mature bone is critical to understanding its role in bone health. There is the potential for vitamin E to protect against oxygen derived from free radicals. In the mature, 11-month old rats of this study, dietary vitamin E did not produce significant effects on bone mineral area, bone mineral content or BMD. Since dietary changes are cumulative over time and subtler than the bone response was to HLU, the bone mineral area, bone mineral content and BMD might not be apparent during the relatively brief time course of this experiment. Nutrition studies may require extended depletion or supplementation periods before HLU to enable differences to be observed during the relatively short HLU period. Additional data may be obtained through studies that analyze markers over time. Time series studies examining markers of bone resorption and also changes in gene expression due to HLU and nutritional or pharmacological interventions could provide insight into alterations in bone metabolism.
