Effects of Microgravity on Cell Mediated Immunity and Reactivation of Latent Viral Infections

Record ID: DSO 497
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This study proposed to study the effects of space flight cell mediated immunity and reactivation of latent viral infections from blood and urine samples. This study comprised Dr. Stowe's Ph.D. thesis research. This study addressed the mechanisms of the decreased immune function from space flight and characterized the replication of latent viruses. Studies outlined in this project simply use those components of the blood that would have been discarded otherwise from the Medical Operations blood draws for Shuttle Missions and for annual medical evaluations. Urine and blood samples were collected during the regular medical operation (MedOps) by a registered Medical Technologist following standard venipuncture procedures from crewmembers. The blood draw was collected ten days pre-flight, at landing, and three days post-flight as well as during the annual medical evaluations.

The hypothesis of this study was that the combined effects of microgravity along with associated physical and psychological stress would decrease cell-mediated immunity and reactivate latent viruses resident in peripheral blood leukocytes (e.g., EpsteinBarr virus), and that they may also be released into plasma and urine (e.g., Cytomegalovirus).

APPROACH:
Medical Operations Crew data from the pre and postflight physicals and the annual medical evaluation was requested for correlation with data obtained from DSO 497: (1) the CBC and differential counts for each crewmember, (2) the immunoglobulin levels, and (3) hormones (cortisol, epinephrine, norepinephrine, ADH, and adrenocorticotrophic hormone) measured in plasma/urine. Also, viral titers on the first annual medical evaluation as requested to compare to flight values. Physical and psychological stress, which has been shown to reactivate latent viruses, may be characterized by the stress hormones (listed above). Analysis of leukocyte subpopulations postflight are necessary to correlate these results with changes in viral shedding. Immunoglobulin levels were used to indicate previous/past infection, and some latent viruses (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus) infect B-cells resulting in increased IgM/lgG levels. The expected results are that increases in stress hormones will reactivate latent EBV-infected B-lymphocytes and cause polyclonal expansion of this cell population. This cell population in turn will be detected using PCR and FISH/Flow cytometry.

RESULTS:
A significant decrease (p <.05) in the anti-EBNA IgG was found at L-10 as compared with baseline. Anti-EBNA antibodies further decreased (p <.01) after flight (R+0 and R+3) as compared with baseline values. Anti-VCA was significantly increased (p <.01) at all three time points (L-10, R+0, and R+3) as compared with baseline. No significant difference was found in anti-measles antibodies at any time point. The control group showed no significant change between baseline and day 15 samples in either the log2 VCA IgG antibody titers or the log2 EBNA IgG antibody titers. In addition, no change was found in the antibody titers to the non-latent measles virus.
