Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The NASA Specialized Center of Research and Training (NSCORT) Resource-Recovery (R2) research group consisted of nine projects with 11 principal investigators. In the four years of Center operation, the research investigators have successfully completed a number of project objectives. Highlights of their research accomplishments are summarized as follows.

The “Proof of Concept” was established for the simultaneous treatment of graywater and gas loaded with ammonia and hydrogen sulfide in the BREATHe I prototype. Key design parameters such as size, recirculation rates, media type and flow rates for BREATHe I were optimized. The biodegradability of surfactants was assessed, surfactant degradation byproducts were screened and metabolite biodegradation potential analyzed.

In BREATHe II, the feasibility of biofiltration technology was established for treating multi-component contaminated air representative of major cabin air trace contaminants during long-duration space missions. Desirable biofiltration operating strategies in terms of packing media type and bioreactor configuration were recommended. A mathematical model was developed for the BREATHe II process. A trace contaminant lab established in year 4 was set up for on-line evaluation of biofilter outlet gases.

Independent and combined effects of UV radiation and iodine on water disinfection using Bacillus subtilis spores as the challenging microorganism have been investigated. Research endeavor in membrane processes in ALS contributed to the understanding of how the MF pretreatment membrane and NF polishing membrane can be modified and synthesized to improve wastewater recovery efficiency and mitigate membrane fouling.

APPROACH:
The NSCORT Resource Recovery group has maintained a high-level of collaborative interaction throughout the 4-year research period, which is essential since the projects are significantly interrelated. Also, significant research collaborations between NASA field center scientists and NSCORT R2 group PIs were established. Student involvement with the research projects has been extensive and has resulted in the training and graduation of numerous graduate and undergraduate students. Research findings from this group have resulted in a plethora of publications both in conference proceedings and peer-reviewed journals. In conclusion, the group has successfully completed the resource recovery research projects and made significant progress toward NSCORT research objectives.

RESULTS:
In human-inhabited environments that incorporate recirculation and reuse of water and air, control of pathogenic microorganisms is critical. Ultraviolet (UV) radiation has been demonstrated to be effective for disinfection of water in these settings, in that UV-based systems provide broad-spectrum antimicrobial behavior with minimal disinfection by-product formation; these systems also have low requirements for power and space. These same attributes suggest that UV-based systems may be effective for control of airborne microbial pathogens.

This project involved three basic components. First, existing literature information suggests that the UV dose-response behavior of airborne microorganisms is influenced by relative humidity; however, the experiments that have reported this behavior to date have been based on crude systems that do not allow for precise determination of several critical factors in the experiment, including UV dose delivery. Investigators have developed an experimental method that allows for precise measurement of UV dose delivery, as well as the response of microorganisms to UV exposure. Moreover, this system allows for precise control of relative humidity and temperature, so that unambiguous results can be obtained. This system was used to characterize and quantify UV dose-response behavior of several relevant microorganisms as a function of relative humidity. These dose-response data were then used as input to a numerical model developed based on the combined application of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and UV intensity (I) field models. Investigators have used this CFD-I modeling approach successfully to design a highly efficient UV-based reactor for disinfection of water; the application to air disinfection is a natural extension of this modeling approach. The CFD-I modeling work allowed investigators to identify an efficient reactor design through the process known as numerical prototyping. Based on these results, a physical prototype of the reactor was built and constructed to validate the reactor’s performance, as well as the predictions of the CFD-I model.
