Summary / Description

The Gemini program objectives met the purpose of bridging gaps between the Mercury and Apollo programs by increasing the operational efficiency and knowledge of technology in manned space flight. Specifically, the following accomplishments distinguished the Gemini program: (1) investigation of the medical and technicological aspects of long duration flight; (2) development of rendezvous techniques and postdocking maneuvers between two spacecraft; (3) development of reentry flight path control; (4) attainment of flight and ground crew proficiency; (5) development of extravehicular capability; and (6) in-depth scientific experimentation.

The first of the Gemini flights, Gemini 3 (G-3), was intended to test the maneuverability and performance of the new spacecraft, the first equipped with an on board guidance computer, in a three orbit flight. Gemini consisted of two modules: the reentry module, of similar configuration to but slightly larger than Mercury, with pressurized cabin, reentry control, and rendezvous and recovery sections; and the adapter module, with retrorockets and equipment. The complete spacecraft weighed about 8,000 lb at launch and measured approximately 18 by 10 feet; the reentry module was 11 by 7.5 feet in size. The atmosphere was at 258 mm Hg and 100% oxygen. A modified Titan Intercontinental Ballistic Missile was used to launch the Gemini 3 crew into orbit.

The two Gemini 3 astronauts, Command Pilot Virgil I. Grissom and Pilot John W. Young, became the first to manually steer their craft in flight. They tested the new thrusters and maneuvering system. During reentry another newly developed system, called the automatic water expulsion system, was tested. It ejected water in a specially engineered system so that the ionization radio blackout was counteracted, and the ground monitoring stations were able to pick up the craft’s radio signals and track the craft during reentry through the Earth’s atmosphere.

Continuous, real-time physiological information on all Gemini crewmembers was monitored via telemetered data and voice transmissions. Data was provided by a two-lead electrocardiogram, an impedance pneumograph measuring respiration, an oral thermistor measuring body temperature, and a blood pressure measuring system. All of this hardware was mounted on a biosensor harness that fit under the astronauts’ flight suits. Additionally, some inflight film footage was utilized for postflight assessment of crew health during space flight.

Two biological science experiments were flown on Gemini 3. One managed by Ames Research Center (ARC), a study of Sea Urchin Egg Growth Under Zero-G, was not successfully completed during the flight due to hardware malfunction. The second, a study of the Synergistic Effect of Zero-G and Radiation on White Blood Cells, was affiliated with the Atomic Energy Commission and completed successfully.

Gemini 3 accomplished its objectives to test and prove the spacecraft’s maneuverability, life-support systems reliability, and the new method of controlled reentry. It led the way for later and longer duration flights, which in turn carried valuable life science experiments.
