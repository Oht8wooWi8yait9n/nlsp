Predicting and Assessing Postural Reentry Disturbances

Record ID: NNJ04HI07G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
This study investigated a technique for predicting the severity of re-entry disturbances of posture and locomotion experienced by astronauts in space flight relevant environments and developing techniques for attenuating them. We are concerned not only with return to earth from orbital flight but also transfer to other force environments astronauts may encounter in the future, such as moon gravity, a rotating artificial gravity vehicle, or Mar’s gravity. The investigators developed a hold and release (H&R) paradigm and mathematical model for quantifying multi-link postural performance, investigating the influence of flight-related factors and understanding neuromotor mechanisms. The linear model they developed of upright posture as a four link inverted pendulum in a regime of small oscillations, a H&R experimental paradigm for producing transient postural oscillations, and data showing that the model fits the postural responses to H&R. The fitted parameters discriminate normal subjects and vestibular loss patients. This approach provides a way of quantifying astronauts’ postural changes following re-entry, in terms of the latency of EMG responses, and characteristic stiffness and damping of the ankles, knees and hips. It may also provide a means of assessing the efficacy of countermeasures while astronauts are still in-flight.

Specific Aims:

  • Measure postural responses to the hold and release technique in parabolic flight during conditions of weightlessness with the aid of a hold-down device and during high force levels
  • Measure postural responses to the hold and release technique before and after adaptation to artificially increased or decreased body weight
  • Measure postural responses to the hold and release technique before, during, and after exposure to increased gravitioinertial force level in a rotating room

    RESULTS:
    Hold & Release in Parabolic flight:
    The aim of the study was to quantify recovery from simulated falls in altered force backgrounds and to investigate the mechanisms of recovery, using the H&R paradigm. The investigators tested three healthy men and two healthy women ranging in age from 26 to 60. The experiment was conducted during the 0g and 1.8g phases of parabolic flight as well as during 1 g straight and level flight and 1 g on the ground pre- and post-flight. 0g trials were conducted with 50% body weight bungee cord loading. Subjects were tested both with their eyes open and closed in all force backgrounds. During testing, subjects stood feet side by side with the soles of their shoes anchored to a force plate attached to the deck of the aircraft. Loss of balance was elicited using the H&R paradigm. A horizontal force was applied to the subject’s sternum, which he or she actively resisted. Removing the force caused subjects to sway forward, and they had to react to prevent falling. The exact time of release of the holding force was measured with a load cell. Motion of the head, shoulder, waist, knee and ankle were monitored with an OPTOTRAK, and surface EMGs were collected from tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, biceps femoris, and rectus femoris muscles.

    The kinetic and kinematic data showed the characteristic features of the H&R paradigm: center of foot pressure initially remains unchanged after release as body momentum develops and then the gastrocnemius and biceps femoris muscles are activated and produce torques that move the center of pressure in front of the center of mass thus arresting the forward momentum of the body. The earliest EMG response was a simultaneous activation of gastrocnemius and biceps femoris at 50-60 ms in 1g. This initial reaction was significantly longer in 0g and shorter in 1.8g, but vision did not affect it. A second synergy involved tibialis anterior and rectus femoris, and it occurred later than 200 ms in 1g. The onset latency of the secondary reaction was significantly reduced in the presence of vision but was not affected by g level. The latencies of both synergies differed in the sequential 1g test periods. That is, latencies were longer in 1g in flight than 1g pre-flight and got longer still in 1g post-flight. The rate of body sway prior to EMG responses was not correlated with g level. This study shows that the H&R paradigm is feasible for postural evaluations in hypo-g and hyper-g force backgrounds as well as in 1g. G-related modulation of the earliest corrective EMG responses could be due to altered vestibulo-spinal modulation of muscle spindle gain, as a function of background force level. Later EMG responses to induced falls are under the control of vision.

    Hold & Release During Galvanic Stimulation
    To assess vestibular influences on recovery of balance during sudden falls, the investigators measured the postural responses of five healthy subjects to a hold and release perturbation coupled with galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS). Two electrode pairs were located with the anterior electrode of each pair over the mastoid process and the posterior electrode over the trapezius muscle on the same side. Bilateral unipolar GVS was generated when a holding force against the sternum was released and the subject began moving forward, by individually driving left and right electrode pairs as cathode-anode or vice versa at 1 ma for 12 seconds or 2 ma for 6 seconds. The investigators computed the frequency and damping parameters of a multi-link inverted pendulum model of the body which best fit the transient postural oscillations after release for each subject. These parameters did not differ significantly across conditions indicating that GVS did not modify the preset overall strategy of postural recovery. The intensity and polarity of GVS significantly biased both the postural lean during the oscillatory period and the resting postural stance after recovery, deviating them forward for cathodal-anodal stimulation and backward for anodal-cathodal. Because the GVS affected postural bias but not dynamics, it is likely that GVS either biased signals from otolith regular fibers or provided erroneous velocity signals leading to vestibulospinal compensations complementing the segmental stiffness and damping mechanism. The residuals of the multi-link fit, i.e., the frequency spectra of the actual body sway ripples about the modeled sway, were different across conditions, which also suggest that GVS affects the descending velocity and acceleration drive of the vestibulo-spinal pathway. This finding is consistent with recent theoretical analyses of the influence of GVS on the semicircular canals and otolith organs of the inner ear.

    Response to Hold & Release in Patients with Bilateral Vestibular hypofunction
    The investigators measured the postural responses of six bilateral vestibular hypofunction patients and 19 control subjects to a hold and release test paradigm that induced a brief sudden forward pivot about the ankles. A linear four link inverted pendulum model was used to fit the resulting recovery of balance that was achieved without stepping. EMG latencies of the initial muscle responses to brake the forward tilt of the body did not differ across subject groups. Both subject groups exhibited linear time invariant behavior throughout the recovery period after release maintaining constant stiffness and damping. Both groups also exhibited significantly greater damping, energy dissipation, when light touch of the hand at mechanically non-supportive force levels or sights of the surroundings were permitted. The patients showed significantly less accurate control of the hip sway component than the normal subjects. The findings indicate that for both normal subjects and vestibular deficient subjects the pattern of response to be initiated at release is preset based on the body configuration and hold force prior to release and is executed as a unit.

    The H&R paradigm reveals adaptation of long latency reflexes during exposure to a rotating artificial gravity environment.
    The aim of this study was to quantify recovery from simulated falls in altered force backgrounds and to investigate the mechanisms of recovery, using the H&R paradigm. The investigators tested 16 healthy men and women pre-, per-, and post- 10rpm rotation in the center of the Brandeis slow rotation room. Subjects were divided into two groups: one group had an extra load equivalent to 25% of their body weight strapped to their waist, the other did not. Subjects voluntarily rocked back and forth about their ankles in 30 second trials, 3cm sway amplitude, for 3 pre-rotation, 20 per-rotation, and 10 post rotation trials. Loss of balance was elicited with the H&R perturbation paradigm in 3 pre-rotation trials and 3 trials immediately post-rotation. An OptoTrak system was used to measure postural sway and response to H&R.

    Pre-rotation subjects successfully swayed fore and aft. During per-rotation trials they initially swayed in a deviated path, of approximately 20 degrees, reflecting the influence of Coriolis forces on the body. With additional trials, subjects swayed in a progressively more fore-aft pattern. Adaptation was fastest for the loaded subjects. Post-rotation subjects initially swayed in a mirror image path to that initially seen during rotation. The aftereffect was largest for the subjects with additional load. A comparison of pre- and post-rotation H&R trials indicated that the postural recovery response is not anti-parallel to the direction of the induced fall. Instead, it is in the same direction as the voluntary sway aftereffects, indicating a remapping of both voluntary sway and also of reflexive responses to postural perturbations. After 10 post-rotation sway trials, sway patterns returned to pre-rotation patterns.
