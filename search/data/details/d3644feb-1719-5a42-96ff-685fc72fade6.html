Cardiovascular Regulation in Microgravity

Record ID: 284080
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The D-2 experiment, Cardiovascular Regulation in Microgravity, flown on Spacelab, was designed to test the general hypothesis that human cardiovascular adaptation to microgravity is rapid and effective but associated with significant dysfunction on return to Earth. The principal specific objectives were to (1) contribute to better understanding of the control of body fluid volumes at microgravity by characterizing the responses to rapid expansion of intravascular volume before, during, and after space flight, and (2) to define the mechanisms underlying post-flight orthostatic hypotension.

APPROACH:
Responses to rapid intravenous fluid loading
Preflight measurements were obtained, supine and sitting, in the four D-2 payload crewmembers at six and nine months before flight and in-flight on mission day 5. A system for warming saline to 37 degrees Celsius and an air-free intravenous infusion system was developed through a collaborative effort involving the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and NASA Johnson Space Center. The isotonic saline solution, 22 ml/kg, was delivered over 20 minutes by air pressure from two inflatable bladders compressing a plastic saline bag. The driving pressure was generated by a hand-held inflation bulb and displayed by an aneroid manometer. Mean delivery in microgravity was 95.9% of the intended volume which ranged from 1595 to 1919 ml. Finger arterial pressure was monitored. Cardiac output was measured by the foreign gas rebreathing technique.

Mechanisms of post-flight orthostatic intolerance
The stand test had a scheduled duration of 10 minutes with detailed cardiovascular measurements at rest supine, after five minutes standing, and during the last minute of the test. An electrocardiogram and Finapres arterial pressure were recorded continuously. Cardiac output was measured with the acetylene rebreathing technique. Leg volume was measured at rest and during standing using a stocking plethysmograph.

RESULTS:
Studies of the four D-2 payload crewmembers before and immediately after flight were an essential part of a detailed study of orthostatic intolerance that also involved crewmembers from the flights of the Spacelab life sciences missions SLS-1 and SLS-2, in 1991 and 1993 respectively. Combined, 14 crewmembers, 11 men and 3 women, were examined preflight and within four hours of landing after flights of 9 to 14 days.

Preflight, all crewmembers were able to complete the ten-minute stand test. Post-flight, nine of the fourteen (64%) were unable to finish. Pre- and post-flight supine haemodynamics were similar in both groups except slightly (p < 0.05) higher systolic and mean arterial pressures in the finishers. Post-flight, finishers and non-finishers had equally large postural decreases in stroke volume during standing, and also similar degrees of leg pooling. Non-finishers had a greater (but not significantly so) heart rate increase. Cardiac outputs during standing were also similar. The salient difference between finishers and non-finishers was a greater vasoconstrictor response among the finishers with a mean total peripheral resistance during standing (p <0.01).

The cardiovascular effects of fluid load on-orbit were significantly different from the preflight supine data and closely approached, but did not consistently reach, the preflight sitting response levels. The stroke volume data from microgravity were virtually identical to preflight measurements in the sitting position.

At the time of this experiment, the exact mechanism(s) underlying the inadequate vasoconstrictor response early after return to 1-G were yet to be defined. Maximal oxygen uptake was reduced post-flight but maintained at preflight levels after five to eight days in space, which was consistent with grossly intact cardiovascular regulatory mechanisms at microgravity.

Responses to a graded intravenous infusion of the alpha-adrenergic agonist phenylephrine were measured as changes in leg and total peripheral resistance; responses noted two days after return to 1-G were not different from preflight. However, a series of stand tests performed early after return from space demonstrated lower plasma norepinephrine levels in non-finishers than in finishers.

The results from D-2, SLS-1, and SLS-2 provided the base for a new series of detailed and specific studies of cardiovascular control mechanisms planned for Neurolab, a Spacelab flight, flown in 1998, and for a series of long duration flights in the Russian space station Mir.
