Systems Impacts of Waste Processing

Record ID: Waste_Processing
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Solid waste processing has potential and unavoidable impacts on all life support sub-systems. Either waste is produced that must be disposed of, waste processing has impacts on other sub-systems that must be considered, or waste is usable to reduce the cost of supporting the mission. The crew produce both trash and metabolic waste, food crops produce large quantities of inedible biomass, all systems produce used spares and consumables, and processing of the various kinds of waste typically produce particulate and trace gas contaminants, as well as producing or using quantities of water. Investigators proposed to identify life-support-related solid waste streams, options for processing the waste, costs and benefits, and recommend particular approaches for particular near-term missions.

APPROACH:
The approach for this experiment continued to be built around equivalent system mass (ESM), as has previous life-support work that has done over the past decade. Expected waste streams for long-duration missions based on historical and current data were defined. This involved working closely with Kennedy Space Center (KSC), Shuttle Transportation System (STS), and International Space Station (ISS) personnel, as there is no waste monitoring program currently in place for these programs. Data from earlier programs was also investigated. Based on these data, waste stream models were developed for the missions defined in the Safety and Mission Assurance Panel (SMAP) Reference Missions Document. This involved extrapolation to longer missions and for different environments. Waste processing scenarios ranging from simple dumping to complete destruction of waste were developed. ESM impacts of the various technologies were determined, and assessed mission ESM impacts based on generally agreed assumptions about infrastructure.

RESULTS:
Waste processing was compared for terrestrial and space applications. Typical quantities of terrestrial domestic municipal waste were about 0.6 kg/person per day, with sewage being collected separately and gaseous emissions typically not considered at all. The standard waste production models for space missions that were developed previously were expanded to include new exploration missions and non-metabolic wastes. Typical rates varied from 1.8 to 3.5 kg/md. Potential life support contingencies and probable consequences for the waste system were identified. In general, contingencies would increase waste loads, sometimes by orders of magnitude. New types of wastes could be created that would be challenging to deal with.

For the Mars transit, surface, and base waste models, each of the types of waste was classified by proportion of five categories: ash, equipment, organic materials (biodegradable), plastic (non-biodegradable organics), and water content. This data was used to determine sizing of equipment in calculating ESM.

Missions were tracked, including the new exploration missions, and appropriate equivalencies were determined for trade studies. Technology drivers were identified and their impacts on waste processing considered. Equivalencies are anticipated to fall due to development of infrastructure technologies over the next several decades as exploration missions take individuals further from Earth.

Waste processing technologies were identified for Skylab, STS, and ISS missions, and from recent Advanced Life Support (ALS) projects. Waste technology interfaces were identified. Specific performances were calculated for the different technologies. Results varied from 1.7 kg processed per kg ESM for lyophilization on a transit mission to 7,000 for compacting on a Mars base. However, interface impacts could not be considered on a technology basis. Pre- and post- processing was in some cases as large an impact as the primary processing technology.

Waste approaches were optimized using ESM as the primary metric. Interfaces were a driver in trade studies especially where oxidative processes were considered. Water balance was a major factor in determining the optimum life support system. Water balance varied depending on the technologies used, from a loss of about five kg per crewperson per day for an open loop system, to a gain of about 0.7 kg with the maximum water recovery from waste. Extravehicular Activity (EVA) could result in a loss of about 0.4 kg/md with current EVA technologies and a Mars surface EVA model. Bioregeneration reduces the amount of waste and supply needed, but can have an unfavorable effect on water balance as less water is imported in food. Thus, any losses such as for EVA become more important. Some improvements in ALS reference missions were identified.
