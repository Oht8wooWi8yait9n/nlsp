Radiation Measurements in Shuttle Crew Compartment

Record ID: DTO 684
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Trapped protons in the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) have a rather narrow pitch angle distribution and exhibit east-west anisotropy. In low Earth orbits, the E-W effect results in different amounts of radiation dose received by different sections of the spacecraft. This effect is best studied on missions in which the spacecraft flies in a fixed orientation. The magnitude of the effect depends on the particle energy and altitude through the SAA.

There are three main sources of radiation in free space. These are the trapped protons and electrons, solar energetic particles, and the galactic cosmic rays. For human missions to Mars or the moon, the trapped protons and electrons are of a minor concern. The flux of trapped particles at a fixed spacecraft altitude, and of galactic cosmic rays, decreases as the solar activity increases; however, the probability of a solar particle event increases as the solar activity increases. As these particles interact with the shielding material of the spacecraft and human body, they produce secondary particles, such as lighter fragments, target fragments, and neutrons. These particles can have far more damaging effects on cells than the primary particles. Thus, the radiation environment is very complex and varies with time. This experiment reviews the sources of charged-particles that contribute significantly to radiation risk to the astronauts, and estimates of exposure for some typical missions, such as Space Shuttle, International Space Station, and a human mission to Mars.

APPROACH:
The Space Shuttle carried an active tissue equivalent proportional counter (TEPC), and passive thermoluminscent detectors (TLDs), and two types of nuclear emulsions to measure radiation dose rates in different areas of the Space Shuttle. The detectors were activated on the second flight day. TLD detectors were flown in a middeck locker. Using nuclear emulsions, charged particles and secondary neutron energy spectra were measured.

RESULTS:
Results from this experiment are available for the STS-94 flight only. Refer to the cited publication and datasets for more details.

The ratio of dose rates from the two directions at this location in the middeck was 2.7. As expected from model calculations, the spectra from the two directions are different, that is the ratio is energy dependent. The data can be used to distinguish the anisotropy models. The combined galactic cosmic radiation plus trapped charged particle lineal energy spectra measured by the TEPC and the linear energy transfer spectrum measured by nuclear emulsions are in good agreement. The charged particle absorbed dose rates varied from 112 to 175 µGy/day, and dose equivalent rates from 264.3 to 413 µGy/day. Neutrons in the 1 - 10 MeV contributed a dose rate of 3.7 µGy/day and dose equivalent rate of 30.8 µGy/day, respectively.

Conclusions:
The east-west asymmetry of dose rates due to trapped protons in low Earth orbit has been clearly demonstrated. At 300 km altitude and for 28.5 degree orbit, this asymmetry was about a factor 2.7, consistent with predictions of vector flux models. Improved predictions of crewmember dose rates aboard oriented spacecrafts in low Earth orbit would require consideration of the anisotropy of trapped proton fluxes. Omni-directional trapped belt models, such as AP-8 MIN/MAX are no longer adequate. There is good agreement between measurements of dose rates made by different passive dosimeters, as well as good agreement between the LET spectra measured by TEPC and nuclear emulsions. Additionally, the neutron contribution to crew dose equivalent is significant.
