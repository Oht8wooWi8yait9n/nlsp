Summary / Description

The STS-86 mission was the seventh docking mission between the U.S. Space Shuttle and Russia's Mir Space Station. The Space Shuttle Atlantis launched on September 25,1997, carrying a double Spacehab module and seven crewmembers, including Commander James D. Wetherbee, Pilot Michael J. Bloomfield, Mission Specialists Vladimar G. Titov, Scott E. Parazynski, Jean-Loup J. M. Chretien, Wendy Lawrence, and David A. Wolf. Astronaut Wendy Lawrence was scheduled to replace Michael Foale onboard Mir for the sixth NASA increment. However, due to her small size and concerns that the Russian Mir Orlan EVA spacesuit would not fit properly, David Wolf was selected to join the Mir 24 crew in her place. Foale took Wolf's place on the Shuttle for the return trip to Earth.

During the 5 days of joint U.S.-Russian operations, astronauts and cosmonauts transferred experiment hardware, science samples, and data collected by the Mir 23/Mir 24/NASA 5 crew from the Mir to the Shuttle, where they were returned to Earth to be analyzed by researchers. Crewmembers also transferred water, food and other supplies to the Mir to support the health and well being of the Mir 24/NASA 6 crew and the science investigations on the station.

Mission Specialists Scott Parazynski and Vladimir Titov conducted a five-hour space walk, or extravehicular activity (EVA), on the fourth day that Atlantis was docked to the space station. This was the first U.S. space walk to include the participation of a foreign astronaut. The EVA was performed to retrieve four suitcase-sized experiments called the Mir Environmental Effects Payload (MEEP) from the exterior of Mir's docking module. The experiments were attached to the docking module during Shuttle mission STS-76 in March 1996.

In addition to transferring the MEEP back to the Shuttle, the astronauts tethered a solar array cap to the exterior of the Mir docking module. The cap was to be placed on the damaged Spektr module during a later Russian space walk to seal off the base of the damaged array on Spektr if the array was jettisoned by cosmonauts.

Two additional EVAs were also performed. First, a space walk was performed to evaluate equipment and techniques that will be used to build the International Space Station (ISS). Second, astronauts evaluated the Simplified Aid for EVA Rescue (SAFER), a small jet-backpack designed for use as a type of life jacket during ISS assembly.

Life sciences experiments performed during the STS-86 mission included those classified as Detailed Supplementary Objectives (DSOs). A DSO is a NASA-sponsored investigation performed by Space Shuttle crewmembers, who serve as the test subjects. These studies are designed to require minimal crew time, power and stowage. Biomedical DSOs focus on operational concerns, including space motion sickness, cardiovascular deconditioning, muscle loss, changes in coordination and balance strategies, radiation exposure, pharmacokinetics and changes in the body's biochemistry.

Other payloads included SEEDS-II, KidSat, Commercial Protein Crystal Growth, Cosmic Radiation Effects and Activation Monitor, Cell Culture Module-A, and Shuttle Ionospheric Modification With Pulsed Local Exhaust.

The Space Shuttle Atlantis landed at Kennedy Space Center after a successful mission on October 6, 1997. Data gained from the mission supplied insight for the planning and development of the ISS, Earth-based sciences of human and biological processes and the advancement of commercial industry.
