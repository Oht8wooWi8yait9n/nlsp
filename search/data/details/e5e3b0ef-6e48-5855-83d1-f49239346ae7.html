Microfluidic Device-Based Capture and Concentration of Microbial Contaminants in Recycled Water

Record ID: Micro_Contaminants
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
As the duration of space mission’s increase, recycling of metabolic and hygienic wastes becomes a necessity. Thus, the microbiological quality of recycled water is of critical importance to astronaut health and environmental quality during such missions. Accidental or catastrophic failures of the recycling process can introduce opportunistic organisms, pathogens, and organisms that cause biofouling of water distribution lines. It is critically important that water quality monitoring systems are able to capture, concentrate, and detect these contaminants effectively and efficiently. An efficient microbial capture and concentration device is needed for concentrating the sample prior to detection. The overall objective of this project was to design, fabricate, test, and optimize a prototype microfluidic device that can capture and concentrate microbial contaminants based on electrostatic trapping and electrophoretic motion principles. The microfluidic device was designed to concentrate microbial contaminants from 120L/day of recycled water on a continuous basis. The salient feature of the device is that the final concentrated volume will be 1 mL which will facilitate integration with any current or future contaminant detection technologies. In this pilot study proposal, investigators proposed to demonstrate bacterial capture and concentration by the microfluidic device and detection by culture and PCR-based detection and characterization. This interdisciplinary project involving engineering and microbiology delineated the design and performance characteristics of the microfluidic device. In addition to the benefits to NASA, the proposed technology has value in terrestrial and homeland security applications as well.

APPROACH:
Reclaimed and bottled water samples at pH values ranging from 5.2-6.5 were seeded with bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, and Pseudomonas) and viruses (MS-2 and Echovirus). Negative control and capture experiments were performed simultaneously using two identical devices. Culture based methods were utilized to characterize the capture efficiency as a function of the species type, time, flow rate, and applied electric field.

RESULTS:
Funding from the NASA Phase 1 Pilot Study project was used to design and demonstrate the applicability of a microfluidic device-based capture and concentration of microbial contaminants in recycled water. During the initial phases of this project investigators developed a basic understanding of the electrophoretic mobilities of selected bacteria under conditions that were realistic within recycled and drinking water distribution systems. Electrophoretic mobility and zeta potential measurements of bacterial cells in municipal drinking water can lead to enhanced understanding of bacterial surface interactions and adhesion to surfaces in realistic environmental conditions. During the second half of this project a large number of laboratory experiments were performed to design, fabricate, and test the efficacy of a microfluidic-device based microbial capture system. These studies have led to the development of a microfluidic device based on electrophoretic transport and electrostatic trapping of charged particles for continuous capture and concentration of pathogens from water. Reclaimed and bottled water samples at pH values ranging from 5.2–6.5 were seeded with bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas) and viruses (MS-2 and Echovirus) and used in device testing. Both culture based methods and real-time PCR analysis were utilized to characterize the capture efficiency as a function of time, flow-rate, and applied electric field. Based on differences between the capture and negative control data, capture efficiencies of 90% to 99% were achieved for E. coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, and MS-2, while the capture efficiency for Echovirus was around 75%. Overall, the device exhibits 16.67 fold sample volume reduction within an hour at 6 mL/hr. This results in a concentration factor of 15 at 90% capture efficiency. Scaling of the device to sample 5 L/hour can be achieved by stacking 835 identical micro-channels. The device can thus function either as a filtration unit or as a sample concentrator to enable the application of real-time detection sensor technologies. The ability to continuously sample water without chemical additives facilitates the use of this device for NASA applications within enclosed environments. The successful completion of this project constitutes the first step in the development of a continuous, microbial capture and concentration system from large volumes of potable water. A key driving force in the device design was to achieve reduced mass, and reduce power and volume requirements.
