Human Circadian Rhythms and Sleep in Space

Record ID: 9401639
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
The objectives of the study were (1) to assess the circadian rhythms and sleep of healthy male astronauts experiencing microgravity (weightlessness) while living on board the Mir Space Station and (2) to determine the effects of prolonged space flight on the endogenous circadian pacemaker, as indicated by oral temperature and subjective alertness rhythms, and their ramifications for sleep, alertness, and performance.

APPROACH:
This investigation was conducted for the first time as part of the NASA-Mir Increment 4 mission. Three crewmembers completed measurements relating to the protocol preflight, inflight and postflight. For each phase of the flight, the experiment was divided into three 12- to 14-day blocks of time (spread throughout the mission). The inflight measurement blocks were scheduled early in the mission (mission day 37-49), in the middle (MD 79-91) and at the end (MD 110-122) of the mission. The experiment protocols were repeated on the NASA-Mir Increment 5 mission using two additional crewmembers.

Within each measurement block, subjects measured oral body temperature and subjective alertness (five times per day) and did a performance test (one per day). After each sleep episode, subjects completed a modified computer-based version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Diary. This diary was used to assess the estimated timings of sleep onset and offset, recorded the number and nature of within-night awakenings, and recorded the use of space adaptation sickness and hypnotic medications. The questionnaire concluded with visual analogue scale ratings of subjective sleep quality, mood on awakening (tense vs. relaxed), and alertness on awakening (sleepy vs. alert).

In addition to the above measurements, subjects completed a pre-sleep questionnaire (also part of the Pittsburgh Sleep Diary) at the end of each work shift, reporting on stresses such as extravehicular activity (EVA), equipment malfunction and changes in procedure, as well as ratings of how long the shift lasted and how satisfied the subject was with his work on it.

RESULTS:
Many unplanned and potentially catastrophic events occurred during the NASA-Mir Increment 4 mission that impacted the results. During a portion of Block 1 (MD37-49), there were six rather than three crewmembers on board. In addition to cramped conditions, a serious fire occurred on MD43 causing all six crewmembers to miss one night of sleep. For several days after the fire, the crew wore oxygen filter masks, which further disrupted normal activities.

During Block 2 (MD79-91) the temperature inside Mir rose to very uncomfortable levels, causing the crew to check their skin temperature twice a day. Also, the crew was instructed not to exercise due to heat and the rising levels of CO2. An ethylene glycol leak led to unpleasant smells and concerns about toxicity. Block 3 (MD110-122) saw another slight increase in CO2 levels and the breakdown of several onboard systems, including the urine reclamation/ processing system, the condensate recovery system and the thermal control system.

There were no missed pre- or post sleep questionnaires during either the preflight or postflight baseline data collection during NASA-Mir Increment 4. During flight sleep Block 1, a small amount of data was lost due to the fire, but the NASA 4 subject collected additional data to make up for the missed sessions during Block 1. During flight sleep Blocks 2 and 3, small amounts of data were not collected.

Circadian rhythms in body temperature and subjective alertness were analyzed by sinusoidal technique to derive an estimate of phase, amplitude and mean level for each subject with each measurement block. Sleep questionnaire variables were used both as measures and modifiers of the other sleep indices (e.g., "tagging" nights that may have been influenced by space adaptation sickness, hypnotic medications or excessive work load). Performance and mood variables were used to derive both time of day effects and mean daily levels. Analysis of the questionnaires showed that on days following sleep periods of five hours duration or less, alertness, mood and perceived work performance were decreased.

The results from examination of one subject's circadian alertness and oral temperature rhythms suggested that the endogenous circadian pacemaker seemed to function quite well up to 90 days in space. Thereafter (on days 110-122), the influence of the endogenous circadian pacemaker on oral temperature and subjective alertness circadian rhythms was considerably weakened, with consequent disruptions in sleep.

Researchers concluded that space missions lasting more than 3 months might result in diminished circadian pacemaker influence in astronauts, leading to eventual sleep problems. Additionally, more research is needed to determine how the sleep, circadian rhythms and astronaut performance respond to major events whether they be scheduled, such as an EVA, docking or crew transfer, or unscheduled, such as a fire or collision.
