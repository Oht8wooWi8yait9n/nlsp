Renal Stone Risk Assessment During Long-Term Space Flight

Record ID: 9401651
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Exposure to the microgravity environment of space alters human physiology. A number of these changes are expected to contribute to an altered urinary function that will in turn influence the potential for renal stone formation. Studies conducted in the Space Shuttle program have shown that, immediately postflight, the urine of most crewmembers is saturated with stone-forming salts. These astronauts are at an increased risk for developing calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate and uric acid stones. Also, there was a difference in the values of astronauts flying on short missions (up to six days in space) and those with a longer exposure to microgravity (7 to 14 days), which led to the hypothesis that the risk of calcium oxalate stones increases as the length of the mission increases.

The most obvious changes observed in astronauts are the increased excretion of calcium and the negative calcium balance thought to be the result of bone loss. Other demonstrated changes include decreased urinary output, urinary pH, magnesium, and citrate concentrations and increased urinary phosphate. All of these changes would be expected to increase the urinary supersaturation of stone-forming salts.

For this experiment, three objectives were formulated:
1. To quantitate the risk of renal stone formation associated with extended duration space flight.
2. To assess the risk factors which are affected by flight duration.
3. To estimate the contribution of dietary and environmental factors to the risk of renal stone formation.

APPROACH:
To assess the renal stone-forming potential, 24-hour urine collections were taken preflight, inflight and postflight. During the inflight period, individual urine voids were collected over a 24-hour period into urine collection devices containing 1 ml 2.5 M lithium chloride as a volume marker. From each urine void, two 7 ml urine aliquots were withdrawn into syringe tubes containing 0.05% thymol or 0.1% thimerosal, mixed well and stored at ambient temperature until return to Earth for analyses.

Following the return of the samples, urine volumes were determined from the diluted lithium concentration. The urine samples were analyzed for calcium, phosphorus, uric acid, sodium, potassium, magnesium, creatinine, pH, citrate, oxalate, and sulfate. Uric acid was always determined by analyzing the thymol preserved sample, while citrate and pH were determined from the thimerosal preserved sample. Other analytes were not restricted to a specific sample.

During the NASA 6 and NASA 7 missions, an additional 14 ml of urine from each void was collected into the Dried Urine Chemistry device. The device sampled urine on a card, which was dried at ambient temperatures and stored under controlled environmental conditions until analysis. For analysis, the urine cards were rehydrated and standard assays were performed. The urinary data from the Dried Urine Chemistry cards were compared to the liquid samples and the efficiency of this new technology was assessed.

Pre- and postflight, the subjects collected 24-hour urine pools. After total volume and pH were recorded, two 10 ml aliquots were removed for biochemical analysis. The first sample was analyzed for creatinine, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium and uric acid. The second 10 ml aliquot was acidified with 6M hydrochloric acid (HCl) and analyzed for calcium, magnesium, citrate, oxalate and sulfate. All samples were processed immediately or stored at -20 degrees Celsius until analysis.

From the determined urinary levels of calcium, sodium, phosphorus, uric acid, oxalate and sulfate, the urine’s supersaturation with stone-forming salts, such as calcium oxalate, brushite (calcium phosphate), struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate), sodium urate, and uric acid, was determined and the renal stone risk was estimated.

In addition, detailed diet logs were maintained by the crewmembers for 24 hours prior to and during the 24-hour urine collection period in order to assess the effect of dietary factors on the potential for renal stone formation.

RESULTS:
This investigation continued to support previous data from both short-term Shuttle and long duration Mir missions that crewmembers exposed to the microgravity environment of space flight increase their risk of renal stone formation. The crewmember provided excellent compliance with the objectives of this investigation with the exception of the effects of flight duration. Due to scheduling difficulties, no early in-flight session was performed so the effects of mission duration could not be evaluated. Theses data will be combined with data obtained from other long duration missions and the potential for renal stone development assessed.
