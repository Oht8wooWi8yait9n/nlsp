Non-Adrenergic Mechanisms of Cardiovascular Deconditioning

Record ID: NCC958CA00405
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
In the vasculature, investigators have identified novel mechanisms by which nitric oxide (NO) regulates vascular endothelial function. We have also demonstrated that enhanced NO dependent signaling (as a result of NOS phosphorylation) contributes to vascular contractile hyporesponsiveness, a central component of orthostasis. Investigators are currently using in-vivo techniques to non-invasively and serially measure vascular stiffness (as measured by pulse wave velocity) and determine the contribution of altered arterial compliance to orthostatic intolerance (OI). In addition, one of our graduate students, Mr. Eric Tuday, was selected as a summer intern at the Johnson Space Center, in Drs. Janice Meck's and Steven Platt's Cardiovascular Laboratories. His collaborative studies and Dr. Platt's group has proven to be very fruitful and investigators will determine the changes in aortic and arterial compliances before, during, and after long-term bed rest in human volunteers. These studies test the hypothesis that long-term bed rest increases arterial compliance which subsequently can decrease the cardiac output response during orthostatic challenges. With regard to the heart, investigators have determined for the first time that it is not loss of cardiac mass, but altered contractile function that contribute to OI. This depressed contractile function is likely the result of altered adrenergic signaling or altered NO signaling. The investigators have made significant contributions to the understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying the role of NO in the modulating cardiac contractile structure and function in general. The investigators currently have a patent on the target site to inhibit Arginase II. They are working with a venture capital group to further fund work to develop a specific designer drug which will inhibit Arginase II.

RESULTS:
Key findings:
The investigators have been able to show the dysregulation in vascular smooth muscle currently has a target site by which this dysregulation occurs. Their data demonstrating that endothelial cells isolated from ArgII-/- mice are resistant to ROS production and do not exhibit a decrease in NO production following OxLDL exposure suggests that it is the endothelium that is the source of Arg and thus, the target for Arg inhibition.

In animal models, they have used aortic atherosclerosis and plaque formation as the target endpoint, as this is an accessible site for the study of the vascular changes. Since it is well accepted that dysfunctional endothelium is the root cause of initiating the atherosclerotic process, ArgII would promise to be a target for all sites of atherosclerosis.

Investigators have sought additional funding to develop a specific inhibitor of ArginaseII. This search has resulted in a partnership between The Johns Hopkins University, the University of Pennsylvania and Acidophil, LLC to start a new company. This company will develop new specific blockers of Arginase II and bring these products to the market place.
