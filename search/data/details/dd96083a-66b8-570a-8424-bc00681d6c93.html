NSCOR: Charged Particle Radiation and Resultant Oxidative Stress Elicit Deleterious Functional Changes in the Central Nervous System

Record ID: NNX10AD59G
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Understanding the responses of the central nervous system to charged particle radiation has two main benefits – risk assessment and optimization of radiotherapy. The general population and aviation crews are exposed to charged particles during cross country and trans-oceanic air travel at high altitudes as well as to radon in their homes and workplaces. While these exposures are not as high as those of astronauts they are not insignificant. Cancer risks have traditionally been the focus of health risk estimates but the new awareness of sensitive targets in the brain such as neurogenic centers in the hippocampus raises question about the negative effects of such radiation on late neurodegenerative diseases and potential decline of cognitive abilities. This project directly addressed the cognitive and neurogenesis issues and indirectly addressed late neurodegenerative effects which share reactive oxygen chemistry with radiation as underlying mechanisms. Alzheimer’s, Huntington’s, and in particular, ALS diseases, are of interest. In the last ten years, the use of charged particles in radiation therapy has grown dramatically with proton and carbon ion treatment centers being established in the U.S., Europe, and Asia. Always limiting the effectiveness of radiotherapy is the response of normal tissue near the tumor site. The precise dose localization associated with charged particle therapy makes it ideal for tumors of the head and neck where careful avoidance of critical structures such as the optic nerve is critical. Knowledge of the dose “tolerance” of brain tissue and the reactions to relatively low doses is critical in optimizing these modalities of treatment.

APPROACH:
This study investigated responses of the mouse brain to several radiation exposure regimes corresponding to galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events. It characterized behavioral, electrophysiological, biochemical, and structural changes both in vivo and in vitro. Radiation-elicited oxidative stress mechanisms were investigated as important mediators of the biological responses in both wild type and mutant animals. In addition to characterization of normal tissue responses, the potential enhancement of three late risks by radiation exposure were explored. These were seizures, ischemic injury, and traumatic injury.

RESULTS:
The NSCOR team has demonstrated that low doses of charged particles (<=10 cGy) can elicit a number of persistent changes to the brain, especially in the hippocampus. At the structural level, these include reductions in neurogenesis correlated with microglial activation and substantial reductions in dendritic branching and spine numbers. Electrophysiological changes affect both excitatory and inhibitory neurons and include altered intrinsic membrane properties and dysregulation of excitability and synaptic plasticity. Multiple behavioral tests that measure aspects of memory and cognition show impairments in performance. Elevation of oxidative stress is seen soon after irradiation, is persistent, and is accompanied by elevated markers of inflammatory processes. The mechanistic importance of the oxidative stress is shown by the protective effects on all endpoints of elevated catalase activity in MCAT transgenic animals. Together these observations point to a broad-based undesirable reaction of brain tissue to irradiation at doses which do not cause gross pathological changes or cell death. The mechanisms are poorly understood at this time but may involve oxidative mechanisms and inflammatory process. The significance of the impairments in the rodent models to human performance are presently unknown but demonstrate that there is a basis for concern. >p>
