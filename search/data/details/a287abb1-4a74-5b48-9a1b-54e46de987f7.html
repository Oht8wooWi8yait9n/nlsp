Vitamin D Metabolites and Bone Demineralization

Record ID: LS-1
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
Research data from previous calcium imbalance studies have shown that exposure to microgravity during space flight may cause bone mineral loss, increased plasma calcium, increased urinary calcium excretion, and an overall negative calcium balance. The objective of this experiment was to determine whether plasma levels of vitamin D metabolites, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) were altered in the crew of the 8 day Spacelab 2 mission. Significant changes in these hormones during space flight may indicate that the calcium homeostatic mechanism is adversely affected by exposure to microgravity.

The calcium homeostatic mechanism operates through the integration of intestinal calcium absorption, calcium deposition and resorption from bone, and calcium excretion. The mechanism is regulated by the interaction of several hormonal factors, including PTH, calcitonin, and vitamin D. Vitamin D plays a key role because one of its metabolites, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D), regulates intestinal calcium absorption and, in conjunction with PTH, induces bone mineral resorption to maintain normal blood calcium levels. An increase in circulating levels of these two hormones could be responsible for initiating changes in the calcium homeostatic mechanism by activating bone remodeling sites and by regulating calcium transport in the intestine, kidneys, and in bone. Long term space flight may cause hormonal imbalances in this mechanism which can lead to detrimental effects such as bone demineralization and kidney stone formation.

APPROACH:
During the preflight testing phase, investigators studied hormone levels in blood samples from both humans and animals to devise new and better methods for metabolite analysis. The results of the tests helped investigators choose the best methods for analysis of the in flight samples and provided them with a broad range of data to use for comparative analysis.

Four crewmembers participated in the study and were divided equally into two teams, the red team and the blue team. The protocol called for samples to be collected at 24, 18, and 3 days prelaunch, immediately after landing, and at days 3 and 10 postlanding. The in flight protocol required each subject to collect two blood samples during the mission. One sample was collected early in the mission while the other was collected toward the end of the mission. The blue team collected their early samples at 18 hours into the mission while the red team collected samples at 31 hours. The late samples were collected at 6 days, 19 hours and 7 days, 7 hours for the blue and red teams, respectively. Two tubes of blood were taken during each sample session; one tube was used for the vitamin D metabolite analysis while the other tube was used for measurements of PTH, albumin, calcium, and phosphorus. All samples were collected in glass tubes, centrifuged to separate the plasma from the blood cells, and frozen until the analysis was performed. The volume collected for all samples was 25 ml and each sample yielded 4 to 5 ml of plasma. The vitamin D metabolite analysis included measurements for 24,25(OH)2D, 25 OHD, and 1,25(OH)2D.

RESULTS:
The results of the plasma analyses showed no significant difference between preflight and postflight mean values for any parameter measured. However, the early in flight samples indicated that levels of 25 OHD did increase significantly when compared to preflight means but the in flight value was within the normal range. The level of 24,25(OH)2D increased markedly in one crewmember late in the mission, however, this increase was within the normal range and not significant when compared to preflight values. Essentially, the levels of two biologically active vitamin D metabolites, 25OHD and 24,25(OH)2D, remained unchanged at all time points. In contrast, levels of the key biologically active vitamin D metabolite, 1,25(OH)2D, did change. The first inflight measurement of this hormone showed an increase of 57 percent when compared to preflight means. This increase was significant at the (p < 0.05) level. The second in flight value was 22 percent lower than the preflight mean but was not significantly different. Thus, levels of this key vitamin D metabolite were elevated early in flight but returned to normal by the end of the seventh day. Measurements of parathyroid hormone from the early and late in flight samples showed decreases of 47 and 34 percent, respectively, when compared to preflight means. These decreases were not statistically significant due to the small number of subjects. Plasma levels of calcium, phosphorus, and albumin were essentially normal throughout the mission.
