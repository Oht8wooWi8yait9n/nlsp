Eye-Hand Coordination During Space Motion Sickness

Record ID: DSO 451
Official Source: National Aeronautics and Space Administration Life Sciences Data Archive
View Official Record on NASA NLSP →

Summary / Description

OBJECTIVES:
In weightlessness, major loads are removed from muscles and from a variety of proprioceptive sensors, especially force sensors. While these sensors are rapidly adapting to microgravity and nominally work at all angles in relation to the gravity vector, virtually all actions are modulated if not initiated by the cerebellum and higher centers. In weightlessness, signals from the gravity sensitive otolith organs are distorted and incongruent with semicircular canal vision and cause some crewmembers to experience space motion sickness (SMS).

There are extensive vestibular inputs to the higher centers of the central nervous system, especially the cerebellum, as well as the spinal cord. Without study one could not be sure that weightlessness would not affect movements, particularly finely coordinated movements and especially during SMS when a major sensory conflict is present. Simple operational experience has demonstrated that muscular activities were not significantly affected by weightlessness; however, it was difficult to say the same during SMS.

To document this in a more sensitive and orderly fashion, one of the simplest tests of sensorimotor function was devised for use on STS-5 and continued on STS-7. It consisted of a crewmember selecting a given spot on a chart with a scribe marking that location. Then, while looking away, the crewmember attempted to return the scribe to the same point, again marking the chart at that point. This was repeated a number of times with and without loading equivalent to weight of the segment used. No significant changes were found in normal subjects in weightlessness.

This study was refined and expanded on STS-8 by utilizing a recorder which moved a wide paper chart past a marker which was free to move transversely across the paper. In addition to measuring kinesthetic sensitivity, a visual tracking task was added by prerecording sine and triangular waves at various frequencies and amplitudes and a series of random step functions. This was used extensively on STS-8 but not during SMS. To reduce size and weight and to facilitate automatic data analysis, an electronic recording scale with a light-emitting diode (LED) tracking display was constructed to specification for Shuttle flights. It has provisions for both kinesthetic testing and eye hand tracking and was flown and used on SL-3 but not during SMS.

APPROACH:
The electronic tracking unit consisted of a 25 cm linear track 23.5 cm along which a pointer and handle was free to move. Adjacent to the track is a linear array of 50 LEDs one of which was illuminated. An internal generator sequentially drove the illuminated LED in a sequence of functions. Position of pointer and light was recorded by the FM instrumentation recorder. In the kinesthetic position mode, pointer position was recorded as the subject chose a position with eyes averted then moved the pointer away from it and attempted to find the original position for a total of ten times with shoulder rotation only. This was repeated with free use of all arm points. Eye hand tracking consisted of attempting to maintain the pointer on the lighted LED as it performed the various functions which were displayed for tracing.

Preflight testing and baseline data were nominally performed. Two subjects were requested to perform the in flight testing on FD-1 or 2 and then again one day from FD-3 to recovery. Records were obtained for each scheduled subject.
